## Research conclusions

### New Zealand flag and Southern Cross

Manatu Taonga documents the New Zealand flag as a royal-blue field with four five-pointed red stars, each bordered white, on the fly. The national proportion is 1:2. The official digital references are approximately `#02216E` for Pantone 280C blue and `#C91235` for Pantone 186C red. The 1902 description fixes the star cross orientation, relative size, and white border. The New Zealand flag's four-star count must not become the Australian five-star pattern.

The NZHistory maritime history page records the Blue Ensign origin, the short-lived red `NZ` badge from 1867, and the replacement by four red stars with white borders in 1869. This gives the base state a historically grounded maritime continuity signal without requiring the Union Jack canton.

Sources: [Manatu Taonga New Zealand flag](https://www.mch.govt.nz/our-work/flags-anthems-and-emblems/new-zealand-flag), [NZHistory maritime origins](https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/flags-of-new-zealand/maritime-origins), [Te Ara Southern Cross](https://teara.govt.nz/en/southern-cross).

### Silver fern

Te Ara identifies `Cyathea dealbata` as the silver fern and records its use as an important unofficial national emblem from the 1888 New Zealand Natives rugby tour. It has also appeared on military badges, graves, and meat and dairy export marks. A flat original silver or white fern is therefore historically defensible as a national identity motif. The source photograph itself is not a reusable art source, and no commercial silver fern trademark should be copied.

Source: [Te Ara wearing the silver fern](https://teara.govt.nz/en/photograph/2579/wearing-the-silver-fern).

### Red Ensign and maritime rescue vocabulary

NZHistory records the New Zealand Red Ensign as a merchant maritime flag adopted in 1903. The documented lineage includes a red field, the Union Jack canton, and a white Southern Cross in the fly. For a fictional Pacific Relief Republic, the safe design direction is to reference the red maritime field and white four-star constellation while omitting the Union Jack and introducing a distinct relief emblem.

The IMO life-saving appliance resolution identifies lifeboat, rescue boat, liferaft, lifebuoy, lifebuoy with line, and lifebuoy with light or smoke categories. Its recommended emergency-equipment symbols are white on green. This supports an original white lifeboat or lifebuoy silhouette in a green or sea-green civic roundel, but the flag must not look like an operational ISO or IMO station sign.

Maritime New Zealand rules identify red light or flare, orange smoke, and orange canvas with a black square and circle as distress or aerial identification signals. These are operational signals, not identity marks. They can inform a small accent only. Do not copy the exact orange and black locator panel or present a flag as an active distress signal.

Sources: [NZHistory other official flags](https://nzhistory.govt.nz/page/other-official-flags), [IMO Resolution A.760(18)](https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/KnowledgeCentre/IndexofIMOResolutions/AssemblyDocuments/A.760%2818%29.pdf), [Maritime New Zealand Part 23](https://www.maritimenz.govt.nz/media/jj2perei/part23-maritime-rule.pdf).

Coastguard Tautiaki Moana history supports a lifeboat-state story. It records volunteer rescue services forming in the late nineteenth century, the first official lifeboat at Timaru in 1864, and a permanent rescue service at Sumner in 1898. The current Coastguard logo, chevron, bow-wave mark, bilingual wordmark, and name are not reusable source art.

Source: [Coastguard mission and history](https://www.coastguard.nz/our-story/about-us).

### Civil Defence

New Zealand's regulated Civil Defence mark is a yellow triangle in a blue circle with `CD` lettering and `CIVIL DEFENCE` wording. The regulation specifies Pantone 294 blue and Process Yellow. This is useful context for emergency-management colour language but is not a generic rescue symbol. The exact circle, triangle, letters, and wording must not appear on any fictional flag.

Source: [Civil Defence Emergency Management Regulations 2003, Schedule 1](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/regulation/public/2003/0089/latest/DLM189393.html).

### Maori-derived motifs

Manatu Taonga describes the Tino Rangatiratanga flag as a preferred national Maori flag that can complement the New Zealand flag. Its documented layout is black at the top, red at the bottom, and a white koru nearest the hoist. The koru is a curling fern frond associated with unfolding new life, hope, and renewal. The same page records consultation and display protocol. This is a living cultural symbol, not a generic spiral.

The source should only inform a route with explicit Maori partnership and separate cultural review. Do not paste the Tino Rangatiratanga layout onto a generic New Zealand successor. Do not invent anonymous Maori tattoo bands, moko, waka, pounamu, kowhaiwhai, iwi patterns, or an invented Maori rescue glyph. Te Ara records multiple Maori interpretations of the Southern Cross, including an anchor or sky canoe, so no single interpretation should be assigned to all iwi.

Sources: [Manatu Taonga Tino Rangatiratanga flag](https://www.mch.govt.nz/our-work/flags-anthems-and-emblems/new-zealand-flag/tino-rangatiratanga-flag), [Te Ara Southern Cross](https://teara.govt.nz/en/southern-cross).

### Protected humanitarian and state marks

The ICRC states that the Red Cross, Red Crescent, and Red Crystal are protected emblems with defined medical and humanitarian meanings. The Pacific Relief Republic must not use those emblems or a close imitation. Use an original lifeboat, wave, fern, or maritime seal instead.

The Flags, Emblems, and Names Protection Act 1981 declares the New Zealand Ensign the national flag and protects the official design and close imitations. All three fictional flags must be visibly distinct from the exact national flag, the Red Ensign, and regulated government logos.

Sources: [ICRC emblems](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/emblems), [New Zealand Flags, Emblems, and Names Protection Act 1981](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1981/0047/latest/whole.html).

## Reference-constrained ImageGen brief

Generate three separate clean flat orthographic flag designs, one for each identity. Use the cited New Zealand flag, Red Ensign, silver fern, IMO life-saving, and cultural sources as strict design references. The design must be intentional heraldic graphic work, not event art.

Common constraints:

- Preserve a 1:2 flag ratio and export to normal `82x52`, medium `41x26`, and small `10x7`.
- Keep every design flat and orthographic with no fabric folds, flagpole, sky, lighting, perspective, shadows, gradients, vignettes, painterly texture, or scene around the flag.
- Use no readable text, initials, slogans, seals, or invented heraldic claims.
- Keep the main emblem legible at `10x7`. Test the normal, medium, and small versions together.
- Retain four Southern Cross stars whenever the Southern Cross is used. Do not add Australia's fifth star.
- Use original geometry rather than tracing page images, official logos, Coastguard marks, Civil Defence marks, or safety signs.
- Preserve the cited star orientation, colour relationships, and fern direction. A fictional variant may rearrange the composition to remain distinct from an official flag, but it must not falsify the documented motif.

Identity prompts:

- Base New Zealand Lifeboat State: deep royal blue, four red stars with white borders, original silver fern, calm sea-line accent, civic maritime continuity, no Union Jack.
- Pacific Relief Republic: red maritime field, white four-star constellation, original green rescue roundel with a simple white lifeboat or lifebuoy and a blue wave, open-port humanitarian civic identity, no medical emblems and no exact rescue sign.
- Southern Refuge: near-black navy, compact white or cool-silver four-star constellation, upright silver fern, narrow dark-red accent if needed, guarded southern navigation and closed refuge, no radiation trefoil.
