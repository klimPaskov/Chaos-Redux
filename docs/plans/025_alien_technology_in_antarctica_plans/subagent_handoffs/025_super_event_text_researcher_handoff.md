# Event 025 Super-Event Text Research Handoff

Research date: 2026-08-29

## Scope and package boundary

This note covers the text research requested for Event 025, including the opening first-reveal presentation and optional wording for a later final-recovery report.

The Event 025 package specifies one opening super-event, not a separate final-recovery super-event. The final-recovery material below is therefore a contextual or report-text recommendation only and is not a recommendation to add a second super-event slot, trigger, or wiring package.

No gameplay files, localisation files, event files, GUI files, asset files, or super-event wiring were edited.

The research used the Event 025 specifications and research note, the Chaos Redux super-event skill, the Event 016 text-research and pattern notes, the shared quote ledger, and the Event 025 cross-event notes. The allowed repository material did not contain a separate Event 036 super-event text-research package, so this handoff makes no claim about Event 036's current player-facing wording.

## Recommended opening package

### Title direction

Recommended title direction: `A LIGHT OVER THE ICE`

This is original parent-authored wording, not a quotation or an attribution. It reflects the package's public report of a brief light over Antarctic ice, stays specific to the setting, and does not assert that the object is extraterrestrial, technological, occupied, or understood.

Backup title directions:

- `THE SOUTHERN UNKNOWN` — original wording; clear uncertainty and geography, but less directly tied to the observed descent.
- `TERRA AUSTRALIS INCOGNITA` — historically grounded cartographic allusion; stronger archival flavour, but archaic and less immediate than the recommended title.

Rejected title direction: `THE SINKING STAR`. It is a Tennyson allusion, but it risks identifying the object as a star and therefore conflicts with the package's insistence that the craft's nature remains unproven.

### Selected opening quote

> “To follow knowledge, like a sinking star, / Beyond the utmost bound of human thought.”

Author: Alfred, Lord Tennyson.

Work and date: “Ulysses,” first published in 1842; the source edition's note says the poem was written soon after Arthur Hallam's death, presumably in 1833.

Source: [Project Gutenberg, *The Early Poems of Alfred, Lord Tennyson*, “Ulysses”](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/8601/8601-h/8601-h.htm), lines 8858–8861 for the date/editorial context and lines 8900–8908 for the quoted text.

Attribution confidence: high. The wording appears in the poem under Tennyson's name, and the source distinguishes Tennyson's expansion from the earlier Dante material.

Rights status: low risk for the underlying original English text because the poem is nineteenth-century and public-domain by age in the United States. The Project Gutenberg page carries its own distribution terms and warns users outside the United States to check local law; use the underlying text only, without Project Gutenberg branding or boilerplate.

Exact intended use: the opening super-event's main quote or epigraph.

Fit: the line joins scientific ambition with an unreachable boundary and gives a visual echo of the reported descent without naming the object. It supports discovery, uncertainty, and the beginning of a race while withholding the craft's nature and technology.

Risk and handling: the line is spoken by Tennyson's Ulysses and is aspirational rather than a literal Antarctic report. The “sinking star” image could be over-read as an identification of the object, so the title and description should remain literal and observational. If the parent wants a less symbolic quote, use the Cook backup below.

## Opening quote candidates considered

### James Cook — polar ambition and danger

> “as far as it was possible for man to go”

Speaker and work: James Cook, *A Voyage Towards the South Pole and Round the World*, Volume 1, first published in 1777.

Source: [Project Gutenberg, *A Voyage Towards the South Pole and Round the World, Volume 1*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15777/pg15777-images.html), lines 1832–1836, especially line 1835.

Attribution confidence: high. This is a first-person passage in Cook's expedition account.

Rights status: low risk for the underlying original English text because the work is eighteenth-century and public-domain by age in the United States. Apply the same local-jurisdiction check noted for the selected quote.

Fit: excellent for polar ambition, possession of a limit, and the danger of going farther than known expeditions. It is a short exact fragment rather than a complete sentence, so it should be presented as a deliberate fragment or retained as a backup rather than silently expanded.

Decision: backup opening quote, not selected because the fragment is less self-contained and less connected to the reported light than Tennyson.

### Robert Falcon Scott, as quoted in Charles Turley — the unknown ahead

> “Yet before us lay the unknown. What fascination lies in that word!”

Speaker attribution: Robert Falcon Scott, explicitly identified as the writer in the cited retelling.

Source work and date: Charles Turley, *The Voyages of Captain Scott: Retold from The Voyage of Discovery and Scott's Last Expedition*, 1914.

Source: [Project Gutenberg, *The Voyages of Captain Scott*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6721/pg6721-images.html).

Attribution confidence: medium-high for the wording as presented in Turley's book and for Turley's attribution to Scott, but lower than a direct Scott primary-source edition because the consulted source is a retelling.

Rights status: low risk for the 1914 source text by age in the United States. The attribution and source distance should still be documented if used.

Fit: exceptionally strong for the first reveal's uncertain horizon and expeditionary mood. It is not selected because the source is secondary and the opening package prefers a primary or clearly attributable text where a clean alternative exists.

### Francis Bacon — knowledge and discovery

> “The end of our foundation is the knowledge of causes, and secret motions of things.”

Author and work: Francis Bacon, *The New Atlantis*, published posthumously in 1627.

Source: [Project Gutenberg, *The New Atlantis*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2434/2434-h/2434-h.htm).

Attribution confidence: high.

Rights status: public-domain original.

Fit: thematically strong for scientific institutions and hidden knowledge, but it describes the purpose of an established research foundation rather than the first uncertain observation.

Decision: rejected for Event 025 because Event 016 already uses a *New Atlantis* quotation in its super-event package. Reusing the same work would weaken separation between the two presentations.

### Mary Shelley — dangerous knowledge

> “how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge”

Author and work: Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus*, 1818.

Source: [Project Gutenberg, *Frankenstein*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/84/84-h/84-h).

Attribution confidence: high.

Rights status: public-domain original.

Fit: it would support the package's hidden-danger theme, but it pushes the opening toward a moral warning about creation and directly overlaps Event 016's selected Shelley material.

Decision: rejected for Event 025 because it repeats an Event 016 source family and reveals a stronger ethical interpretation than the first reveal is meant to establish.

## Selected opening button or cultural remark

Recommended button or remark: `Terra incognita.`

Use type: short historical-cultural allusion, not a quotation attributed to a single speaker.

Historical basis: the University of Texas at Arlington collection record describes a historical world map whose Antarctic land mass is marked “Terra Incognita.” The German Maritime Museum's contextual page also describes Cook's southern voyage as aimed at the imagined `Terra Australis Incognita`.

Sources: [University of Texas at Arlington / Portal to Texas History, *Descrittione del mappamondo*](https://texashistory.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metapth192720/) and [German Maritime Museum, “James Cook in the Southern Ocean”](https://www.dsm.museum/en/exhibition/news/james-cook-in-the-southern-ocean-or-the-carte-de-lhemisphere-austral).

Attribution confidence: high for the phrase as historical cartographic usage; there is no single author or speaker to credit in the player-facing line.

Rights status: very low copyright risk for the two-word historical phrase. No map image, scan, or modern descriptive copy is being reused.

Exact intended use: the opening super-event's short button or cultural remark, with normal sentence case in the final localisation if desired.

Fit: dry, institutional, and immediately legible as uncertainty. It evokes the old cartographic label for an unknown southern land without claiming that the observed object is alien or understood. It is preferable to a joke or a modern pop-culture reference.

### Button and remark backups

1. `“To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield.”` — Alfred, Lord Tennyson, “Ulysses,” 1842, [same Project Gutenberg source](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/8601/8601-h/8601-h.htm), lines 8941–8946. Public-domain original and high attribution confidence. It fits the international race but is more triumphalist and generic than `Terra incognita.`.

2. `“I cannot rest from travel:”` — Alfred, Lord Tennyson, “Ulysses,” 1842, [same source](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/8601/8601-h/8601-h.htm), lines 8882–8888. Public-domain original and high attribution confidence. It is a compact expeditionary reaction, but the colon and heroic first-person voice make it less natural as a button.

3. `“The ice was all around:”` — Samuel Taylor Coleridge, *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*, first published in 1798, [Project Gutenberg source](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/151/pg151-images.html), lines 92–105. Public-domain original and high attribution confidence. It is a literal polar remark, but its supernatural seafaring context and slightly playful effect make it a weak fit for Event 025's controlled seriousness.

## Optional final-recovery contextual text

### Recommended recovery-context line

> “Every step will be an advance in geographical science.”

Author: Sir Ernest Shackleton.

Work and date: *South: The Story of Shackleton's Last Expedition, 1914–1917*, published in 1919. The line appears in Shackleton's reproduced programme for the proposed trans-continental Antarctic party.

Source: [Project Gutenberg, *South*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5199/5199-h/5199-h.htm), lines 173–177.

Attribution confidence: high. The source identifies Shackleton as author and presents the programme in his own account.

Rights status: low risk for the underlying original English text by age in the United States. Check the distribution jurisdiction before release, and use only the quoted text rather than the Project Gutenberg wrapper.

Exact intended use: optional epigraph or cultural-context line in a future final-recovery or winner report after the confirmed sector and recovery outcome are known. It should not be wired as a second super-event under the current Event 025 package.

Fit: it frames the recovery as an advance in knowledge and field science rather than as a generic world-changing revelation. It does not disclose the recovered technology, the winner's country, the survivor state, or the future dependence mechanic.

Context caution: this is a pre-expedition programme statement, not a sentence written after Shackleton completed a recovery. Use it as a contextual epigraph, not as a fabricated report of Shackleton's outcome.

### Semantically stronger but rights-caveated recovery backup

> “But the rails of science are laid; our knowledge is richer than before.”

Speaker and source: Fridtjof Nansen, introduction to Roald Amundsen's *The South Pole*, dated May 3, 1912, in the English translation by A. G. Chater.

Source: [Project Gutenberg, *The South Pole, Volumes 1 and 2*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/4229/4229-h/4229-h.htm), lines 344–353.

Attribution confidence: high for Nansen's authorship of the introduction and for the wording in the consulted edition.

Rights status: the underlying 1912 work is old, but the consulted English translation's copyright status was not independently cleared here. Treat it as `PD-US underlying work / translation caveat`, not as globally cleared text.

Fit: this is the best semantic match for a successful recovery report because it explicitly says that the expedition leaves knowledge behind. Use it only if the parent confirms that the translation may be distributed, or replace it with the Shackleton original-English line.

### Additional recovery candidate considered

> “Then we have added a great deal to the knowledge of life in the pack”

Speaker and source: Robert Falcon Scott, *Scott's Last Expedition*, Volume I, published in 1913, [Project Gutenberg source](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11579/pg11579-images.html), line 1083.

Attribution confidence: high for the line as presented in the Scott journal edition.

Rights status: low risk for the underlying original English text by age in the United States.

Fit: it is a clear scientific-results statement, but it is specifically about biological observations in sea ice and does not translate well to a recovered object or core. It remains a backup only.

## Copyright, attribution, and placeholder decision

No modern copyrighted song, film, game, or contemporary literary line is needed for the recommended package.

The selected opening quote, selected button phrase, and selected optional recovery line use either public-domain historical text or a short historical phrase. The button phrase is contextual rather than a claimed quotation, and no archival image is being copied.

No source placeholder is required for the recommended opening package. The only unresolved rights issue is the A. G. Chater translation in the Nansen backup; do not ship that line as cleared text unless the parent performs the jurisdiction-specific translation review. If a future recovery presentation needs text before that review is complete, use the original-English Shackleton line or parent-authored wording instead.

## Implementation recommendation for the main agent

Use `A LIGHT OVER THE ICE` as the opening title direction, Tennyson's exact two-line fragment as the main quote, and `Terra incognita.` as the short button or cultural remark.

Keep the opening description factual and limited to the observed descent, uncertain site, and international race. Do not let the title, quote, or remark state that the object is alien, that a survivor exists, that a signal is active, or that a technology family is known.

Treat the Shackleton line as optional final-recovery report context only. Do not add a final-recovery super-event unless the Event 025 specification is explicitly changed. Record the source attribution in the parent research/source ledger according to the Event 016 handoff pattern, while keeping player-facing localisation concise and free of research notes.
