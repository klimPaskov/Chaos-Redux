## Coronation evidence

### Selected main quote

“And out of the houses the rats came tumbling.”

- Author/work: Robert Browning, *The Pied Piper of Hamelin*.
- Date: first published in *Dramatic Lyrics* (1842); the consulted illustrated printing is 1888.
- Source: [Project Gutenberg text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/18343/18343-h/18343-h.htm); [Morgan Library publication record](https://www.themorgan.org/printed-books/166062).
- Confidence: high. The source title page names Browning and the line appears in the poem's rat-emergence passage.
- Rights: public-domain nineteenth-century poem. Project Gutenberg states the edition can be copied or reused under its license; Browning died in 1889.
- Fit: directly names the rats and shows them leaving human houses in a coordinated mass. It supplies emergence and movement; the super-event description must supply the new command structure and crown.

### Coronation backup quote

“Uneasy lies the head that wears a crown.”

- Speaker/work: King Henry IV, William Shakespeare, *Henry IV, Part 2*, Act 3, scene 1, line 31.
- Date: early-modern play, conventionally c. 1598–1600.
- Source: [Folger Shakespeare Library text and citation](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/henry-iv-part-2/).
- Confidence: high; the Folger page identifies the speaker and exact line.
- Rights: Shakespeare's text is public domain by age.
- Fit: directly expresses crown burden and royal legitimacy. It is less rat-specific, so it is a backup when the visual package foregrounds the throne more than the brood emergence.

### Selected coronation button remark

“Sic transit gloria mundi.”

- Source/meaning: traditional Latin phrase meaning “Thus passes the glory of the world,” used as a papal coronation/inauguration admonition.
- Source: [Vatican Publishing House account of the Petrine inauguration rite](https://www.vatican.va/news_services/liturgy/2006/documents/ns_lit_doc_20061221_ministero-petrino_en.html).
- Confidence: high for the cultural reference; it is a traditional phrase rather than a single-author quotation.
- Rights: traditional Latin wording; no modern copyrighted-work risk.
- Fit: gives the rat coronation a cold ceremonial warning and avoids a generic triumphant monarchy reaction. Use the Latin exactly, or label any English rendering as a gloss rather than an historical quotation.

### Coronation remark backup

“Uneasy lies the head that wears a crown.” The same Folger-verified Shakespeare line can serve as the button if it is not also used for the main quote. It is a direct crown reference but lacks the rat/piper specificity of Browning and the fatalistic ceremony of the selected Latin phrase.

## World-end evidence

### Selected main quote

“And I looked, and behold a pale horse: and his name that sat on him was Death.”

- Source/work: Revelation 6:8, King James Version. This is the exact opening sentence of the verse; the complete verse continues with power over a fourth of the earth and killing by sword, hunger, death, and beasts.
- Date: first-century Christian scripture; English translation first published 1611.
- Source: [Bible Gateway Revelation 6:8 KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation+6%3A8&version=KJV); [Bible Gateway KJV rights page](https://www.biblegateway.com/versions/King-James-Version-KJV-Bible?vm=r).
- Confidence: high for both verse and translation.
- Rights: Bible Gateway labels the KJV public domain in the United States. The United Kingdom's separate Crown-rights treatment of the Authorized Version remains a jurisdictional caveat.
- Fit: the pale horse and named Death give terminal judgment weight while remaining an allusion rather than a claim that the Rat King is a biblical figure. The source context carries plague, beasts, and mass mortality associations.

### World-end backup quote

“Therefore hath the curse devoured the earth, and they that dwell therein are desolate.”

- Source/work: Isaiah 24:6, King James Version.
- Date: Hebrew prophetic text traditionally associated with Isaiah's eighth-century BCE setting; KJV English 1611.
- Source: [Bible Gateway Isaiah 24:6 KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+24%3A6&version=KJV).
- Confidence: high for the verse and translation; exact historical composition dating is necessarily approximate.
- Rights: KJV public domain in the United States, with the same United Kingdom caveat.
- Fit: stronger than Revelation for empty cities and a consumed earth. Keep it as backup if the terminal description emphasizes abandoned human habitation over a named Death figure.

### Additional world-end candidate

“Nothing beside remains.”

- Author/work: Percy Bysshe Shelley, “Ozymandias,” first published 1818.
- Sources: [Project Gutenberg's Shelley text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4798/pg4798.html); [Poetry Foundation text and context](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/46565/ozymandias/).
- Confidence: high for author, poem, and line; other punctuation/capitalization varies by edition.
- Rights: Shelley died in 1822; poem is public domain.
- Fit: extremely concise ruin imagery, but less plague-specific and more generic than Revelation or Isaiah, so it is not selected.

### Selected world-end button remark

“A plague o’ both houses!”

- Speaker/work: Mercutio, William Shakespeare, *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 3, scene 1, line 1562.
- Date: early-modern play, conventionally c. 1595.
- Source: [Folger Shakespeare Library full text](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/romeo-and-juliet/read/).
- Confidence: high; Folger identifies the speaker and line. The immediate continuation later repeats the curse and names “a dog, a rat, a mouse, a cat,” making the allusion especially apt for Event 20.
- Rights: Shakespeare's text is public domain by age.
- Fit: five words, bitter and plague-coded. Its original context is a dying character cursing feuding houses, so it reads as a final human reproach while the Rat King inherits the cities.

### World-end remark backup

“All hope abandon, ye who enter in!”

- Source/work: the inscription on the gate in Dante Alighieri's *Inferno*, Canto III, in Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's nineteenth-century English translation.
- Source: [Project Gutenberg edition of Longfellow's *Divine Comedy, Hell*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1001/pg1001-images.html).
- Date: Dante c. 1320; Longfellow translation nineteenth century.
- Confidence: high for the line and translation. Do not replace it with the common paraphrase “Abandon all hope, ye who enter here” without labelling that as a paraphrase.
- Rights: Dante and Longfellow's translation are public domain by age; Project Gutenberg marks the edition as derived from texts not protected by U.S. copyright.
- Fit: the strongest cold terminal-gate remark, but it evokes infernal entry more than plague or rat rule, so it remains a backup.
