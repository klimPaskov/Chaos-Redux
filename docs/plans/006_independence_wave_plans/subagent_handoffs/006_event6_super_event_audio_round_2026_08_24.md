### Composition rights versus recording rights

The Clarke composition is public domain because the composer died in 1707.

The exact 1948 recording first published in 1949 is a separate pre-1972 sound-recording right.

The current Commons raw description still uses `{{Cc-zero-project}} {{Pd-old-70}}` for the file permission field.

`Cc-zero-project` identifies the Swiss Public Domain Project/Wikimedia cooperation and is not the legal `CC0` dedication for this recording.

`Pd-old-70` supports the old composition/authorship term and does not establish a United States public-domain status for the 1949 recording.

The Public Domain Project's own copyright-term page says its phonogram holdings are evaluated under Swiss copyright law: <https://pool.publicdomainproject.org/index.php/Copyright_term_(Copyright)>.

The U.S. Copyright Office's Music Modernization Act FAQ says that pre-1972 recordings first published from 1947 through 1956 receive 95 years plus a 15-year transition period: <https://www.copyright.gov/music-modernization/faq.html>.

The current Commons United States guidance states the same 110-year term for recordings first published from 1947 through 1956 and requires a U.S. public-domain or free-license basis: <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Copyright_rules_by_territory/United_States>.

For the documented 1949 first release, the exact recording remains protected in the United States through 2059 and would not enter the U.S. public domain before 1 January 2060 absent a separate permission, waiver, or valid dedication.

The reviewed evidence still contains no U.S. public-domain tag for the recording, no legal CC0 dedication for the recording, and no rights-holder permission covering redistribution of this exact recording.

License confidence for the exact recording is therefore **insufficient for United States redistribution**.
