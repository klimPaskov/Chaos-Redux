# Research and Design Rationale

## Method

The asset search tested whether a reasonably attested historical flag existed for each identity before any fictional design work began.

Only the approved public-domain Asante artwork was retained as a published source design.

The remaining online candidates were too late, conjectural, geographically ambiguous, movement-specific, or self-published to support a historical reconstruction claim.

Those six ladders therefore use original alternate-history designs built from culturally specific symbol and regalia vocabulary.

No design uses text, a generic Pan-African tricolor, a human caricature, or an invented “historic flag” claim.

## DOX — Asante

The source artwork is the public-domain [Flag of Ashanti](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flag_of_Ashanti.svg) published on Wikimedia Commons.

[Flags of the World](https://www.fotw.info/flags/gh_asa.html) records the yellow, black, green, white-fimbriated, and golden-stool design while also documenting variants.

The package treats it as the approved published Asante design and does not claim that this exact digital drawing was a single immutable imperial standard.

The golden stool remains the central charge because it is the distinctive approved source symbol.

## DSX — Oyo revival

The research did not identify a reliable historical Oyo Empire flag.

A modern Oyo State flag was rejected because it is a later state symbol, not evidence for an empire flag.

The [Smithsonian Oyo Empire record](https://www.si.edu/object/oyo-empire%3Asiris_sil_552036) and the [Wikimedia Commons Oyo Empire category](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Oyo_Empire) were retained as contextual starting points rather than pixel sources.

The fictional revival design uses a large interlaced Ìbọ̀/Solomon-knot charge and restrained outward cavalry spearheads to join documented Oyo artistic vocabulary with the empire's cavalry identity.

The indigo, ivory-gold, and dark-red palette is intentionally distinct from a generic Pan-African scheme.

## DUX — Kanem-Bornu revival

The online file labeled [The Kanem-Bornu Empire old flag](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Kanem-Bornu_Empire_old_flag.jpg) was rejected as historical evidence because its own description is conjectural and its asserted medieval identification is not sufficiently supported.

The modern [Kanuri movement flag material](https://www.crwflags.com/fotw/flags/ng%7Dkb.html) was also rejected because a contemporary movement flag is not a historical Kanem-Bornu imperial standard.

The fictional revival design combines an abstract Lake Chad crescent with court lances and small horse-tail finials to evoke geography, Sahel cavalry, and an Islamic court.

The disputed palm-and-keys composition was explicitly excluded from the generation prompt.

## DYX — Luba revival

No reliable historical Luba Empire flag was identified.

The [Metropolitan Museum of Art overview of the Luba and Lunda empires](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/kingdoms-of-the-savanna-the-luba-and-lunda-empires) was used as cultural context rather than as a source image.

The fictional revival design centers a stylized asymmetrical lukasa memory board, a small set of enlarged memory nodes, copper color, and a restrained integrated stool base.

The emblem is intentionally abstract and does not copy a particular museum object.

## DZX — Lunda revival

No reliable historical Lunda Empire flag was identified.

The modern Lunda-Tchokwe movement flag discussed by [Flags of the World](https://www.crwflags.com/fotw/flags/ao-lunda.html) dates from a contemporary political context and was rejected as an imperial source.

The [Metropolitan Museum of Art overview of the Luba and Lunda empires](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/kingdoms-of-the-savanna-the-luba-and-lunda-empires) was used as broader cultural context.

The fictional revival design uses a monumental lukano-inspired bracelet ring, copper and ivory color, and a simple four-armed crossroads structure to suggest regalia and Lunda political and trade networks.

The emblem is original and does not reproduce a particular museum object.

## EMX — Kilwa revival

A flag-like mark associated with the East African coast appears in Fernão Vaz Dourado's 1576 atlas, available through the [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/2021668001/) and reproduced in a [Wikimedia Commons vector](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flag_used_in_the_east_coast_of_Tanzania_according_to_a_Portuguese_1576_map.svg).

[Flags of the World](https://www.crwflags.com/fotw/Flags/eaf-1576.html) notes uncertainty over whether the map symbol represented an actually used flag and over the location it marked.

It was rejected as a Kilwa historical flag because the evidence is ambiguous and postdates the end of the Kilwa Sultanate as an independent polity.

The fictional revival design instead combines a coral-stone pointed arch with a lateen dhow sail, an ocean-teal field, and a restrained maroon trade band.

## EQX — Zulu revival

No reliable historical Zulu Kingdom flag was identified.

The gold-green-red tricolor sometimes labeled as Zululand is conjectural and overlaps with Boer-republic flag traditions, as discussed in [Flags of the World material on Zululand](https://www.crwflags.com/fotw/flags/za_zu.html).

It was rejected rather than repeated as a historical standard.

The [British Museum Nguni shield record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af-1431) was retained as a material-culture reference rather than a pixel source.

The fictional revival design uses a monumental Nguni hide shield, irregular black-and-ivory hide patterning, crossed copper spears, and restrained ochre regalia tassels.

No person, face, animal figure, or caricature appears in the design.
