The parent-reported English slot 116 quote string is:

```text
Nature to be commanded must be obeyed
Francis Bacon
```

Attribution: Francis Bacon, *Novum Organum*, Book I, Aphorism III. The selected English wording is the 1858 translation in *The Works of Francis Bacon*, volume IV, edited by James Spedding, Robert Leslie Ellis, and Douglas Denon Heath. It is a translation of Bacon's Latin, not English wording written by Bacon. The source attribution is stored on the next line without added punctuation.

Primary wording check: the 1858 volume scan prints the selected words on page 47 and continues the sentence with a semicolon after `obeyed`; [the scanned 1858 edition](https://digicoll.lib.berkeley.edu/record/206653/files/b22240123_4_B000353568.pdf) and [the edition record and page excerpt](https://books.google.com/books?id=RmAJAAAAQAAJ) identify volume IV and the 1858 publication. The parent reports that opening the Berkeley PDF returned HTTP 403 in their environment, then reverified the wording against the [transcribed English Book I text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Novum_Organum/Book_I_%28Spedding%29), Aphorism III, and the [Latin Book I text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Novum_Organum/Liber_Primus). The direct scan URL remains as the primary-scan handoff; the parent-reported recheck rests on the primary-text transcriptions.

Attribution confidence: high for the source work, aphorism, and exact wording in the 1858 English edition. The translated wording should be attributed to Bacon's aphorism through that edition, not presented as a sentence he wrote in English.

Rights: Bacon's 1620 work and the 1858 English edition are public domain.

The parent-reported English slot 116 button string is:

```text
The Moving Finger writes
```

Source and attribution: Edward FitzGerald, *Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám*, first edition, 1859, quatrain LI, lines 512–516 in the linked transcription. The first-edition text reads `The Moving Finger writes; and, having writ,` at the beginning of the stanza. The selected fragment ends before the source semicolon, so the button uses an exact word excerpt without replacing source punctuation. See the [first-edition transcription and facsimile record](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Rubaiyat_of_Omar_Khayyam_%28tr._Fitzgerald%2C_1st_edition%29) and the [first-edition stanza text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Rubaiyat_of_Omar_Khayyam_%28tr._Fitzgerald%2C_1st_edition%29/The_Rubaiyat_of_Omar_Khayyam).

Attribution confidence: high that this wording appears in FitzGerald's 1859 English poem. Do not attribute the English phrase directly to the historical Omar Khayyám: FitzGerald's work is a creative English verse rendering and adaptation, and the exact Persian source for this quatrain is not securely established.

Rights: FitzGerald died in 1883, and the 1859 English text is public domain. No modern copyrighted line is selected.

Fit: Bacon gives the moment a restrained statement about nature's limits without accusing any person or country of causing the impact. FitzGerald's fragment adds a fatalistic allusion to events that cannot be written back out of history. Together they suit an irreversible impact reveal while leaving later atmospheric and political consequences open.

## Considered main-quote candidates

All eight candidates below were checked against the linked text or edition. Short excerpts preserve the source words and identify translation or context where relevant.

1. **Francis Bacon, selected.** Exact excerpt: `Nature to be commanded must be obeyed`. Source: *Novum Organum*, Book I, Aphorism III, published 1620; wording checked in the Spedding, Ellis, and Heath English edition, volume IV, London, 1858, page 47. [Scanned edition](https://digicoll.lib.berkeley.edu/record/206653/files/b22240123_4_B000353568.pdf). Fit: measured reflection on human limits before nature, appropriate after the completed impact. Confidence: high for the aphorism and exact 1858 translation, with the translation qualification above. Rights: public domain. **Selected.**

2. **Titus Lucretius Carus.** Exact excerpt: `A touch might be enough / To cause destruction`. Source: *De rerum natura* (*On the Nature of Things*), Book I, composed in the first century BCE; English translation by William Ellery Leonard, 1916. [Perseus text and edition record](https://beta.perseus.tufts.edu/urn:cts:latinLit:phi0550.phi001.perseus-eng1/). Fit: compact and unusually close to the physical trigger, but it is an excerpt from an argument about matter and cohesion rather than an asteroid or human response. Confidence: high for the words and source passage, explicitly Leonard's translation. Rights: ancient source and 1916 translation are public domain under the applicable long-expired terms. Backup if the parent prefers a more literal impact line over Bacon's philosophical framing.

3. **Ovid, translated by Arthur Golding.** Exact excerpt: `Like to a starre in Winter nights`. Source: *Metamorphoses*, Book II, Phaethon episode; Ovid's poem dates to about 8 CE, and Golding's English translation was published in 1567. [Book II text in Golding's translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Metamorphoses_%28tr._Golding%29/Book_2). Fit: a period literary image of a burning body seen as a falling star, with a direct celestial connection to the impact. Its archaic spelling and the surrounding story's mythic catastrophe make it a less restrained choice. Confidence: high for the quoted translation and source episode. Rights: public domain.

4. **George Gordon, Lord Byron.** Exact excerpt: `The bright sun was extinguish'd, and the stars / Did wander darkling in the eternal space,`. Source: “Darkness,” first published in 1816; checked against *Byron, Works*, 17 volumes, London, John Murray, 1832–33. [University of Toronto's Representative Poetry Online text and edition citation](https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/darkness). Fit: strong for a sky altered by dust, but the poem proceeds to global famine, extinction, and universal darkness. That scope exceeds the event's confirmed consequences. Confidence: high for author, text, and source edition. Rights: public domain.

5. **Revelation 8:10, King James Version.** Exact excerpt: `a great star from heaven, burning as it were a lamp`. Source: Revelation 8:10; the exact English is the King James Version, first published 1611, with the standard 1769 text shown at [Bible Gateway](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Rev.+8%3A10&version=KJV). The biblical book is traditionally attributed to John of Patmos and is commonly dated to the late first century CE, though authorship and dating are not certain. [Source and authorship overview](https://www.biblegateway.com/resources/encyclopedia-of-the-bible/Book-Revelation). Fit: the falling-star image is close, but its context is an apocalyptic angelic judgment and a named star poisoning waters, neither of which is established by this event. Confidence: high for the verse and KJV wording, medium for traditional personal authorship. Rights: the ancient scripture is not a modern copyrighted work; the King James Version's UK printing and distribution rights are administered by the Crown's patentee, so territorial status is not uniform. [UK government notice](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/copyright-notice-duration-of-copyright-term/copyright-notice-duration-of-copyright-term). Rejected for theological and consequence mismatch.

6. **William Shakespeare.** Exact excerpt: `The solemn temples, the great globe itself, / Yea, all which it inherit, shall dissolve`. Source: *The Tempest*, Act IV, scene 1, written around 1610–11 and first printed in the 1623 First Folio; wording checked in the [Folger Digital Text](https://www.folgerdigitaltexts.org/Tmp/). Fit: speaks to impermanence and physical loss, but predicts dissolution of the whole globe and so overstates this impact reveal. Confidence: high for the play, speaker Prospero, and text. Rights: public domain. Rejected for scale.

7. **Percy Bysshe Shelley.** Exact excerpt: `Nothing beside remains`. Source: “Ozymandias,” first published in *The Examiner* on 11 January 1818; the University of Virginia edition identifies its transcription as based on that first printing. [University of Virginia source text and publication note](https://anthology.lib.virginia.edu/exist/apps/LiC/work/ShelleyP/shelley-ozymandias). Fit: a forceful image of ruin and lost power, and a useful button-text alternative, but as the main quote it can imply that nothing survives around the impact. Confidence: high for wording, author, and first-publication source. Rights: public domain.

8. **Isaiah 34:4, King James Version.** Exact excerpt: `the heavens shall be rolled together as a scroll`. Source: Isaiah 34:4, checked in the [King James Version verse text](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+34%3A4&version=KJV). The book is traditionally attributed to Isaiah, but the chapter's compositional history and date are not certain; the quoted wording is the 1611 KJV translation as represented in the later standard text. Fit: a vivid image of a transformed sky, but the oracle is about divine judgment and cosmic dissolution rather than a localized impact with continuing political consequences. Confidence: high for the verse and translation, qualified for personal authorship and date. Rights: as with the other KJV candidate, UK rights are administered by the Crown's patentee; other jurisdictions differ. Rejected for apocalyptic scale.

## Considered button and cultural-remark candidates

1. **`The Moving Finger writes`**, selected. Direct fragment from FitzGerald's *Rubáiyát* (1859), quatrain LI; author of this English wording is Edward FitzGerald. [First-edition source](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Rubaiyat_of_Omar_Khayyam_%28tr._Fitzgerald%2C_1st_edition%29/The_Rubaiyat_of_Omar_Khayyam). It is a short cultural allusion to irreversible history and is public domain. Keep the attribution with FitzGerald, not as a direct quotation from Khayyám.

2. **`The rest is silence`**, backup. Direct fragment spoken by Hamlet at his death in Act V, scene 2 of Shakespeare's *Hamlet*; play composed around 1600–01, early printed texts include the 1604 Second Quarto and 1623 First Folio. The [Folger text](https://www.folgerdigitaltexts.org/Ham/) verifies the phrase in context. It makes a restrained death-and-silence reaction, but is less specific to the impact and appears as a lowercase fragment in the source. Shakespeare's text is public domain.

3. **`Nothing beside remains`**, backup. Direct fragment from Shelley's “Ozymandias,” first published in 1818. [University of Virginia first-printing transcription](https://anthology.lib.virginia.edu/exist/apps/LiC/work/ShelleyP/shelley-ozymandias). It evokes a ruined landscape and the failure of power, but can falsely suggest that no surrounding territory survives. Public domain.

4. **`iacta alea est`**, considered and rejected. Suetonius records the Latin phrase in *Divus Julius* 32, written around 121 CE, as words attributed to Julius Caesar as he crossed the Rubicon in 49 BCE. [Latin text](https://classics.andrewgadsden.com/library/suetonius/divus-julius/32) and [J. C. Rolfe's English translation](https://lexundria.com/suet_jul/32/r). It is an exact historical phrase for an irreversible threshold and the ancient text is public domain, but the account is retrospective rather than contemporary confirmation and the phrase suggests a human decision where the event is a natural impact. Confidence: high that Suetonius wrote it, lower that Caesar spoke those exact words.

5. **`dust to dust`**, considered and rejected. Fragment from the Burial of the Dead in the 1662 *Book of Common Prayer*, in its printed formula “earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust.” [The Church of England's 1662 edition](https://www.churchofengland.org/sites/default/files/2019-10/the-book-of-common-prayer-1662.pdf). It is a solemn and recognizable death-and-dust reference, but carries explicit Christian funeral context. The Church of England states that rights in the prayer-book text are vested in the Crown, so do not assume worldwide public-domain status from its age. [Rights statement](https://www.churchofengland.org/prayer-and-worship/worship-texts-and-resources/book-common-prayer). The selected FitzGerald fragment avoids that rights uncertainty.

6. **`This is the way the world ends`**, modern-work comparison, rejected. Direct seven-word fragment from T. S. Eliot's “The Hollow Men,” published in 1925. The [T. S. Eliot Foundation source](https://tseliot.com/foundation/this-is-the-way-the-world-ends-the-hollow-men-at-100/) identifies the line and poem; [Nobel Prize biography](https://www.nobelprize.org/laureate/619) records Eliot's death in 1965. This is a very short fragment, within the skill's 25-word limit for a non-lyrical source, but it has territory-dependent modern copyright risk and no global permission was established. The UK statutory term is generally life plus 70 years for literary works. [UK Copyright, Designs and Patents Act, section 12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/12). Its wording also implies that the whole world is ending, which this event does not establish. Do not use without rights review and a deliberate world-end role.
