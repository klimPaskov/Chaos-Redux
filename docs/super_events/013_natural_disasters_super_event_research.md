## 1. Meteor shower

Role: abnormal high-chaos escalation. The tone should be shared awe, fear, and public disbelief, not prophecy-for-prophecy's-sake.

### Selected recommendation

- Selected main quote: "Probably no celestial phenomenon ... was viewed with so much astonishment and fear by another class."
- Author or speaker: Denison Olmsted
- Source: "Observations on the Meteors of November 13th, 1833," *The American Journal of Science and Arts*
- Year: 1834
- Source URL: https://www.thebiblesays.org/copy-of-speaking-in-tongues-3
- Attribution confidence: Medium
- Copyright or public-domain note: 1834 scientific writing; public domain
- Selected button text or cultural remark: "Falling Stars."
- Remark source: Olmsted's public appeal on the phenomenon, reprinted in the *Richmond Enquirer* on November 26, 1833
- Remark source URL: https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/2020/09/how-newspapers-helped-crowdsource-a-scientific-discovery-the-1833-leonid-meteor-storm/

Fit: this package is stronger than a purely biblical omen line because the spec wants awe and practical horror from many observers at once. Olmsted gives that exact public reaction without sounding like generic end-times boilerplate.

### Considered quote candidates

- "Probably no celestial phenomenon ... astonishment and fear ..." Denison Olmsted, *American Journal of Science and Arts* 25 (1834). Best fit. Grounded, historical, and tied to mass spectatorship. Source URL: https://www.thebiblesays.org/copy-of-speaking-in-tongues-3
- "And the stars of heaven fell unto the earth ..." *Revelation* 6:13, King James Bible. Strong image, but more overtly apocalyptic and more overused. Source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_%28King_James%29/Revelation
- "Upon a windy night of stars that fell ..." Algernon Charles Swinburne, *A Song of Italy* (1867). Poetic and vivid, but less tied to disaster. Source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Songs_of_Two_Nations/A_Song_of_Italy

### Considered remark candidates

- "Falling Stars." Best pick. It is short, period-grounded, and reads like an unnerved official label rather than a meme. Source URL: https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/2020/09/how-newspapers-helped-crowdsource-a-scientific-discovery-the-1833-leonid-meteor-storm/
- "The stars fell like snow." Strong image from later historical retelling of the 1833 shower, but less direct than the Olmsted wording. Source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Music_of_the_Spheres/Chapter_12
- "Untimely figs." Scriptural fragment from *Revelation* 6:13. Memorable, but more symbolic than practical. Source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_%28King_James%29/Revelation

### Backup package

- Backup quote: "And the stars of heaven fell unto the earth ..."
- Backup remark: "Untimely figs."

## 2. Global rupture

Role: first worldwide physical-instability threshold. The tone should be foundations failing, transport breaking, and the ground itself becoming unreliable.

### Selected recommendation

- Selected main quote: "All the foundations of the earth are out of course."
- Author or speaker: Asaph, Psalm 82:5 in the King James Bible
- Source: *Psalms*, Psalm 82
- Year or period: Biblical text; KJV translation 1611
- Source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_%28King_James%29/Psalms
- Attribution confidence: High
- Copyright or public-domain note: Public domain
- Selected button text or cultural remark: "Its pillars tremble."
- Remark source: *Job* 9:6, King James Bible
- Remark source URL: https://biblehub.com/kjv/job/9-6.htm

Fit: this is the cleanest non-generic line for a world-scale earthquake concept. It emphasizes that the world's basic order is failing, which is exactly the role of the first global rupture threshold.

### Considered quote candidates

- "All the foundations of the earth are out of course." Psalm 82:5. Best fit. Short, specific, and less cliched than Isaiah 24. Source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_%28King_James%29/Psalms
- "The earth is utterly broken down ..." Isaiah 24:19. Very strong, but closer to broad apocalypse language. Source URL: https://biblehub.com/kjv/isaiah/24-19.htm
- "Which shaketh the earth out of her place ..." Job 9:6. Strong secondary option, especially if the event leans into aftershock violence. Source URL: https://biblehub.com/kjv/job/9-6.htm

### Considered remark candidates

- "Its pillars tremble." Best pick. Short, concrete, and excellent on a button. Source URL: https://biblehub.com/kjv/job/9-6.htm
- "Out of course." Sharper and stranger, but maybe too elliptical for players who do not recognize the phrase. Source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_%28King_James%29/Psalms
- "Though the earth be removed." Good backup if the super-event leans more solemn than shocking. Source URL: https://biblehub.com/kjv/psalms/46-2.htm

### Backup package

- Backup quote: "The earth is utterly broken down ..."
- Backup remark: "Though the earth be removed."

## 3. Massive eruption

Role: regional devastation with ash-darkened sky, evacuation, buried roads, and long atmospheric pressure.

### Selected recommendation

- Selected main quote: "Its form resembled that of a pine tree ..."
- Author or speaker: Pliny the Younger
- Source: Letter to Tacitus describing the eruption of Vesuvius
- Year or period: c. 79-112 AD; translation in public-domain editions
- Source URL: https://archive.org/stream/lettersofyounger00plinuoft/lettersofyounger00plinuoft_djvu.txt
- Attribution confidence: High
- Copyright or public-domain note: Classical source; public domain translation used
- Selected button text or cultural remark: "Everything is ashes."
- Remark source: Charles Ernest Beule, "How was Herculaneum Destroyed?"
- Year: 1872
- Remark source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Popular_Science_Monthly/Volume_2/December_1872/How_was_Herculaneum_Destroyed%3F

Fit: Pliny gives a real eruption image instead of stock judgment language. "Everything is ashes." then lands the aftermath cleanly and brutally, matching the spec's buried transport and darkened-day tone.

### Considered quote candidates

- "Its form resembled that of a pine tree ..." Pliny the Younger to Tacitus. Best fit. Instantly volcanic, visually specific, and historically traceable. Source URL: https://archive.org/stream/lettersofyounger00plinuoft/lettersofyounger00plinuoft_djvu.txt
- "I will cause the sun to go down at noon ..." Amos 8:9. Excellent for ash-darkened daylight, but less eruption-specific. Source URL: https://biblehub.com/amos/8-9.htm
- "The Destroyed and the Destroyer ..." Charles Dickens, *Pictures From Italy* (1846). Strong literary backup, especially if the image direction centers a ruined settlement beneath the volcano. Source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Pictures_From_Italy/Chapter_12

### Considered remark candidates

- "Everything is ashes." Best pick. Clear, bleak, and directly tied to the ash-burial aftermath. Source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Popular_Science_Monthly/Volume_2/December_1872/How_was_Herculaneum_Destroyed%3F
- "The sun at noon." Good allusive fragment from Amos 8:9 if the event image emphasizes daylight turned dark. Source URL: https://biblehub.com/amos/8-9.htm
- "The Destroyer." Strong single-word literary option, but more abstract and less materially specific than the ash line. Source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Pictures_From_Italy/Chapter_12

### Backup package

- Backup quote: "I will cause the sun to go down at noon ..."
- Backup remark: "The sun at noon."

## 4. Moving storm corridor

Role: first public reveal of a moving, map-visible storm hazard with path prediction and uncertainty.

### Selected recommendation

- Selected main quote: "The LORD hath his way in the whirlwind and in the storm ..."
- Author or speaker: Nahum 1:3, King James Bible
- Source: *Nahum* 1:3
- Year or period: Biblical text; KJV translation 1611
- Source URL: https://biblehub.com/nahum/1-3.htm
- Attribution confidence: High
- Copyright or public-domain note: Public domain
- Selected button text or cultural remark: "Rage, blow!"
- Remark source: William Shakespeare, *King Lear*, Act III, Scene 2
- Year or period: c. 1605-1606
- Remark source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/King_Lear_%281917%29_Yale/Text/Act_III

Fit: the selected quote is unusually good for the exact role because it includes both storm force and a path. That makes it a better match for a moving corridor than a generic storm lament. Shakespeare then gives the button a hard, immediate snap.

### Considered quote candidates

- "The LORD hath his way in the whirlwind and in the storm ..." Nahum 1:3. Best fit because it explicitly implies motion and path. Source URL: https://biblehub.com/nahum/1-3.htm
- "Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks! rage! blow!" Shakespeare, *King Lear*, Act III. Excellent energy, but better as a remark than as the main quote because the event is not only noise and violence; it is also movement and prediction. Source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/King_Lear_%281917%29_Yale/Text/Act_III
- "The mountains quake before him ..." Nahum 1:5. Strong storm-power backup, but less suited to the corridor's moving-path identity. Source URL: https://biblehub.com/kjv/nahum/1-5.htm

### Considered remark candidates

- "Rage, blow!" Best pick. Short, famous, and forceful without being too long. Source URL: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/King_Lear_%281917%29_Yale/Text/Act_III
- "In the whirlwind." Good clipped scriptural fragment if the team wants a colder tone. Source URL: https://biblehub.com/nahum/1-3.htm
- "Clouds are the dust ..." Very flavorful, but less immediate on a button. Source URL: https://biblehub.com/nahum/1-3.htm

### Backup package

- Backup quote: "Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks! rage! blow!"
- Backup remark: "In the whirlwind."

## Copyright risk note

- Selected text packages avoid modern copyrighted material entirely.
- The only moderate sourcing risk in this handoff is the meteor Olmsted excerpt, because the exact wording was confirmed through a secondary transcription that cites the 1834 journal rather than a directly browsed page image.

## Implementation recommendation for the main agent

- Meteor shower: use the Olmsted package if the goal is collective shock and public testimony. Use the *Revelation* backup only if the final image and audio lean explicitly omen-heavy.
- Global rupture: use the Psalm 82 package. It is the least generic and most structurally precise.
- Massive eruption: use the Pliny plus "Everything is ashes" package. It is the most event-specific set in the whole batch.
- Moving storm corridor: use the Nahum plus Shakespeare package. It matches both motion and violence better than the alternatives.
- If the main agent wants one consistent tonal rule across all four, the strongest pattern is historical or scriptural main quote plus a shorter fragmentary remark.
