## Quote research

Selected: Psalm 68:31, King James Version (1611): "Princes shall come out of Egypt; Ethiopia shall soon stretch out her hands unto God."

- Wording verified character for character on 2026-10-05 at <https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2068%3A31&version=KJV> and <https://www.blueletterbible.org/kjv/psa/68/31/>.
- Archived source: Project Gutenberg eBook #10, *The King James Version of the Bible*, archived by the Internet Archive at <http://web.archive.org/web/20260911033715/https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/10/pg10.txt> (snapshot 2026-09-11).
- Pan-African grounding: the verse is the founding text of Ethiopianism, the religious and political current that carried Pan-African thought from Edward Wilmot Blyden's 1880 address *Ethiopia Stretching Out Her Hands unto God; or, Africa's Service to the World* into the twentieth century. See Graham Duncan, "Ethiopianism in Pan-African Perspective, 1880-1920", *Studia Historiae Ecclesiasticae* 41 (2), 2015, <https://unisapressjournals.co.za/index.php/SHE/article/view/85>. In that tradition "Ethiopia" names Africa as a whole, so the line does not single out one host.
- Fit: the first clause shows princes coming to Africa, the role's foreign envoys, and the second gives the Gods their religious register without inventing a proverb or presenting a line as African wisdom.
- Rights: the King James text is public domain in the United States; the Crown's letters patent apply only to printing in the United Kingdom and do not restrict a short quotation.
- Confidence: high. The quotation is unaltered and keeps its original semicolon.
- Uniqueness: no other Chaos Redux super-event, event or document uses the verse (repository search, 2026-10-05).

Candidates considered and not used:

- Marcus Garvey, "Up, you mighty race, you can accomplish what you will." Not used: Garvey already supplies the slot 101 quote, and the published wording varies between collections, so it was not taken to verification.
- Kwame Nkrumah, Independence Day speech, Accra, 6 March 1957, on independence being meaningless unless linked to the total liberation of the continent. Not used: it speaks of liberation rather than the world's answer to Africa, and the rights status of the speech text is unclear.
- John Donne, Meditation XVII, as the main quote. Not used as the quote because it is a European voice, which suits the outside power's reaction better, so it became the remark.

## Remark research

Selected: "Never send to know for whom the bell tolls."

- Source: John Donne, *Devotions upon Emergent Occasions* (1624), Meditation XVII, "Nunc lento sonitu dicunt, Morieris". The full sentence reads "And therefore never send to know for whom the bell tolls; It tolls for thee." The same meditation says that every man is "a peece of the Continent" and that "Europe is the lesse" when a clod is washed away.
- Verified on 2026-10-05 at <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devotions_upon_Emergent_Occasions>, which quotes the passage from *Donne's Devotions* (Cambridge University Press, 1923), p. 98.
- Archived source: Project Gutenberg eBook #23772, *Devotions Upon Emergent Occasions*, archived by the Internet Archive at <http://web.archive.org/web/20260925072548/https://www.gutenberg.org/files/23772/23772-h/23772-h.htm> (snapshot 2026-09-25).
- Form: a short direct fragment with modern capitalisation of its first word and the original word order. It carries no attribution in game, as for the other Event 012 remarks.
- Fit: the button is the outside power's reaction. A foreign ministry learns that it no longer needs to ask whom Africa's demands concern, and Donne's "Continent" and "Europe is the lesse" sit under the line.
- Rights: public domain.
