# Event 011 Secret Alliance historical research

## Research purpose

Secret Alliance is a procedural alternate-history system. It should not copy one historical bloc. Research is used to identify repeatable structures that can make a hidden coalition believable against any player country.

The useful structures are:

- secret commitments hidden behind public agreements
- member recruitment through a shared threat narrative
- permanent liaison or committee mechanisms
- intelligence exchange before formal military alliance
- sabotage and subversion below open war
- turned agents and controlled information
- coalition weakness created by mixed motives and incompatible war aims

## Secret protocols and concealed spheres

The Secret Additional Protocol attached to the German-Soviet Nonaggression Pact in August 1939 shows how public diplomatic language could coexist with confidential territorial and political arrangements. The protocol divided spheres of influence in Eastern Europe and explicitly required secrecy.

### Design lessons

- The hidden pact should have private commitments that are more concrete than its public diplomacy.
- Members can agree on routes, claims, and future settlements before they create a public faction.
- Spoils promises should generate later conflict because private territorial expectations can overlap.
- Exposure of a protocol can damage cohesion even when the public coalition still forms.

### Boundary

The event should not reproduce the historical partition terms or treat every coalition as a land-partition conspiracy. The design takes the structure of concealed commitments, not the specific agreement.

Source: Yale Law School, Avalon Project, “Secret Additional Protocol,” August 23, 1939.

## The Anti-Comintern Pact as an expandable security framework

The German-Japanese agreement of November 25, 1936 formally described information exchange, consultation, defensive measures, and a standing committee. It also provided for jointly inviting third states to participate. The published wording therefore supplies a useful model for a pact that begins through intelligence and consultation, then expands through recruitment.

### Design lessons

- The founders need a recurring meeting or liaison structure, not only a one-time event.
- Intelligence exchange can be the first practical benefit of membership.
- A shared-threat narrative can recruit countries with different motives.
- Invitations to third states should be a continuing mechanic.
- A standing committee is a strong historical basis for the pact's hidden conference cycle.
- Public defensive language can conceal more aggressive planning without making every member equally committed.

### Boundary

The event is not an Anti-Comintern reskin. Its target and ideology are procedural. The pact can form against democratic, fascist, communist, or non-aligned targets. The research supports organizational structure only.

Source: United States Department of State, Office of the Historian, Agreement and Supplementary Protocol Between Japan and Germany, November 25, 1936.

## The Tripartite Pact and linked theaters

The United States Department of State describes the September 27, 1940 Tripartite Pact as linking the conflicts in Europe and Asia. That provides a useful design anchor for a coalition whose major sponsor can turn several regional grievances into one strategic front.

### Design lessons

- A major sponsor should join only when minor members provide useful geography, access, or another theater.
- A second major can create a real multi-theater threat rather than a larger stat bonus.
- Major entry should change the scale and coordination of the pact.
- The coalition should be able to connect otherwise separate regional disputes.

### Boundary

The event should not guarantee a global two-major alliance at Evolution III. The second major remains rare and strategically validated.

Source: United States Department of State, Office of the Historian, “Japan, China, the United States and the Road to Pearl Harbor, 1937-41.”

## Double Cross and controlled channels

MI5's official history describes the Double Cross System as the use of turned German agents to pass controlled disinformation. The system demonstrates that counterintelligence can do more than arrest an operative. A preserved channel can become more valuable than immediate exposure.

### Design lessons

- Turning a clerk, courier, envoy, or member should create an ongoing channel.
- A turned channel needs protection, exposure risk, and a concrete later payoff.
- False mobilization plans should weaken the coalition opening only if the channel survives until reveal.
- The player should sometimes choose between collecting Evidence and preserving deception.
- A turned founder or sponsor is a rare campaign-defining success, not a routine click.

### Boundary

The event should not imply that every captured agent can be turned. Motive, risk, access, and counterparty confidence matter.

Sources: MI5, “World War II” and “MI5 in World War II.” Imperial War Museums, “Collection Highlights: SOE and Secret War.”

## Simple sabotage and low-visibility disruption

The CIA's history of the OSS Simple Sabotage Field Manual describes methods that could undermine communications, transport, workplaces, and administration through actions that might resemble incompetence or routine failure. This is a useful model for the baseline stage, where the player should see disruption without immediate proof of a coalition.

### Design lessons

- Early sabotage should often be ambiguous.
- Transport errors, delayed communications, damaged tools, missing parts, and administrative obstruction can matter without permanent building destruction.
- Repeated methods create a pattern that investigators can compare.
- Severe violence belongs to Evolution II, after the system has established its quieter vocabulary.

### Boundary

The specification uses these themes at a high level. It does not reproduce procedural sabotage instructions. Player-facing incidents describe consequences and evidence rather than real-world operational methods.

Source: Central Intelligence Agency, “The Art of Simple Sabotage,” April 1, 2019, discussing the declassified OSS manual.

## Common enmity and alliance recruitment

In Thucydides' account of the Corcyraean appeal to Athens, the speakers argue that Corinth seeks to prevent opponents from becoming united by common enmity and urge Athens to form plans before waiting for hostile plans to mature. This is a useful classical statement of coalition logic.

### Design lessons

- The pact does not require friendship among members.
- A common target can temporarily overcome unrelated disputes.
- Recruitment should emphasize what the candidate fears will happen if it waits.
- The coalition can be strongest before victory makes its conflicting expectations concrete.

### Boundary

This is thematic support, not a claim that the procedural pact reproduces the Peloponnesian War.

Source: Thucydides, *History of the Peloponnesian War*, Book I, public-domain translation hosted by Project Gutenberg.

## Conspiracy recognition and warning failure

Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar* provides a compact literary model for a warning that names several conspirators and warns that security gives way to conspiracy. The scene is useful for the super-event and investigation themes because it centers on information arriving before the target chooses whether to act.

### Design lessons

- A clue should matter only when the player preserves and interprets it.
- A list of suspects can be correct, incomplete, or delivered too late.
- The reveal becomes more effective when the player remembers earlier warnings.

### Boundary

The event should not copy Caesar's assassination plot or use Roman imagery throughout. The literary source is suitable for a short public-domain button remark or backup quote only.

Source: William Shakespeare, *Julius Caesar*, Act II, Scene III, Project Gutenberg.

## Design synthesis

The strongest procedural model combines the sources rather than choosing one.

1. Three minors establish confidential commitments and a liaison committee.
2. They exchange intelligence and conduct ambiguous disruption.
3. They recruit other states through a shared-threat narrative.
4. A major sponsor can connect regional concerns into a strategic coalition.
5. The target can preserve a controlled channel and feed false plans.
6. Exposure converts secret commitments into a public faction.
7. Mixed motives become the main wartime fracture mechanic.

## Historically grounded working vocabulary

These are structural working terms, not final localisation.

- liaison committee
- standing conference
- supplementary protocol
- common defense consultations
- coordinating bureau
- invited adherent
- military liaison mission
- access arrangement
- confidential annex
- joint intelligence exchange
- security guarantee
- spheres of responsibility
- provisional war council

Avoid overusing bureaucratic nouns in player-facing event prose. They are useful for mechanics and documents, but the emotional center should remain the people, damaged systems, missing couriers, guarded borders, and visible preparations.

## What should not be added

- A fixed Axis-inspired ideology requirement
- Historical leaders or copied real treaties as members
- A new country tag or secret-society state
- A dedicated focus tree for every possible member
- A universal partition of the target
- A scripted world-end branch
- Real-world sabotage instructions
- Modern intelligence terminology that does not fit 1936 to 1945 presentation
