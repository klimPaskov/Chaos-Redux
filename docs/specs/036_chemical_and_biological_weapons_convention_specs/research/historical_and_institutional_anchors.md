# Historical and institutional anchors

## Research role

This note provides historical and institutional anchors for Event 036.

The event is deliberate alternate history.

No historical source supports a real international convention that legitimized chemical and biological first use in the way designed here.

The sources are used to make the fictional diplomatic process specific, period-aware, and internally coherent.

Research accessed on 1 September 2026.

## The 1925 Geneva Protocol

The 1925 Geneva Protocol prohibited the use in war of asphyxiating, poisonous, or other gases and of bacteriological methods of warfare.

Its title itself links chemical and biological methods as prohibited means of war.

The protocol did not create the later comprehensive system of bans on development, production, and possession.

The ICRC’s treaty materials and historical summary describe it as a use prohibition and explain that later biological and chemical conventions extended the legal framework.

Design use:

- Event 036 can plausibly begin by rewriting an existing use taboo without inventing an entirely new category of diplomacy.
- Retaliation reservations fit the historical importance of reciprocity and conditional state commitments.
- Chemical and biological policy can begin together while later treaty families treat them differently.
- The opening should sound like a revision of accepted rules of war, not a sudden scientific discovery.

Sources:

- [ICRC treaty database, Geneva Protocol of 17 June 1925](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/geneva-gas-prot-1925)
- [ICRC, International humanitarian law treaties: essential documents](https://blogs.icrc.org/cross-files/international-humanitarian-law-treaties-essential-documents/)

## The 1932 Conference for the Reduction and Limitation of Armaments

The United Nations Audiovisual Library of International Law records that the League of Nations convened the Conference for the Reduction and Limitation of Armaments in 1932.

The conference discussed a broad disarmament convention and included chemical and bacterial warfare provisions.

The cited draft language treated chemical abstention as subject to reciprocity while stating the bacterial undertaking without the same reservation.

The conference failed to produce a legal instrument and was eventually discontinued.

Design use:

- A large international conference is period-appropriate for a 1930s campaign.
- Ratification, reservations, failed agendas, and repeated attempts have a direct institutional precedent.
- Chemical and biological clauses do not need identical reservation logic.
- Conference failure should leave a continuing diplomatic dispute instead of ending the event system.
- The convention coordinator should be an administrative host, not a supranational government.

Source:

- [United Nations Audiovisual Library of International Law, Biological Weapons Convention historical overview](https://legal.un.org/avl/ha/cpdpsbbtwd/cpdpsbbtwd.html)

## Later comprehensive biological prohibition

The Biological Weapons Convention moved beyond a battlefield-use rule and prohibited development, production, and stockpiling of biological and toxin weapons.

The United Nations legal history describes the convention as a later comprehensive step that emerged after earlier attempts at general disarmament failed.

Current United Nations discussions still refer to the absence of a BWC verification mechanism and the continuing role of confidence-building measures.

Design use:

- Event 036’s biological branch should have weaker verification than its chemical branch.
- Laboratory declarations, confidence-building measures, intelligence, and limited inspections provide the credible model. A universal perfect inspection system would not.
- Biological cooperation can create research and containment benefits while preserving suspicion and exposure risk.
- Catastrophic outbreaks need a higher Condemnation floor because a permissive treaty cannot make uncontrolled spread administratively routine.

Sources:

- [United Nations Audiovisual Library of International Law, BWC historical overview](https://legal.un.org/avl/ha/cpdpsbbtwd/cpdpsbbtwd.html)
- [United Nations transcript, Sixth Session of the Working Group on strengthening the BWC, 14 August 2025](https://transcripts.un.org/en/asset/k1h/k1hxluho1c)

## Chemical declarations, challenge inspections, and protection

The Chemical Weapons Convention prohibits development, production, acquisition, stockpiling, retention, transfer, and use of chemical weapons.

The OPCW overview describes national declarations, an extensive verification annex, consultations, challenge inspections, and assistance and protection for states attacked or threatened with chemical weapons.

OPCW materials also describe declarations covering weapons, facilities, chemicals, materials, equipment, and destruction plans.

Design use:

- Event 036 can invert the purpose of a strong chemical verification structure while retaining its institutional forms.
- Chemical treaty families can use declarations, production records, challenge inspections, facility commitments, assistance, and protection.
- Protection and assistance should remain valuable to reservation members and opponents.
- A chemical inspection system can use stronger verification. A biological registry should remain less reliable.
- Inspections should reveal only evidence that the game can prove.

Sources:

- [OPCW, Chemical Weapons Convention](https://www.opcw.org/chemical-weapons-convention)
- [OPCW, The Verification Regime of the Chemical Weapons Convention: An Overview](https://www.opcw.org/media-centre/news/2008/11/verification-regime-chemical-weapons-convention-overview)
- [OPCW, Eliminating Chemical Weapons](https://www.opcw.org/our-work/eliminating-chemical-weapons)

## Nuclear consequences and the remaining floor

The ICRC describes nuclear weapons as uniquely destructive and emphasizes the catastrophic humanitarian consequences of their use, including blast, heat, radiation, large civilian casualties, destroyed medical capacity, and long-term illness.

The ICRC also notes the importance of no-first-use and other risk-reduction policies in current legal and humanitarian discussion.

Design use:

- Event 036 may weaken nuclear restraint without treating nuclear warfare as ordinary chemical warfare.
- Nuclear Condemnation relief must remain bounded.
- Populated-capital use requires a high floor.
- Thermonuclear use receives no convention relief.
- Nuclear first-use AI must retain payload scarcity, target value, fallout, civilian harm, retaliation risk, and national-survival checks.

Sources:

- [ICRC, Nuclear weapons](https://www.icrc.org/en/law-and-policy/nuclear-weapons)
- [ICRC, International humanitarian law and the challenges of contemporary armed conflicts](https://international-review.icrc.org/articles/reports-and-documents-ihl-and-the-challenges-of-contemporary-armed-conflicts-927)

## Design conclusions

The research supports an Event 036 structure built from:

- a use-taboo reversal
- national reservations
- repeated multilateral conferences
- partial ratification
- chemical declarations and stronger inspections
- biological confidence-building and weaker verification
- protection and assistance obligations
- first-use debates
- nuclear restraint that weakens but never disappears
- a late international research organization with strict provider boundaries

The research does not support copying modern treaty institutions directly into the 1930s setting.

Final localisation and visuals should use period diplomatic, military, scientific, and medical institutions while preserving the event’s alternate-history premise.
