# Event 049 Doomsday Specification

## Part 3: Doomsday Societies and Country Responses

## Doomsday Societies as a movement family

Doomsday Societies are a public umbrella term for thousands of organizations that share the predicted date while disagreeing about cause, duty, authority, and the proper use of the remaining time.

The umbrella must never become one uniform cult.

A country can contain religious observance groups, secular pacifists, survival settlements, communal kitchens, ecstatic crowds, debt abolition leagues, military loyalists, revolutionary cells, skeptical continuity committees, and commercial fraud at the same time.

The movement’s dominant and secondary currents determine the country’s incidents and political route.

## Core movement currents

The labels below are working design labels, not final localisation.

### Pacifist societies

Pacifist societies argue that no person should die in a war when ordinary history may be ending.

They organize pledges, legal defense, prisoner support, sanctuary, public marches, military-family networks, negotiations, and relief work.

Their strongest political demands include:

- Suspension of conscription.
- Release of conscientious objectors.
- Withdrawal from offensive wars.
- Armistice talks.
- Conversion of arms production to food, shelter, transport, and medicine.
- Transfer of military stores to civilian reserves.

A disciplined pacifist current can preserve civilian order while weakening military power.

### Prophetic and calendar-centered societies

These societies focus on calculation, signs, dreams, numerology, astronomical patterns, scripture, folk memory, or supposed independent confirmation.

They produce the strongest direct Conviction pressure, especially after Evolution I.

They may be centralized around famous interpreters or decentralized through copied calendars and local witnesses. Centralized groups are easier to disrupt and more vulnerable to scandal. Decentralized groups survive repression and reinterpretation more easily.

### Devotional and penitential societies

These societies prepare through prayer, confession, pilgrimage, fasting, charity, reconciliation, burial rites, and final observance.

They can strengthen relief and social discipline when allowed to operate. They can also pull labor away from production, schools, and military service.

They are the most likely current to support a negotiated concordat with government, provided the state does not demand that one tradition monopolize the Last Day.

### Survivalist societies

Survivalists expect physical destruction, social collapse, or divine selection.

They stockpile food, fuel, medicine, tools, weapons, seed, records, vehicles, and shelter materials. Some form remote settlements. Some sell fraudulent protection. Some provide useful emergency capacity.

The current should split between civic preparedness and exclusionary hoarding. A government that cooperates with the first can gain real reserves. A government that tolerates the second can worsen famine, migration, and local violence.

### Communal societies

Communal societies argue that ordinary states and markets have already lost legitimacy. They build kitchens, cooperatives, local councils, shared farms, childcare networks, clinics, repair groups, and autonomous settlements.

They are central to peaceful government replacement and the Communal Stewards current inside the Final Assembly.

Their strength grows where municipal government, labor organization, village institutions, or mutual aid already exist.

### Ascetic societies

Ascetic groups reject consumption, property accumulation, luxury, and long-term economic growth.

They can reduce consumer demand and preserve some reserves. They can also accelerate industrial contraction, tax collapse, and the abandonment of urban employment.

They are more likely to demand debt forgiveness, land access, and the cancellation of prestige projects.

### Hedonist societies

Hedonist groups believe that restraint no longer matters.

They drive spending, absenteeism, gambling, public festivals, theft, consumption of reserves, and rapid liquidation of savings.

They are poorly organized for government capture, but they can hollow out institutions and create financial disorder that other currents exploit.

They should not be portrayed as a single criminal movement. Their effects range from family spending and public celebration to fraud and predation.

### Apocalyptic state-loyalists

These groups accept the date while insisting that the nation, army, ruler, revolution, or empire must reach it undefeated.

They support emergency mobilization, final offensives, discipline, sacrifice, and state-directed preparation.

They provide the social basis for the Nothing Left to Lose posture. Their route can temporarily sustain War Support while creating severe post-window collapse and suppression risk.

### Revolutionary and avenging societies

These groups grow from violent repression, occupation, atrocity, elite hoarding, and the belief that the old order must be judged before the Last Day.

They target prisons, police archives, property records, military depots, and government offices. They can overthrow governments under Evolution II.

Their growth must come from campaign causes. They should not appear at high strength only because the country has a particular religion or ideology.

### Skeptical continuity networks

These networks doubt the prediction or refuse to let it decide public policy.

They include teachers, engineers, archivists, medical workers, civil servants, officers, journalists, clergy, union organizers, and local officials who keep institutions functioning.

They are not represented as a society demanding the end. They are a hidden counter-network that increases institutional continuity and creates strong failed-date recovery.

## Movement lifecycle

### Emergence

Small groups form around existing institutions, local rumors, public meetings, veteran networks, congregations, labor organizations, universities, newspapers, markets, and families.

### Consolidation

Successful groups gain meeting places, supply routes, public spokespeople, legal support, local councils, and links to foreign societies.

### Specialization

The movement develops a dominant current based on local conditions and government response.

### Political representation

Large movements demand consultation, prisoner release, demobilization, debt policy, reserve access, and public control of final preparations.

### Parallel administration

Under Evolution II, organized societies can operate food systems, courts, shelters, municipal councils, military-refusal networks, and local taxation outside normal government.

### Capture, accommodation, collapse, or persistence

The country may install a Doomsday administration, integrate the movement into state institutions, destroy its open organization, or survive until the failed date.

## Country response postures

Each country selects one principal posture. The working labels below are not final localisation.

### Civic Continuity

The government refuses to endorse the prediction and keeps ordinary institutions open through evidence, public debate, continuity missions, and practical reassurance.

Main strengths:

- Best long-term institutional continuity.
- Strongest recovery if the date fails.
- Lower suppression backlash.
- Better education, research, and administrative retention.

Main risks:

- Requires visible success and public trust.
- Failed missions are interpreted as proof that the government is powerless.
- A major external confirmation shock can damage the posture severely.
- It provides less immediate control than repression.

### Concordat of Vigil

The government works with religious, civic, pacifist, communal, and municipal leaders to preserve order while allowing preparation and observance.

Main strengths:

- Converts movement organization into relief, shelter, and social stability.
- Reduces violent conflict between state and society.
- Preserves more local administration in high-Conviction countries.
- Creates a stable path into a custodial Doomsday administration if needed.

Main risks:

- Shares legitimacy with nonstate institutions.
- Public cooperation can validate the date and raise Conviction.
- Radical or exclusionary currents can exploit access.
- The government may lose the ability to withdraw from the agreement.

### Emergency Order

The government restricts societies, arrests organizers, controls publications, protects strategic industry, and enforces military duty.

Main strengths:

- Reduces open organization and visible disruption quickly.
- Protects priority factories, railways, and military units in the short term.
- Delays local government capture.

Main risks:

- Builds suppression backlash.
- Can create martyrs and underground networks.
- Risks real deaths, Condemnation, and revolutionary takeover.
- Performs poorly after a failed date if abuses become public.

### National Preparation

The government treats the threat as possible and redirects national policy toward shelters, reserves, records, continuity sites, and emergency settlement.

Main strengths:

- Builds assets that remain useful against famine, bombing, plague, disaster, and displacement.
- Reduces final-month disorder.
- Protects archives, seed, medicine, and skilled personnel.

Main risks:

- Official preparation raises Conviction.
- Long-term civilian and military projects are cancelled or delayed.
- Hoarding and unequal access can provoke unrest.
- A failed date leaves debt, idle shelters, and distorted investment.

### Peace Before the End

This posture is available to countries at war or under severe mobilization pressure.

The government accepts strong pacifist influence and prioritizes truce, prisoner release, demobilization, and conversion of arms production.

Main strengths:

- Can end unwinnable or unpopular wars.
- Reduces desertion through negotiated service changes.
- Converts organized pacifism into relief and reconstruction.

Main risks:

- Aggressive enemies may exploit demobilization.
- Allies can treat the country as unreliable.
- Offensive military capacity declines before peace is guaranteed.
- A failed negotiation can destroy government legitimacy.

### Nothing Left to Lose

This late militarist posture becomes available under high Conviction, strong state-loyalist currents, or a government committed to final victory.

The regime argues that defeat before the Last Day would be the only unforgivable outcome.

Main strengths:

- Can restore short-term War Support, deployment, and offensive tempo.
- Attracts apocalyptic state-loyalists.
- Resists pacifist government capture.

Main risks:

- Accelerates casualties, coercion, depletion, and post-window collapse.
- Creates severe peace pressure among civilians and unwilling soldiers.
- Raises the chance of mutiny or revolutionary takeover if the offensive fails.
- Produces the worst failed-date military and political aftermath.

## Posture selection and change

The first response should give the player a concise posture choice after the opening super-event.

Posture selection should not be a free reversible toggle.

A country can normally change posture once through a costly emergency realignment after one of these events:

- A major Conviction threshold is crossed.
- A war changes direction sharply.
- A government loses control of key institutions.
- Evolution I or II activates.
- A major international confirmation or disconfirmation shock occurs.

The change should consume political authority, stability, institutional capacity, or movement trust according to the direction of change.

## Country-specific response differences

### Stable democracies

Stable democracies are better at public hearings, open evidence, negotiated prisoner policy, and municipal cooperation. They are vulnerable to party fragmentation, election capture, public spending demands, and legal paralysis.

### Authoritarian regimes

Authoritarian regimes can protect priority institutions and suppress open organization quickly. They are vulnerable to hidden backlash, unreliable reporting, elite flight, army refusal, and sudden collapse when coercive legitimacy breaks.

### Totalitarian or highly mobilized regimes

Highly mobilized regimes can use apocalyptic state-loyalism to sustain war. The route should carry high casualty, atrocity, and post-date costs.

### Colonial empires

Colonial governments face different interpretations in the metropole and subject territories. The Last Day can be read as the end of imperial authority, a chance for local autonomy, a religious judgment, or proof that the empire cannot protect its subjects.

Colonial repression should strengthen anti-colonial and communal currents. Cooperative relief can preserve order while empowering local councils that later demand political change.

### Occupied countries and governments in exile

Occupied populations can interpret the date as liberation, judgment, or final resistance. Governments in exile can use the movement for solidarity or lose control to local networks.

The occupier’s suppression policy and the occupied country’s resistance state should shape outcomes.

### Countries in civil war

Each side can develop a different movement composition and posture. The event should not require one national value that ignores the split.

Peace pressure may produce local truces, prisoner exchanges, or joint final observances without ending the civil war automatically.

### Small states

Small states should retain meaningful agency. Their lower aggregate weight is balanced by faster movement penetration, stronger local institutions, and the ability to become visible examples of continuity, demobilization, or peaceful government transfer.

### Major powers

Major-power choices have greater global effect, especially demobilization, government replacement, and Final Assembly membership. Their country contribution remains capped so one major cannot determine the whole meter.

## Leadership and portrait handling

Most voluntary administrations should retain existing leaders or use already defined political figures.

A revolutionary takeover can use a people-free institutional council portrait when no grounded individual is appropriate. The asset package should plan a small set of institutional portraits, such as a civic hall council, a communal delegates’ table, a religious and civic synod, a military refusal committee, and an emergency relief board.

The portrait must not invent a specific real person for a grounded country. A country-specific grounded leader requires the portrait source workflow and attributed source material.

## Local incident families

The societies should appear through recurring but bounded report families.

Recommended incident families include:

- Students leave universities and technical schools.
- Officer cadets refuse commissions.
- Families liquidate savings and debts.
- Pacifist marches surround recruitment offices.
- Pilgrims and vigils block transport routes.
- Communal kitchens replace failing municipal services.
- Survival settlements request land, tools, and seed.
- Fraudulent protection schemes drain household wealth.
- Workers abandon a shipyard or long industrial project.
- Religious and civic councils offer to manage shelters.
- Police raids create prisoners, martyrs, and hidden cells.
- Local mayors transfer authority to a Last Days council.
- Soldiers refuse an offensive while agreeing to defend nearby civilians.
- Hedonist festivals consume reserves and overwhelm public order.
- Skeptical teachers reopen a school under threat.
- Archivists protect records from destruction or seizure.

An incident should mention dynamic country, state, city, institution, war, or movement-current context where possible.

## Society success and failure

Society success should not always harm the country.

A pacifist or communal group can keep food moving, shelter refugees, preserve public order, and reduce local violence. A survivalist settlement can preserve seed and medicine. A devotional network can staff hospitals and burial services.

Society failure can include fraud, hoarding, factional violence, collapse of a promised settlement, leadership scandal, or destructive misinterpretation.

The player’s response should determine whether movement capacity becomes relief, political competition, institutional replacement, or violence.

## Government capture paths

Under Evolution II, a country can become a Doomsday administration through one of five routes.

### Voluntary transfer

The government accepts that its remaining purpose is custodial and invites movement representatives into authority.

### Parliamentary transfer

A legislature or constitutional process forms a Last Days administration after ordinary parties lose membership and confidence.

### Municipal cascade

Enough local governments transfer services and authority that the national government formalizes the new system.

### Military refusal

The armed forces will defend civilians but refuse offensive orders. A caretaker administration forms around logistics, medical services, veterans, and local government.

### Revolutionary collapse

Violent suppression, hunger, elite flight, and high organization produce an uprising that replaces the old government.

The route affects leadership, idea state, focus weighting, Final Assembly current, failed-date aftermath, and the risk of further violence.

## Movement restraint rules

- A peaceful movement should not become violent without a cause.
- A high-Conviction religious movement should not automatically become theocratic.
- A pacifist movement should not automatically surrender civilians to an invading enemy.
- A survivalist group should not automatically become a bandit organization.
- A communal movement should not automatically become communist or anarchist in game ideology terms.
- A revolutionary current should grow from repression, occupation, atrocity, hunger, or political exclusion.
- A country’s real religion or culture should not determine one fixed movement current.
- The event should preserve uncertainty about the prediction without using religious believers as a joke.
