# Historical and Folklore Research for Event 018

This research supplies inspiration and design constraints. It does not turn Event 018 into a historical reenactment. The discovery remains random and the subterranean species is fictional.

## Large discoveries and concession politics

Saudi Arabia’s Dammam No. 7 reached commercial oil production in 1938 after a concession agreement, years of surveying, and repeated drilling. It is a strong period example of a discovery that rapidly changed a state’s economic horizon and later required major export infrastructure. Event 018 uses this pattern for the transition from survey result to roads, rail, company towns, foreign contracts, and new trade routes.

Source: Saudi Aramco, Our History
https://www.aramco.com/en/about-us/our-history

Mexico’s 1938 expropriation of foreign oil-company assets shows how resource ownership, labor conflict, compensation, national control, and foreign pressure could become a diplomatic crisis. Event 018 uses this as inspiration for state authority, foreign concession, nationalization, compensation, and contract-breaking routes.

Source: United States Office of the Historian, Mexican Expropriation of Foreign Oil, 1938
https://history.state.gov/milestones/1937-1945/mexican-oil

## International administration and demilitarization

The Saar Basin was governed by a League of Nations commission after the First World War, while France held rights over the coal mines. The governing arrangement also prohibited military service and fortifications in the territory. Event 018 does not copy the Saar settlement, but it uses the precedent to support a negotiated resource commission, foreign exploitation rights, and a demilitarized field state when several countries fear an armed seizure.

Sources: United States Office of the Historian, Section IV, Saar Basin
https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1919Parisv13/ch12subch4

United States Office of the Historian, Government of the Territory of the Saar Basin
https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1919Parisv13/ch12subsubsubch2

## Resource-bearing border conflict

The Gran Chaco dispute grew from a long territorial conflict. Contemporary expectations and discoveries of oil raised the stakes, though oil should not be treated as the sole cause of the war. Event 018 uses this cautiously. A field near a disputed border can intensify an existing rivalry, but it cannot create an instant war without claims, failed negotiations, troop concentration, and a willing aggressor.

Sources: Library of Congress country profiles for Bolivia and Paraguay
https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/frd/copr/Bolivia.pdf
https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/frd/copr/Paraguay.pdf

United States Office of the Historian, 1938 Chaco peace treaty text
https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1938v05/d148

## Strategic materials and wartime interest

USGS historical material notes that concerns about access to strategic and critical minerals grew after the First World War and became acute during the Second World War. This supports dynamic foreign interest based on import dependence, war status, resource deficits, stockpiles, and proximity. A resource-poor belligerent should value the field more than a distant country with abundant domestic supply.

Sources: United States Geological Survey, statement on strategic and critical minerals
https://www.usgs.gov/congressional/statement-charles-g-groat-director-usgs-6

United States Geological Survey, Critical Minerals in Subduction-related Magmatic Systems
https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/sir20235082/full

## Mining health and worker protection

Mining exposes workers to dusts, toxic chemicals, gases, and long-term occupational disease. ILO material identifies chemical exposure as a major mining hazard and documents the historical recognition of silicosis. Event 018 uses ordinary industrial hazards as the believable first explanation for coughing, lesions, fatigue, corrosion, and missing shifts. The supernatural process remains fictional and should emerge only after ordinary safety measures stop explaining the pattern.

Sources: International Labour Organization, Chemical Exposures in Mining
https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2024-05/ILO_Report_Mining-r11.pdf

International Labour Organization, historical development of occupational-disease protection
https://researchrepository.ilo.org/view/pdfCoverPage?download=true&filePid=13115324700002676&instCode=41ILO_INST

## Extractive conflict and local pressure

World Bank and UNDP research treats extractive conflict as both economic and political. Disputes can center on control of the resource, revenue, transport, local communities, and company conduct. Event 018 uses this to connect field development with local labor, smuggling, foreign companies, rail corridors, border pressure, and decisions about who receives the benefit.

Source: UNDP and World Bank, Preventing Conflict in Resource-Rich Countries
https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/873671468000627354/pdf/104022-WP-UNDP-WB-ValueChain-Conflict-FinalPaper1-June2015-PUBLIC-002.pdf

## Mine spirits, dangerous ores, and underground knocking

USGS recounts the link between cobalt, the German word kobold, dangerous arsenic-bearing ores, and miners’ belief that a goblin had spoiled the silver and made workers ill. This is a strong inspiration for Evolution II. The game can let early witnesses use local mine-spirit language without confirming that the creatures match any one folklore tradition.

Source: United States Geological Survey, Kobold to Commodity
https://www.usgs.gov/news/science-snippet/kobold-commodity-a-halloween-minerals-story

University of Exeter research describes Cornish knockers as supernatural miners whose traditions changed with industrialization and followed Cornish workers through the international mining diaspora. The knocking was linked to settling timbers and to underground presences. Event 018 uses this as inspiration for regional flavor skins, warning knocks, missing tools, and workers interpreting the same phenomena through different local traditions.

Source: University of Exeter, Knockers, Tommyknockers and the Question of Ghosts
https://www.exeter.ac.uk/research/centres/ics/research/cornish-diaspora/knockerstommyknockersandthequestionofghosts/

National Coal Mining Museum material also records mine-dwelling Tommyknocker traditions and the use of different names in different mining cultures. This supports procedural regional flavor without making one culture the canonical source of the cave species.

Source: National Coal Mining Museum for England, Tommyknockers
https://www.ncm.org.uk/news/tommyknockers/

## Research-derived design rules

1. Discovery begins with practical surveys, samples, wells, pits, rail plans, and labor demand.
2. Foreign reactions depend on strategic need, access, ownership, and contracts.
3. A border war requires a prior dispute and visible escalation.
4. Demilitarization is a negotiated response to international fear, not a random punishment.
5. Early sickness has credible industrial explanations.
6. Folklore varies by region and should remain interpretation rather than exposition.
7. The cave species receives an original generated identity. It is not named after a real ethnic, religious, or national tradition.
8. Worker deaths use the shared Deaths system and real population loss.
9. The event must show transport and local administration, since extraction without movement and governance is shallow.
10. The owner can choose national control, regulated development, foreign concession, militarized extraction, suspension, or closure.
