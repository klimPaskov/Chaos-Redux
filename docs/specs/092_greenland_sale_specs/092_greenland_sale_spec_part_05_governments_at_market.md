# Greenland Sale: governments at the market

## Buyers with different ambitions

The market should make several kinds of country behave recognizably differently without prescribing a list of winning tags.
A regional consolidator is interested in nearby claims and disconnected holdings it can join.
A naval power is interested in a useful chain of ports and bases.
An industrial importer is interested in an accessible resource region.
A recovering seller may later buy a smaller and more defensible possession with part of an earlier receipt.
A government fighting for survival normally values construction at home above distant expansion.

These are interpretations of the country's live circumstances rather than permanent national personalities assigned at game start.
A continental state that acquires a coastline can develop a new maritime interest.
An island empire that loses its fleet can stop treating a remote base as an attractive purchase.
A country whose claims expand through another event can reassess an existing auction without having been invited by a special historical exception.

A subject, minor, or recently released country may be the most interested buyer of a particular lot.
An enormous industrial power may be the least interested because the same lot adds almost nothing to its network.
The AI must preserve that difference instead of multiplying every country's bid weight by factory count until majors win every sale.
Economic strength governs what can be funded, not whether every purchase is desirable.

## Preparing to compete

The initial announcement lets a government assess its interest before diverting factories.
The AI compares the opening reserve, its expected upper willingness, the time available to accumulate funds, and the construction it would forgo.
A country that cannot reach even the opening reserve in a plausible timeframe should not begin a futile heavy funding commitment.
An already funded country can wait rather than producing unnecessary extra reserve.

The intended retained domestic investment margin becomes stricter during an endangered homeland war, severe supply failure, or an urgent reconstruction program.
It may become more permissive for a nearby core, a genuinely indispensable operating position, or a resource shortage that the lot can actually relieve.
These adjustments change the amount the AI is willing to commit, not the requirement to have paid that amount in advance.

Several auctions require a portfolio choice.
A government ranks the opportunities it can plausibly use and normally funds its highest-priority unresolved opportunity first.
It should not lead three individually attractive lots when the combined acquisition leaves it unable to invest in any of them.
A fully funded second purchase remains possible when the country genuinely has surplus resources and a coherent plan.

## Staying in or dropping out

Before each attempted increase, the AI obtains the current next bid, confirms the lot remains valid, and recomputes any materially changed willingness inputs.
It bids only while the new amount remains worthwhile and affordable.
If another country outbids it above that ceiling, it stops.
Its next useful action may be to restore ordinary construction, invest its returned reserve at home, or follow a different lot.

The AI does not continue because it has already spent time watching the auction.
Returned escrow is not a loss that must be recovered by winning at any price.
A rival's participation may increase the strategic value of keeping it out, but that premium is bounded and does not erase domestic survival needs.
A funded government may also refuse to bid when occupation or access risks make ownership unattractive.

Re-entry is allowed when the campaign materially changes the calculation.
A resource shortage, a new claim, a captured nearby base, or an earlier sale receipt may justify a fresh bid.
A government does not become permanently barred merely because it previously stopped following the auction.
Its new offer still needs to beat the live price and restart the same 10-day clock.

The AI should respond within the closing window rather than wait for an unrelated monthly decision evaluation.
The intended response opportunities occur on a bounded cadence of at most 5 days for registered interested bidders.
The exact weighted execution behavior requires the project probability tools and in-game confirmation.
A weight is not to be described as a literal percentage chance without that evidence.

## Selling for a reason

Later sellers need an affirmative reason to turn territory into reserve.
The common motives are reducing the cost of defending a remote possession, funding urgently needed domestic construction, abandoning an untenable colonial position, consolidating a scattered empire, or deliberately transferring a strategic responsibility to another power.
A state is not listed merely because it passes a geographic trigger.

The AI compares a plausible receipt with both the territory's continuing contribution and the actual use it has for the proceeds.
A government with no affordable or legal construction opportunity and no plausible subsequent purchase has less reason to liquidate a useful possession.
It should not sell a productive colony to accumulate an idle balance indefinitely.
A government with a damaged, defendable mainland and a vulnerable outlying possession has a much stronger case.

Before publication, the seller identifies at least one intended use of the likely proceeds.
This is an AI planning priority and a player-facing explanation, not a compulsory earmark on every normal seller receipt.
The player can change its mind after settlement.
The AI should reconsider its reconstruction plan if the campaign has made the original project impossible.

A failed sale should teach the seller that its price, lot, or timing was unattractive.
After the relisting cooldown, it may choose a lower permitted reserve, prepare a smaller coherent lot, or retain the possession.
It must not continuously republish the same unaffordable offer until the world is filled with auction notifications.

## Overlords, allies, and rivals

Faction membership is not a purchase discount or a guaranteed bid.
Allies may value a useful transfer and may also compete for the same scarce position.
A seller's AI can consider the danger of losing an essential shared base when deciding whether to list, but once it publishes a valid auction it cannot secretly veto an otherwise eligible winner on faction grounds.
The published conditions have to be the actual conditions.

Related countries are not barred from genuine transactions simply because they are related.
Their bids still require real reserve and their exchanges create no additional value.
AI governments must never submit dummy bids solely to raise the price for their overlord, subject, or ally.
Achievement conditions that require meaningful competition distinguish independent competitors from financially or politically controlled bidders.

Direct enemies cannot transact during their war.
A third-country rival may bid, and its possible ownership can affect willingness on both sides.
A seller that cannot tolerate any eligible rival owning a strategic lot should keep the lot or set a defensible reserve before publication.
It does not get an unannounced right to cancel after discovering that the rival can afford it.

## When the AI should do nothing

An empty or quiet market is acceptable.
Countries should not manufacture buyers and sellers to keep a mechanic visually busy.
If no possession offers a sensible exchange between land and construction capacity, no new lot needs to appear.
If an expensive strategic location attracts no bid, remaining unsold is a meaningful outcome.

AI countries normally avoid selling an indispensable active logistics link, a possession whose loss would ruin their remaining country's defense, or a core merely to finance a decorative building.
They normally avoid buying a disconnected colony they cannot supply, a resource region whose output they cannot access, or a location whose follow-up costs exceed the remaining benefit.
Exceptions need a coherent current strategic reason and an affordable plan, not a general high-Chaos instruction to behave randomly.

Higher evolutions expand the set of possible bargains and the pressures that make them plausible.
They do not remove budget discipline, make every owner desperate, or require every possible lot to be sold.
The most interesting high-Chaos auction may still end with a government deciding that some land is worth keeping.

## Observable decisions

A player following a competitor should see enough public behavior to understand the contest without seeing its exact private ceiling.
The useful evidence is whether that country has bid, the amount it committed, and a brief explanation of its visible strategic interest where the game can support one.
The player should not receive a perfect map of every hidden AI valuation, future budget target, or willingness threshold.

Developer-facing evaluation should distinguish a rejected bid, an unaffordable bid, a valid but low-priority opportunity, and a weighted action that did not execute before closing.
Those are different balancing problems.
The design is successful when diverse live situations produce sensible choices, not when every test fixture produces a particular country's victory.
