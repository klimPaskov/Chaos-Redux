# Greenland Sale: the value of a possession

## Three different numbers

A lot has an assessed value, a seller's opening reserve, and a different willingness ceiling for each prospective buyer.
They answer different questions.
The assessment describes the territory's economic and practical value in the present campaign.
The reserve describes the minimum price at which that seller is prepared to let the auction proceed.
A buyer's ceiling describes how much that particular government considers the acquisition worth, subject to what it can actually fund.

The auction price is the accepted competitive bid, not an average of these numbers.
Two countries may agree that an island has modest industrial value while valuing its naval position very differently.
The system should produce such disagreements without secretly selecting a preferred winner.

## The economic assessment

The assessment begins with the current lot, not its starting history file.
It values existing industrial buildings, usable resource deposits, transport and basing infrastructure, available development capacity, and a modest contribution from the economic potential of the resident population.
It also discloses damage, disruption, and exceptional local obligations.

Industrial buildings are valued in equivalent replacement construction work.
A functioning civilian factory is a durable productive asset, while a damaged factory is worth less than a working one but still has repairable value.
Military factories and dockyards also count even when the seller would prefer to retain them for its war effort.
Their contribution must not disappear merely because Industrial Reserve itself is earned through civilian industry.

Ports, airfields, infrastructure, rail connections, supply facilities, radar, defenses, and other ordinary improvements contribute according to their actual level and function.
A disconnected railway fragment is not valued as a completed supply route.
An unfinished project contributes only through a supported measure of the work or usable result that will really pass to the buyer.
When unfinished work cannot be represented reliably, it is disclosed as unfinished and is not credited as a completed building.

Resources use actual present quantities and the ability to exploit them.
A newly discovered deposit changes the next assessment even if it did not exist at the start of the game.
The general economic assessment should not assume that every unit of every resource has identical value.
The buyer's particular shortages belong in its private strategic assessment, however, and must not be added again as a universal premium paid by everyone.

Population contributes as a limited indicator of economic potential and administrative responsibility.
It is not a sale price assigned to individual people, and it cannot dominate the valuation of a nearly empty but operationally decisive base.
Population loss, famine, or displacement can reduce expected economic usefulness without generating an auction reward for causing those conditions.
Existing shared population records remain authoritative.

An undeveloped state still has some value as territory with access and development possibilities.
That floor must distinguish a usable coastal foothold from a landlocked wasteland with no practical route to the buyer.
Geographic area alone is not a reason to price an enormous ice-covered region above a small industrial island.

## How the assessment is combined

The intended assessment is additive across distinct economic components, with discounts applied to the components actually affected by damage or inaccessibility.
A damaged port discounts that port's contribution.
It should not automatically erase an unrelated inland resource deposit.
A supply failure may then reduce the buyer's expected usefulness of both, but that is a separate, explainable risk adjustment.

The starting balance direction gives the strongest weight to installed productive industry, followed by exploitable resources and usable logistics.
Empty land, spare slots, and population potential are smaller contributions.
Strategic premiums can nevertheless exceed the purely economic value when the campaign gives a location an exceptional role.
The implementation balance pass must calibrate these weights against ordinary construction costs and actual campaign economies.
It must not interpret illustrative totals in this part as measured vanilla prices.

A multi-state lot is valued from its distinct states and distinct shared facilities once each.
The same port cannot appear as a state asset, a regional port asset, and an additional bundle facility at full price three times.
Coherent geography may improve usefulness, but the bundle receives no blanket discount merely because it contains many states.
Its size follows the map rather than a fixed count of discounted state tokens.

## The seller's reserve

The founding Greenland reserve begins at 75 percent of the rounded economic assessment.
That proposed starting ratio leaves room for competitive price discovery while making developed Greenland expensive before bidding begins.
It is not a guarantee that a buyer will recover the entire assessed value through immediate resale.

Later sellers choose between an ordinary asking posture, a firm reserve, and an urgent disposal posture when the lot is prepared.
These are reserve policies within the listing process, rather than extra currencies or persistent national strategies.
An urgent seller accepts a lower minimum in exchange for a better chance of obtaining usable funds.
A firm seller is more willing to accept no sale.

The proposed opening-reserve policies apply to the live economic assessment and are rounded upward to the 5-point quantum.
They are starting balance choices, not fixed territorial prices.

| Lot type | Ordinary reserve | Firm reserve | Urgent reserve |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Ordinary overseas or non-strategic colonial region | 75% of assessment | 100% | 50% |
| Strategic non-core possession | 125% | 150% | 100% |
| Seller-core homeland lot | 150% | 200% | 100%, only under the specified severe pressure |

A strategic core lot uses the stronger applicable reserve floor and still needs both permissions.
A seller that considers even the firm policy insufficient should retain the possession rather than rely on canceling an unfavorable winner.
The founding Greenland lot uses its separate 75-percent rule and has no optional seller repricing.

Core territory and exceptionally important strategic possessions impose higher minimum reservation standards under Parts 08 and 09.
A government cannot mark a homeland lot as an ordinary distant island merely to evade those standards.
An AI seller compares the proposed proceeds with the territory's continuing contribution to its remaining country.
It should not list its only useful overseas base for a trivial price because its generic economic component is small.

The reserve is published before the inspection period.
It does not rise after countries have spent time preparing bids.
Only an unpublished draft can be revised or voluntarily withdrawn.
After publication, the seller cannot change the asking terms, withdraw for preference, or reject a valid winner simply because a richer government remained silent.
A different listing requires the earlier lot to reach a terminal outcome under its actual rules and the applicable cooldown to end.

## What a buyer values

A buyer begins with the lot's current economic assessment and then considers its own position.
The most important additions are a useful new operating position, a missing link in an existing network, access to an important resource shortage, an existing territorial claim, and the ability to keep a dangerous rival out of a position the buyer can itself maintain.

Strategic opportunity is judged by marginal usefulness.
A government with several nearby bases gains less from another redundant harbor than a government whose nearest usable base is far away.
An airfield beyond the buyer's useful operating range is not automatically a forward air hub.
A port behind hostile or closed waters has a different value from one on a usable route.
The AI may still invest in a future position, but the required follow-up construction and defense must fit its plan.

Claims raise interest rather than entitlement.
A current claim can justify paying more for a peaceful acquisition and can make a modest neighboring country a more determined competitor than a remote major.
An existing buyer core may justify stronger interest than a claim, but no additional core is created by the transaction.
The claim or core belongs to the current country and current map, not to a hardcoded historical narrative.

Rival denial has a ceiling.
It is useful when the rival genuinely competes for the lot or would gain a material operational advantage from it.
It is not a global instruction to buy every possession at any price merely because another ideology is present.
The buyer must still expect to use, maintain, or defensibly deny the location itself.

Administrative burden, resistance, vulnerability, isolation, resource inaccessibility, and current military commitments reduce willingness.
These penalties can make an apparently rich colonial lot unattractive to a government that cannot govern or supply it.
A seller's promise that the possession is peaceful does not override a live occupation problem.

## Affordability and willingness

The hard affordability ceiling is the country's free, already funded reserve.
The AI's willingness ceiling can be lower.
It reflects the lot's expected usefulness and the opportunity cost of abandoning other construction or future bids.
The AI bids only when the next quoted amount is no greater than both ceilings.

The player is allowed to value a possession differently from the AI model.
It may overpay for an ambitious strategic plan, roleplay objective, or speculative opportunity.
It cannot exceed its actual funds, bypass the seller's reserve, or acquire territory that the seller cannot legally convey.

The AI must also decide how much reserve to build before bidding.
Its initial target is bounded by its valuation, its sustainable spare construction over the inspection and bidding horizon, and a retained domestic investment margin.
It must not assume that it can fund an expensive colonial empire with one factory before the no-bid window expires.
A seller's large, already earned receipt can support later purchases even when that seller now has a small industrial base.
The budget model should recognize such accumulated value rather than infer wealth solely from factory count.

## Changes while the auction is running

The lot's identity and reserve are fixed at publication, while bidders can reassess its current usefulness before making a new bid.
Additional factories or discovered resources can encourage stronger competition.
A shifted front, new claim, lost supply route, or rival alliance can reduce or increase interest.
None of these observations alone resets the closing clock.

A substantial deterioration in the physical lot protects bidders from a materially different purchase.
As a proposed starting standard, loss of 25 percent or more of the disclosed usable economic assessment, or loss of a specifically advertised operational capability such as a working access port, cancels the lot and refunds escrow.
The comparison concerns the property and capabilities described at publication, not a bidder's changing political preferences.
Ordinary small changes are a disclosed risk accepted by bidders.

Ownership change, hostile occupation, or removal of any promised state invokes the stricter whole-lot cancellation rule regardless of percentage.
A new resource discovery does not cancel a sale just to give the seller a higher starting price.
Deliberate demolition cannot enrich the seller, because a canceled auction provides no proceeds and a new valuation recognizes the damage.

## Worked design examples

Consider an illustrative Greenland assessment of 100 reserve points.
The 75 percent founding reserve is 75.
If one country bids 75 and another bids 80, the first receives 75 back, the second commits 80, and the second country's 10-day closing period begins.
A third country needs 85 to take the lead.
These figures illustrate auction arithmetic, not the expected value of an actual vanilla Greenland state.

Now consider the same mapped lot after a campaign has added substantial productive industry and valuable resources, raising the illustrative assessment to 4,000 points.
The opening reserve becomes 3,000.
The next bid after an accepted 3,000 is 3,150.
A country that could afford the earlier island cannot compete unless it has built a much larger reserve or has meaningful earlier sale proceeds.
The state has not remained cheap simply because it still has the same name or identifier.

Finally, consider two bidders with 1,000 points available each.
A regional power may value a useful foothold at 600, while a remote power values the same lot at 250 after considering defense and access.
At a next bid of 300, the remote AI stops even though it has the money.
The regional AI continues if its other commitments still support the purchase.
Equal wealth therefore does not imply identical bidding behavior.
