# Greenland Sale: Overseas Auctions

## A precedent other governments can use

Evolution I becomes eligible at 200 Chaos and opens the market in distant possessions after Greenland's founding auction has been validly published.
The ordinary first auction can remain active, finish with a buyer, or finish unsold.
The precedent is that governments can advertise a possession and let funded competition determine its purchaser.
It does not require the original Greenland buyer to become an auctioneer or the original seller to survive forever.

The new opportunity is a recurring decision to exchange an awkward outlying holding for useful domestic investment.
Countries with scattered possessions gain a way to consolidate.
Countries without an overseas position gain a peaceful but economically demanding way to obtain one.
The map can develop a patchwork of commercial acquisitions without every auction being a major diplomatic crisis.

## The possessions that qualify

The ordinary Evolution I lot is one whole non-core state owned and fully controlled by its seller.
It must be remote from the country's protected home territory or form an isolated enclave that is costly to maintain.
The founding Greenland lot retains its separate geographic rule and is not split merely because this evolution uses smaller ordinary lots.

Eligible examples include an isolated island, a remote coastal outpost, a non-core overseas state, or a disconnected enclave with a plausible neighboring buyer.
A different continent can be evidence of distance, but it is not the definition by itself.
A nearby, integrated home island should not become disposable merely because the map labels its continent differently.
A distant possession on the same continent can qualify when its separation and defense burden are real.

The current map and ownership determine remoteness.
A possession that becomes connected to the home country through later conquest can cease to fit the ordinary overseas category.
A formerly continuous border district isolated by a territorial change can become a plausible enclave lot.
The event does not maintain a fixed list of historic colonies that remains saleable after independence or integration.

A state's size follows the installed map.
Evolution I does not divide an unusually large state into invented parcels.
However, a possession that falls into the protected strategic category remains outside this tier even when it is only one state.
The distinction between a routine outpost and an important strategic sale is based on what the state presently does, as developed in Part 08.

## Deciding to list

The seller selects a candidate and sees the land it will retain, the estimated reserve range, and the possession's current economic and military contribution.
It chooses an ordinary, firm, or urgent reserve posture before publication.
The seller may withdraw a draft without losing territory or paying a contrived administrative fee.
An AI uses this preparation to reject a sale that would merely replace a useful possession with idle funds.

A remote garrison requirement, an exposed supply route, neglected domestic construction, or a recently changed regional situation can make disposal sensible.
The assessment must also recognize when the holding is paying for itself.
A lightly defended but productive outpost with good access is not automatically a burden because it is overseas.

Publication exposes the lot to all eligible buyers.
The seller does not select a private preferred purchaser after seeing the bids.
It must accept a valid result under its published terms.
This makes the decision to list strategically meaningful, especially where an unfriendly but non-belligerent government may be interested.

## What the buyer can build from a small acquisition

A small purchase should offer a clear next step without awarding a predetermined development package.
A coastal foothold may support a first usable port, a distant island may extend an existing chain of bases, and an enclave may join territory the buyer already owns.
The buyer must still finance construction, secure supply, and meet ordinary ownership rules.

One country may deliberately buy a low-value island and invest in it for years.
Another may buy a claimed enclave because it eliminates a vulnerable border shape.
A third may conclude that the same island is a poor use of industry because it already has a superior nearby base.
These outcomes come from geography and existing systems rather than from mutually exclusive scripted upgrade branches attached to every lot.

The purchase should be visible as a new strategic possibility on the map.
It should not create a new national spirit for every minor island, a special resource counter for each harbor, or an endless recurring development mission.
The native construction and occupation systems remain the main follow-through.

## When no buyer appears

A routine overseas sale is allowed to fail quietly.
The owner keeps the state, no proceeds are created, and its decision to offer the state remains part of the market's history rather than a mandate to sell at any price.
A no-bid lot cannot be reopened immediately with an automatically reduced price.

After a 60-day seller cooldown for an unsold lot, the owner can reassess the possession.
It may choose a lower permitted reserve, wait for a more interested buyer, develop the territory, or decide that keeping it is better.
The minimum opening price remains economically meaningful.
There is no automatic descending-price auction that eventually transfers a developed state for the minimum 5-point bid increment.

## A manageable small market

This tier permits at most two active auctions worldwide, counting the founding lot while it remains active.
An inspection-stage lot occupies a slot because it is already a public commitment that countries may be preparing to fund.
Drafts and private candidate selections do not occupy those slots.
The next lot waits for a real vacancy rather than appearing as an invisible third auction.

A seller may have only one published lot at a time.
After a completed sale, that seller has a 90-day pause before publishing another lot.
A purchased state cannot be listed for resale for 180 days, even if a different market tier becomes available during that period.
These starting pacing rules discourage rapid flipping and notification spam while still allowing a country to reshape its overseas position over a campaign.

New publications are spaced at least 15 days apart worldwide.
An existing bidding contest is not stopped to make room for a newer candidate.
Countries may prepare candidates while the market is full, but preparation is not a promise that the lot will be published or that its current price will remain valid indefinitely.
The exact lot and reserve are rechecked when a slot becomes available.

## The change this evolution should create

The first evolution should make small territorial bargains feel possible and occasional.
It should give players reasons to inspect distant possessions they normally ignore.
Its ideal consequence is a changed operating position or a useful domestic project, not an automatic collapse of every overseas empire.

The AI should retain useful holdings, sell selected burdens, and sometimes compete vigorously for a modest state that fits its regional plans.
A map with few genuinely remote, non-core possessions can have a quiet Evolution I.
The system does not invent colonies to satisfy a content quota.
