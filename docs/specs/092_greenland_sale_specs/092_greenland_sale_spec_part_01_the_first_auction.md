# Greenland Sale: the first auction

## An island at the auction table

Greenland suddenly becomes the subject of an international auction.
The government that actually owns the offered territory is the seller, and the governments that can afford to compete decide how much an Arctic possession is worth to them.
A small regional power can win an uncontested bargain, a wealthy country can exhaust its own construction reserve in a bidding war, and a seller can discover that nobody wants the responsibility at its asking price.
There is no favored historical buyer and no guarantee that a sale will occur.

The event is Minor Fire-Once, has Chaos level 1, and belongs to no cluster.
Its catalog status remains To Be Reworked until implementation and testing establish a different status.
The founding auction is a complete event even when every evolution is disabled.
Its consequences are an actual territorial transfer and an actual exchange of accumulated industrial purchasing power, rather than a temporary territorial lease or a political-power shopping decision.

## What is offered

The first lot contains only eligible territory within the current map's Greenland geography.
It never includes Iceland, another Arctic island, a Danish mainland state, or a substitute island simply because Greenland is unavailable.
Denmark receives no special ownership privilege.
A Greenland owner created by conquest, release, a civil war, a previous territorial event, or an alternate starting map is treated according to its present position.

A single country must be able to convey the whole lot.
When Greenland has one eligible owner, that country's eligible Greenland holdings form the founding lot.
When ownership is divided, the rules in Part 04 select one seller and identify that seller's holdings explicitly.
The announcement must not describe a partial Greenland lot as the sale of the entire island.
The remaining owners retain their territory.

The founding Greenland sale is a geographic exception to the later restrictions on selling cores or strategically valuable possessions.
Otherwise those restrictions could prevent the original concept from happening in precisely the campaigns where Greenland has become valuable.
The exception never allows a capital sale, occupied territory to be conveyed by an occupier, or the seller to disappear.

## The announcement and inspection period

The founding event publishes the seller, the exact lot, its opening reserve, and the economic meaning of the bid unit.
Publication commits the seller to the auction process, subject to the ownership and survival protections.
The old choice to refuse a sale for political power is removed.
A player who controls the seller instead decides how to use a successful sale's proceeds and whether to participate in the later market as it develops.

There is a 15-day inspection period before the first bid can be accepted.
This is a proposed extension of the rough brief, intended to let countries accumulate genuine purchasing power rather than grant them free starting money.
Countries can inspect the lot, allocate available civilian industry to their reserve, and choose whether to follow the auction.
The inspection period happens once per lot and cannot be restarted by changing a government, opening an interface, or revising an AI preference.

The published lot gives a practical picture of what will change hands.
It identifies working and damaged industry, material resources, ports and airfields, existing claims, expected occupation obligations, and any disclosed exceptional state conditions.
A player should be able to recognize the difference between an undeveloped foothold and an expensive, well-equipped Arctic complex without reading the valuation formula.
Foreign armies or aircraft temporarily based there are not included in the sale.

## The first bid

After inspection, the lot remains open for 30 days without a bid.
The first bidder must meet the published reserve and place the entire bid into escrow.
A country cannot bid against income it hopes to earn later.
Its ability to continue funding its reserve remains useful for later increases, but that future production is not collateral.

The first accepted bid starts a new, exact 10-day closing period.
The earlier no-bid deadline no longer matters.
A bid accepted on the final day of the opening window still receives the full closing period.
The public display changes from the opening reserve to the current high bid, the current leader, the next acceptable bid, and the time remaining.

Every economically competent, existing country may enter when it satisfies the same transaction rules.
The seller is ineligible to bid on its own lot.
Engine test carriers and non-government placeholders are not economic governments, even if a test has assigned them artificial assets.
Major-power status, democratic government, historical ownership, player control, and a particular national tag are not entrance requirements.
A landlocked country may pursue a distant port, although its ability to use and defend that acquisition affects whether its AI considers the purchase sensible.
A government at war elsewhere may participate, but a buyer and seller cannot transact while at war with one another.

## Raising the bid

The next acceptable bid is the current bid plus a minimum increase.
The increase is the greater of 5 reserve points or 5 percent of the current bid, rounded upward to a multiple of 5.
The opening reserve is also rounded upward to a multiple of 5.
These are price precision and escalation rules, not a fixed price for Greenland.

One bid action offers exactly the currently displayed next acceptable bid.
The player is not silently committed to a larger offer when the displayed price becomes stale.
A changed quote requires a fresh confirmation.
This keeps a multiplayer click from turning into an unexpectedly expensive purchase.

A new accepted bidder replaces the leader and starts a fresh 10-day closing period.
The previous leader immediately recovers its actual escrowed amount.
That country may bid again later using the returned funds and any reserve accumulated in the meantime.
Only the current leader's bid is committed.

The leader cannot raise its own bid, restart its own deadline, or voluntarily withdraw an accepted offer.
Watching the auction, changing its construction allocation, or dismissing a notification does not alter the deadline.
The leader can stop producing additional reserve without invalidating the money already committed.

## The ten days that matter

The auction closes after 240 in-game hours without a higher accepted bid.
An increase shortly before closing receives the same protection as an increase early in the auction.
There is no shortened final round, secret second-price rule, fixed overall auction duration, or automatic preference for the first country that expressed interest.

A rejected or unaffordable offer does not reset the clock.
A bid arriving at or after the closing instant cannot revive an expired lot.
Where two governments try to make the same next bid at the same instant, the first successfully accepted transaction becomes the leader and the other must obtain the updated quote.
The rules do not promise identical winners under genuinely different multiplayer command orderings.
They require the same accepted command order to produce the same price, leader, funds, and deadline.

An auction can therefore last longer than a month when several countries continue to value the possession highly.
This is an intended bidding contest rather than a reason to award the island arbitrarily.
AI ceilings and rising prices should usually end such a contest naturally.
Human governments that deliberately keep competing are choosing to maintain it.

## A completed sale

When the clock expires, the buyer, seller, lot, and payment are checked together.
A valid closing transfers the specified states and credits the seller with exactly the winning escrowed amount.
The buyer does not pay a second time.
No bidder receives a consolation reward, free claim, stability bonus, or partial territorial interest merely for participating.

The buyer receives ownership and the ordinary responsibilities that follow from it.
The seller loses those holdings and gains a reserve that can be used for useful construction in its remaining territory.
This creates several different endings to the same founding event.
An island can finance mainland reconstruction, become the missing link in a naval network, turn into an expensive occupation problem, or remain undeveloped because the buyer exhausted its investment capacity acquiring it.

Settlement is not accompanied by automatic cores, instant integration, military access through third countries, or the transfer of the seller's armed forces.
Part 04 defines what territorial ownership carries and what it leaves untouched.
Part 11 explains how existing crises and shared systems continue after the sale.

## No sale

If the first-bid window expires without a valid bid, the lot closes unsold.
Greenland remains with its actual owner.
There is no forced discount, consolation buyer, auctioneer subsidy, or second founding attempt selected from another territory.
Countries retain unused purchasing reserves and can use them for ordinary construction.

A published auction also closes without a sale when an external change makes its promised transfer impossible.
Examples include a new owner, hostile occupation, disappearance of a participant, a war between the buyer and seller, or loss of the seller's protected remaining territory.
The current leader receives its actual escrow back when it still exists as a valid government.
The exceptional treatment of an abolished government is described in Part 02.

An unsold but validly published Greenland auction can still establish the political precedent for later auctions.
An event attempt that cannot publish any lawful Greenland lot does not establish that precedent.
The ordinary selector must exclude that inapplicable founding attempt, and a forced invalid attempt must not consume the fire-once publication or create a false successful firing record.
The later market is not a device for concealing that the founding event had no eligible territory.
