# Greenland Sale: Colonial Markets

## Selling a region rather than an isolated state

Evolution II becomes eligible at 400 Chaos and allows a seller to offer a coherent overseas region as one lot.
The change is the scale and continuity of what can be purchased.
The auction still has one seller, one high bidder, one principal, and one 10-day closing rule.
A regional purchase does not become several hidden state auctions with separate winners.

A seller can exchange an expensive colonial responsibility for a substantial reconstruction reserve.
A buyer can acquire the complete economic and logistical setting of a region rather than win one port and discover that its hinterland remains unavailable.
The result can be a peaceful imperial retreat, a regional consolidation, or a large overextension whose costs become apparent after the bidding ends.

## Building a lot from the current geography

A regional lot is an explicitly selected connected set of eligible overseas states owned and fully controlled by the same seller.
Connection means a real land or verified map connection appropriate to the installed geography.
Two possessions do not become one connected region merely because they share an owner or appear in the same broad ocean.
A collection of unrelated islands scattered around the world is not a colonial region for this tier.

The player begins with a candidate anchor state and can extend the proposed lot through adjacent eligible states.
It can also remove boundary states while preserving the lot's required continuity.
A regional bundle contains at least two connected states, which distinguishes the regional permission from a single-state sale.
Beyond that minimum, there is no predetermined number of states or uniform lot size.
A small coherent region can contain few states, while a large uninterrupted possession can contain many.

AI proposals prefer recognizable economic and administrative units.
Useful anchors include a port with its accessible hinterland, a connected resource basin, or an isolated colonial block whose supply and governance are already linked.
These are live relationships rather than a fixed historical list of colonial names and borders.
A prospective region ends where other owners, protected territory, hostile occupation, or the seller's chosen retained holdings interrupt it.

A connected colonial empire may support a very large candidate.
The AI must compare the candidate with smaller coherent alternatives and should not assume that the largest possible lot is the most useful sale.
A whole-region offer can be too expensive for every plausible buyer, while a defensible portion might attract meaningful competition.
The price is allowed to reflect that scale rather than being capped to ensure that a sale happens.

## What stays outside the lot

Seller cores remain excluded from ordinary colonial lots until the homeland-sale rules are enabled.
Protected capitals and the seller's required surviving home territory always remain excluded.
Strategically indispensable possessions still need the strategic-sale permission unless the founding Greenland exception applies.
A seller cannot include an otherwise forbidden state as a tiny part of a large region to bypass its protection.

Territory owned by a subject or another colonial government does not join the lot because it appears inside the proposed region's outline.
Its actual owner must conduct any future sale in its own name.
An enclave controlled by another government remains an enclave after surrounding states are sold.
The auction cannot promise a visually tidy map by annexing that enclave as an incidental effect.

A newly independent country is not a saleable colonial asset of its former ruler.
Release, liberation, and independence change the eligible map immediately.
An old colonial candidate that relied on those states becomes invalid, and a published lot containing them is canceled as a whole.
This interaction is particularly important for Independence Wave.

## Regional value and regional liabilities

A coherent lot carries its entire current economic assessment.
Resources, working industry, ports, transport, and population potential are counted without repeating shared facilities.
The combination may be more useful to a particular buyer than its states would be separately, especially when it creates a viable supply route or joins the buyer's existing territory.
That extra usefulness belongs in the buyer's willingness rather than an unexplained universal bundle bonus.

The same coherence can concentrate liabilities.
A large region may have several occupation problems, damaged logistics, a vulnerable coast, or continuing obligations from an existing crisis.
A buyer must assess the whole burden instead of valuing only the richest state and treating the rest as free.
The lot disclosure should identify the principal strengths and liabilities without turning into a separate dossier for every state.
Detailed state information remains available through inspection.

A large seller receipt is not a reason to grant the seller additional factories on top of the payment.
It already has the reserve to construct what it can legally afford.
The resulting domestic program should follow its actual needs, including the loss of any colonial resources or industry it previously relied on.

## Leaving a colony without selling everything

A seller may retain part of a wider overseas presence.
The listing preview must make the retained shape and any lost supply relationship understandable.
AI sellers avoid leaving a retained possession helplessly isolated unless withdrawal from that possession is independently acceptable and supported by the broader campaign.
They do not rely on an invented perpetual right to use the sold port.

A player can accept an awkward retained position for a strategic reason, but the consequences are disclosed before publication.
It may later negotiate ordinary access, invest in another route, or choose another eligible disposition after the cooldown.
The market does not automatically sell the remainder in a second transaction or force a complete imperial withdrawal.

A failed regional auction may lead to a different proposal after its cooldown.
The seller can reduce the region coherently, revise its reserve posture, or retain it.
The old high bid never carries over to the revised territory, and a previously refunded bidder is not automatically committed to the new lot.
Each new listing is a new promise with a new inspection and closing process.

## Buying a responsibility

The regional buyer becomes the owner of all the sold states, not the ruler of a freshly generated colonial tag.
It receives no automatic country package, special army, core bundle, or scripted loyal administration.
Ordinary release, subject, occupation, and integration options remain available where their existing systems allow them.
The auction does not decide in advance which of those options the player should use.

This is what makes the larger tier strategically different from a bargain island.
A buyer can secure a valuable regional position and still need years of development or political management to make it productive.
A seller can obtain enough reserve for meaningful domestic change and still suffer from the loss of its old raw-material supply.
Both sides are making a consequential exchange rather than receiving an uncomplicated reward.

## Keeping regional sales exceptional

Colonial Markets retains the global limit of two active auctions.
Its larger lots do not justify showing a separate auction for every member state.
The seller and state cooldowns continue to apply, and the entire transferred region's states carry the resale restriction.

The desired rhythm is occasional major colonial bargaining among smaller market activity, not the automatic auction of every overseas state as soon as Chaos reaches 400.
A region remains unsold when nobody can fund a worthwhile bid.
The market's willingness to say no is essential to keeping large territorial transfers meaningful.
