# Greenland Sale: Everything Has a Price

## The homeland becomes negotiable

Evolution IV becomes eligible at 800 Chaos and allows even non-capital parts of a country's own homeland to be offered for sale.
This is the most politically costly extension of the market.
The government is no longer merely shedding a distant responsibility.
It is choosing to exchange territory it regards as part of the country itself for the means to preserve, rebuild, or strengthen what remains.

The country's capital, protected inhabited home, and continued existence remain non-negotiable safeguards.
This evolution cannot sell a country completely out of existence.
It also cannot create a token island, move a capital automatically, or release a replacement country to evade that safeguard.
The title direction expresses an extreme political atmosphere, not permission to ignore the event's core protections.

## Why a government would consider it

AI governments consider core sales under severe, demonstrated pressure.
Relevant pressures include extensive damage to the remaining homeland, a war that has made a compact defensive position more valuable than a vulnerable border district, or a major economic crisis that a plausible receipt could materially alleviate.
A general low-reserve balance is not sufficient.
A government should not sell a core because it has chosen to spend all its reserve on a routine construction project.

The proposed sale needs a credible use for the proceeds and a viable retained country.
The AI compares the loss of productive territory, population, resources, and defensive position with the construction or consolidation it can achieve afterward.
It normally rejects a sale that removes its only meaningful industrial center or exposes the retained capital, even when the formal survival safeguard would leave a valid tag on the map.

The player is permitted to make a core sale without satisfying the AI's desperation score.
It must accept the disclosed political cost and all ownership and survival restrictions.
This leaves room for an unusual strategic bargain, an expensive peaceful border settlement, or a roleplayed retreat without turning a hidden AI preference into a prohibition on player choice.

## The shape of a homeland lot

A homeland lot must be a coherent non-capital border segment or an outlying core possession that can be conveyed as whole states.
The core-sale permission does not authorize arbitrary disconnected pieces of the homeland to be bundled into a single mysterious national liquidation.
The seller chooses an intelligible boundary, and its retained territory is shown before publication.

A single state needs only the core-sale permission.
A connected multi-state core lot also needs the enabled and activated regional-bundling permission from Colonial Markets.
If the lot contains a possession protected for strategic importance, the strategic-sale permission must be present as well.
An enabled top evolution does not silently override a disabled lower evolution's distinct capability.

The combined effect of all of the seller's pending dispositions must preserve its protected remainder.
This matters particularly when homeland and colonial lots are being prepared near the same time.
A government cannot count already promised land as the home that makes another sale safe.
Changing the current capital or losing the protected home to an external event invalidates a conflicting published lot before closing.

## The price of relinquishing a core

Core territory carries a much stronger reservation standard than an ordinary overseas possession.
The proposed normal minimum is 150 percent of its economic assessment, with severe pressure permitting a reduction toward 100 percent rather than an unrestricted discount.
A player can choose a firmer reserve above that minimum.
The high floor does not guarantee a buyer and should not be waived simply to make the evolution produce visible sales.

A neighboring country with an existing claim or core may be willing to pay the premium to avoid a war or repair a fragmented border.
A remote government may regard the same possession as expensive, politically difficult, and strategically awkward.
The seller's cores remain present after sale, and the buyer gains no additional core unless it already has one through an independent system.

The winning price is still the only auction consideration.
The seller cannot accept a lower bid because that buyer promises ideological friendship, or demand a second payment after the clock expires.
Existing diplomacy may influence whether it lists the territory and whether buyers compete, but the accepted auction is not a concealed menu of negotiated treaties.

## The domestic political cost

A completed homeland sale imposes a one-time loss of 10 stability points on the seller.
This is a proposed starting balance cost for voluntarily disposing of seller-core territory.
It applies once per completed lot, not once per state, bid, participant, or announcement.
An unsold or externally canceled lot does not pay the completed-sale penalty.

The founding Greenland sale is exempt from this additional homeland-sale cost because it belongs to the event's original exceptional premise.
The exemption does not extend to later sales of other core territory, and it is not renewed when an earlier Greenland buyer resells the island under the evolved market.
A later sale is evaluated under the rules and core ownership then in force.

The political response should recognize the actual government and circumstance through localisation direction and existing stability systems.
It need not create a new political party, a scripted civil war, or a permanent national spirit for each district sold.
The seller has already lost the land and paid the stability cost.
Further consequences should follow existing crises or an explicit system connection rather than an arbitrary punishment cascade.

## Proving the sacrifice achieved something

A core seller receives a single 90-day domestic-reconstruction mission after settlement.
Before publication, the seller identifies an achievable construction program in its retained core territory with a total quoted cost between 50 and 100 percent of the opening reserve.
The program's qualifying spend target is fixed when the lot is published, so an unexpectedly high winning bid cannot make the mission impossible by enlarging its target afterward.
A core listing with no such legal construction use is not published.

The objective is to complete qualifying construction worth that fixed target in retained, owned and controlled core territory using the sale receipt, while the government continues to exist with its protected capital and home.
The requirement is based on measured spending and completed results, not on a promise to build, a temporary factory modifier, or construction that happened before the sale.
The seller can substitute a different currently legal retained-core project of at least the required qualifying value when circumstances change.
No substitution can count the same construction result or the same receipt twice.

Successful reconstruction restores 5 stability points, recovering part of the original political cost.
It does not refund the land, erase the buyer's ownership, or turn the sale into a net stability reward.
Failure at the deadline provides no additional stability penalty and no automatic civil war.
The government simply fails to make the case for the sacrifice within that period.

If an external change removes every legally achievable way to complete the remaining objective, the mission ends as interrupted without success, a further penalty, or reversal of the completed sale.
Any unspent reserve remains available under the ordinary economic rules.
The deadline is never extended repeatedly by changing the target, and an interrupted mission never awards the success achievement.

Only one domestic-reconstruction mission is active for a country at once.
A further core listing waits until that mission has succeeded, expired, or been resolved after an external invalidation.
The player is not asked to track a separate political timer and receipt quota for every district sold.

## A desperate bargain that can fail

The core market is allowed to be difficult.
A seller may discover that nobody can afford its reserve, that neighboring bidders are preoccupied, or that the political cost makes retention preferable.
A buyer may decide that paying for a core claim is better than war, or that the seller is asking too much for a damaged and exposed district.

The AI does not sell cores automatically at 800 Chaos and does not assume that all countries are equally desperate.
Stable governments usually keep their homeland.
Severely pressured governments become more willing to consider an exchange, but the proceeds must still offer a credible improvement.
The core-sale pressure model is a decision aid and a probability-audit target, not another visible resource the player must manage.

## The final market

The global active-auction limit remains three.
Higher stakes do not justify filling the screen with simultaneous homeland sales.
Ordinary overseas, regional, and strategic activity can continue wherever their own permissions are enabled.
The top evolution expands the limits of what governments will consider selling while retaining the same payment discipline, predictable auction clock, and meaningful option to keep the land.
