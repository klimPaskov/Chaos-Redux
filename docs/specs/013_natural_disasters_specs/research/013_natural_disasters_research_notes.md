# Natural Disasters Research Notes

This note records the external research basis used for the planning package. It is not a final bibliography for implementation, and final super-event quote, cultural remark, image, and audio research still need their own source notes.

## Classification basis

EM-DAT uses a hierarchical classification that separates natural and technological disasters. Its current natural hazard classification uses the groups Geophysical, Hydrological, Meteorological, Climatological, Biological, and Extra-terrestrial. This supports the spec's family grouping for earthquakes, floods, storms, drought, heat, cold, wildfire, volcanic eruptions, tsunamis, and meteor events.

Source: https://doc.emdat.be/docs/data-structure-and-content/disaster-classification-system/

## Geophysical hazard basis

USGS describes the strongest earthquakes, tsunamis, landslides, and volcanic eruptions as occurring in subduction zones, which supports chaining earthquake, tsunami, landslide, and volcano families in shared hazard regions.

Source: https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/natural-hazards

USGS also notes that tsunamis can be generated by rapid seafloor displacement from earthquakes or by shifting masses in submarine landslides. This supports tsunami follow-up logic from earthquake, landslide, volcano, and meteor impacts.

Source: https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/subduction-zone-science/science/tsunamis

## Weather hazard basis

WMO describes extreme weather examples including heatwaves, cold waves, heavy precipitation, drought, tornadoes, and tropical cyclones. It also notes that extreme weather can cause loss of life, infrastructure damage, ecosystem damage, and compounding impacts from sequential events. This supports Evolution II chaining and the recovery-focused design.

Source: https://wmo.int/topics/weather

WMO material on rising risks describes cascading hazards from tropical cyclones, rainfall, flooding, mudslides, heatwaves, drought, poor air quality, wildfires, and subsequent flash flooding. This supports cyclone to flood to landslide chains and heat or drought to wildfire to flood chains.

Source: https://wmo.int/about-us/world-meteorological-day/wmd-2022/rising-risks

NOAA NSSL Severe Weather 101 lists thunderstorms, tornadoes, floods, lightning, hail, damaging winds, and severe winter weather. It also notes that hail can cause major damage and that straight-line winds can cause damage comparable to a tornado. This supports keeping hail and extreme wind as separate meaningful families.

Source: https://www.nssl.noaa.gov/education/svrwx101/

NWS hazard pages list heat, wildfire, cold, drought, dust, floods, hurricanes, and lightning among weather hazards, supporting the family palette and the separate sand or dust family.

Source: https://www.weather.gov/safety/
