# Utopia Manifesto research notes

## Research purpose

The event is a Chaos Redux reinterpretation of Thomas More's Utopia. The design uses the book as a paradox engine rather than a simple socialist buff. The manifesto promises enough land, useful work, public stores, and chosen trades, yet it also contains a dangerous logic of public need that can justify coercive settlement and war. The event should let a minor country try to make a good place, then force it to decide what happens when the good place needs land, labor, soldiers, ports, food, and obedience.

## Primary anchors used in the design

### The island and the no-place pun

More's book was first published in 1516 and describes a fictional island society with shared culture and values. The design uses the island image in two ways. Literal island countries receive naval, port, convoy, and coastal defense content. Landlocked countries can build an inland island through rail corridors, fortified communes, and border wards.

The design also uses the Utopia and Eutopia tension. The country tracks a hopeful Eutopia Index and a failure pressure named Outopia Fracture. Eutopia rises when the state fulfills need through consent, public stores, chosen work, and integration. Outopia rises when the state forces assignments, keeps claims after need disappears, uses coercive occupation, or treats other countries as unused soil.

### Labor by need and trade by inclination

The source society trains everyone in agriculture and lets people learn a trade. A person normally learns the family trade, but talent can move them into another trade, and a person who learns two trades practices the preferred one unless the state needs the other more. The spec turns this into the Vocational Rolls mechanic. The player can favor voluntary job choice, public occasion override, or a balanced compromise. The choice affects production, construction, stability, legitimacy, and Outopia Fracture.

### Public stores and no private property

The source society uses shared storehouses and market halls where families take what they need. The spec translates this into Common Stores rather than a full private-property simulation. Common Stores are built through decisions, focuses, equipment sacrifices, civilian factory burden, rail projects, and local integration projects. Strong stores reduce consumer goods pressure, stabilize occupied wards, and make needful claims less likely. Empty stores make coercive routes tempting.

### Land only when needed

More's Utopians colonize when population pressure exceeds the island's capacity. They first include willing inhabitants, but the book also says they drive out those who refuse and treat resistance as a just cause of war when land is idle and needed for subsistence. The spec makes this contradiction the central expansion mechanic. The country cannot demand land as a normal claim ladder. It must prove Land Need through the ledger. The peaceful path uses leases, plebiscites, charter settlements, and member admission. The coercive path turns need into a war justification and causes long-term fracture.

### The colonial trap

Modern scholarship reads the idle land argument as part of utopian and legal justifications for colonial violence. The spec does not sanitize that problem. It makes the colonial trap playable. A state can become genuinely humane and consent-based, or it can use the language of need to build a righteous empire. The focus tree and decisions reward both paths differently, and the high-chaos route makes the contradiction more dangerous.

## Design translations

| Source theme | Event translation | Player question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No-place and good-place wordplay | Eutopia Index and Outopia Fracture | Is the project becoming a better country or an impossible formula |
| Island society | Literal island branch and inland island fallback | Can the country build security without endless conquest |
| Chosen trades with public need override | Vocational Rolls | When can the state override personal work choice |
| Common stores | Common Stores mechanic and integration support | Can the state fill shelves before it demands more land |
| Colonies based on need | Needful Land Doctrine | Does need justify settlement, lease, wardship, or war |
| Public lectures and useful labor | Education, literacy, and training branches | Can study and skilled work become a military and industrial advantage |
| War fought by volunteers and auxiliaries | Household Guard, volunteers, and controversial auxiliaries | Can the country defend the good place without corrupting it |
| Few laws and distrust of lawyers | Simple law branch and plain-language courts | Can legitimacy improve by making rules readable |
| Religious tolerance with social order | Conscience branch | Can the new order tolerate pluralism when under pressure |

## Public sources consulted

- King's College Cambridge Special Collections, Thomas More's Utopia online exhibition. Useful for publication history and early editions.
- Library of Congress, Thomas Jefferson's Library exhibition on Utopia. Useful for trade choice, agriculture, and labor organization in a reputable public exhibit.
- The Open Utopia, Book II. Useful for island description, common stores, colonies, needful land, and no private property themes.
- Cambridge University Press excerpt from The Cambridge Companion to Utopian Literature. Useful for the Utopia and Eutopia word tension, Renaissance humanism, and utopia as a literary form.
- Houghton and O'Donoghue, Utopias, Colonialism and International Law. Useful for the colonial critique and the poor land use problem.

## Research boundaries

No final super-event title, quote, cultural remark, or audio track is selected in this planning package. The super-event prompt marks those as research-gated tasks for the super-event text and audio researchers.
