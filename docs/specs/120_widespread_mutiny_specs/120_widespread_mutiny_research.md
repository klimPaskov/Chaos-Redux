## S01. Leave grievances and a bounded return to duty

**Source and date:** [Louis Barthas: Eyewitness to the French Army Mutinies, May-June 1917](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/2017/05/01/louis-barthas-eyewitness-to-the-french-army-mutinies-may-june-1917/), Edward M. Strauss, Yale University Press, 1 May 2017. The page includes translated notebooks published in *Poilu* in 2014 and separate historians' summaries.

**Historical facts:** Barthas describes delayed leave, a proposed regimental soviet whose leadership he declined, and his petition seeking leave. A formal promise to restart leave helped restore local order. He also describes coercive escort. The editorial note records later regimental dissolution and dispersal. A separately attributed passage from John Keegan distinguishes reluctance to attack from willingness to defend.

**Perspective and limits:** Barthas supplies a politically engaged participant's local account. The defensive distinction is Keegan's later synthesis. Neither establishes identical motives throughout the French army.

**Authored design inference:** A refusal can target particular orders. Negotiation should address the stated grievance, with promised leave followed by a delivery period. Restored obedience can coexist with subsequent disciplinary and organizational consequences.

## S02. Varied French grievances and contested explanations

**Source and date:** [Les mutineries du Chemin des Dames (1917)](https://www.chemindesdames.fr/sites/default/files/2017-03/Questionnaire-Complementaire-Mutineries.pdf), Service éducatif de la Caverne du Dragon, educational sheet 7, April 2016.

**Historical facts:** This museum synthesis describes protests behind the lines and improvements to leave, food, sleeping arrangements and information. It presents scholarship concerning command credibility, citizenship, war refusal and collective action. It also records disagreement over the importance and extent of coercion.

**Perspective and limits:** The sheet compares historians' interpretations. Its synthesis does not settle every dispute over motives, chronology or repression. No aggregate count is adopted for gameplay balancing.

**Authored design inference:** Supply, rest, confidence in command and political organization remain distinct contextual influences. Recovery can combine material improvements, credible commitments and pressure. The source supports varied outcomes without reducing every mutiny to either a food dispute or a fully developed revolution.

## S03. Local fraternization during continuing war

**Source and date:** [The Christmas Truce](https://ww1.nam.ac.uk/1331/news/christmas-truce/), Hayley Rix, National Army Museum, 22 December 2014. The article draws on contemporary diaries in the museum's collection.

**Historical facts:** Unofficial truces occurred in separate places while fighting continued elsewhere. Some involved no direct contact. Others included greetings, exchanged souvenirs, burial and religious observance. Senior officers opposed friendly contact, yet some pauses continued beyond New Year's Day.

**Perspective and limits:** Individual diary entries establish particular encounters. They cannot establish a universal ceremony, shared political program or permanent peace.

**Authored design inference:** Mutual restraint, direct contact and a continuing agreement are separate steps. A local pause can end quietly or fail to spread.

**Art provenance hint:** The article links collection images NAM.1995-06-89-1-1 and NAM.1995-06-89-1-3. Their captions and item-specific reuse terms require verification before asset production. Shared burial and exchanged objects are supported reference subjects.

## S04. Council authority alongside service discipline

**Source and date:** [Order No. I](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/revolution-in-the-army/revolution-in-the-army-texts/order-no-i/), Michigan State University's *Seventeen Moments in Soviet History*. The primary order is dated 1 March 1917 Old Style, or 14 March New Style. It appeared in *Izvestiia* on 2/15 March. The cited English edition is Frank Golder's *Documents of Russian History*, 1927.

**Historical facts:** Addressing the Petrograd district garrison, the order calls for elected lower-rank committees, political subordination to the Soviet and committee control of weapons. It demands strict discipline during service while asserting civic rights outside duty and prohibiting abusive treatment.

**Perspective and limits:** A prescriptive order establishes intended authority. Its text alone cannot demonstrate uniform compliance throughout Russia.

**Authored design inference:** Councils can contest who authorizes decisions while retaining military obligations. Recognition, treatment and civic rights can matter independently of material concessions. Institutional control needs an explicit mandate and limits.

## S05. A council's uncertain control over its supporters

**Source and date:** [Condition of the Troops at the Front](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/revolution-in-the-army/revolution-in-the-army-texts/condition-of-the-troops-at-the-front/), Michigan State University's *Seventeen Moments in Soviet History*. Army intelligence report dated 13 October 1917, covering 2–13 October. The cited translated edition is Bunyan and Fisher, *Bolshevik Revolution, 1917–1918*, 1934.

**Historical facts:** The report associates shortages, distrust of officers, exhaustion and approaching winter with refusal and fraternization attempts. It describes committees losing soldiers' confidence, peace resolutions treated as authoritative, and German publicity encouraging contact.

**Perspective and limits:** This is an official report using hostile descriptions of soldiers. It records contemporary judgments and reported behavior. Its interpretation of motives requires caution.

**Authored design inference:** Councils can overpromise, lose support or divide over returning to duty. Opposing formations can doubt one another's intentions. Agreement between delegates does not guarantee compliance by all their constituents.

## S06. Rejected representation and escalating unrest

**Source and date:** [Aufstand der Matrosen in Kiel](https://www.bundesarchiv.de/themen-entdecken/online-entdecken/dokumente-zur-zeitgeschichte/aufstand-der-matrosen-in-kiel/), Bundesarchiv. Page undated, historical focus 3 November 1918.

**Historical facts:** A sailors' delegation seeking prisoners' release was turned away. Meetings were prohibited and the union building was closed. Other sailors, workers and unions joined the agitation. Demonstrators sought peace and improved living conditions. Authorization of force preceded violence and wider unrest.

**Perspective and limits:** The curated chronology supports this sequence. It cannot establish that repression invariably produces revolution. A linked letter image was identified but was not transcribed for this research.

**Authored design inference:** A credible negotiating channel matters. Restrictions and arrests can broaden opposition, creating a possible escalation outcome.

**Art provenance hint:** BArch, Bild 183-J0908-0600-002 shows sailors of *Prinzregent Luitpold* with council placards in November 1918. The page gives no photographer's name. Retain the archival identifier and verify the specific image's reuse conditions.

## S07. Local containment after wider diffusion

**Source and date:** [Der Matrosenaufstand 1918](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/kapitel/weimarer-republik/revolution-191819/matrosenaufstand-1918), Arnulf Scriba, Deutsches Historisches Museum, updated 15 August 2015.

**Historical facts:** The museum connects refusal of a final naval sortie with war weariness and perceived futility. Kiel's initial program concentrated on military discipline and freedoms. Noske's amnesty promises contained the local movement after groups of sailors had already carried unrest elsewhere and councils had spread.

**Perspective and limits:** This is a retrospective museum synthesis. Its account of initial demands should not be treated as a complete description of every participant's politics.

**Authored design inference:** Recovery in the originating locality can coexist with separately established unrest elsewhere. Amnesty requires a defined scope. Councils can begin with immediate institutional demands and develop different political positions over time.

## S08. A council's institutional program and declaration

**Source and date:** [Die Kieler 14 Punkte (4. November 1918)](https://germanhistorydocs.org/de/die-weimarer-republik-1918-1933/die-kieler-14-punkte-4-november-1918.pdf), German Historical Institute, *German History in Documents and Images*. Declaration printed in *Schleswig-Holsteinische Volkszeitung*, 5 November 1918, and reprinted in Kurt Ahnert's contemporary 1918 collection.

**Historical facts:** Demands include prisoners' release, proper treatment, immunity upon return to ships or barracks, off-duty freedom and council approval of future measures. The declaration welcomes officers who accept the council and assigns responsibility for order and protection of property.

**Perspective and limits:** This is the council's declared program and assertion of legitimacy. A declaration alone cannot prove complete implementation.

**Authored design inference:** Collective leadership can have responsibilities, rules and accepted officers. Council identity should follow its institutional mandate.

**Research-only quotation:**

> Zum ersten Mal ist die politische Macht in die Hände der Soldaten gelangt.

**Exact attribution:** Kiel Soldiers' Council declaration, printed in *Schleswig-Holsteinische Volkszeitung*, 5 November 1918. This 13-word sentence expresses the council's claim to political power. Translation, context and reuse review remain required before any player-facing adaptation. No translation or rights clearance is supplied here.

## S09. Demobilization and recovery after the armistice

**Source and date:** [Peace and commemoration](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/peace-and-commemoration), National Army Museum. Publication date not displayed.

**Historical facts:** An initial British release scheme privileged essential workers and often left longer-serving soldiers waiting. The museum describes ensuing unrest and temporary soldiers' councils. Revised priorities based on age, service and wounds helped defuse the crisis. Overseas troops still faced transport delays. Overcrowding and illness accompanied unrest at Rhyl, where suppression caused deaths and some later sentences were commuted.

**Perspective and limits:** This overview concerns British and Commonwealth demobilization. Its priorities are historical examples rather than universal administrative rules.

**Authored design inference:** A peace settlement can remove the combat grievance while leaving a discharge commitment to fulfill. Leave, transport home and permanent release have different completion conditions. White Peace therefore supports a separate, bounded recovery phase where circumstances require it. Discharge, punishment, transfer and death remain distinct aftermath records.

## S10. A locally specific non-European grievance combination

**Source and date:** [1915 Singapore Mutiny](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=82db2bb0-b99c-4a9f-9beb-b25626cb3f87), Bonny Tan, National Library Board, Singapore Infopedia. The article states that its information is valid as of 2016.

**Historical facts:** The account describes low morale, divided officers, weak leadership, anti-colonial agitation and rumors that troops scheduled for transfer would be sent against Ottoman Turkey. The uprising fragmented and was suppressed. Subsequent registration requirements caused resentment among a largely loyal Indian community.

**Perspective and limits:** This retrospective institutional account combines several influences. It does not establish religion or ethnicity as a general cause of mutiny.

**Authored design inference:** Untrusted information and disputed deployment can acquire locally specific meanings. Broad punishment can alienate uninvolved people. This case supplies contextual variety without assigning every formation the same grievance or political identity.

## Boundary between evidence and the Chaos evolution

The armed joint council hostile to both governments in Brothers Across the Trenches is a **fictional Chaos 600+ extrapolation**. No exact historical case of that outcome has been established in this dossier. Fraternization, military councils and local revolutionary authority supply separate historical foundations. Their combination into the accepted evolution remains authored scenario design.

History does not establish the event's Chaos threshold, repeat rules, probabilities, cost values, formation accounting, combat controls or implementation feasibility. Those belong to the specification and technical evidence. The sources support differentiated grievances, conditional recovery and institutional aftermath.
