# Event 023 research notes

## Research boundary

Event 23 is alternate history. The research below supplies institutional, technical, command, coercion, humanitarian, and state-succession anchors. It does not claim that the Soviet Union possessed 100 atomic bombs during the normal Hearts of Iron IV start period.

The final event should use these anchors to make fictional early development coherent. It should not paste modern institutions, treaty names, or 1991 political conditions into a 1930s or 1940s campaign without adapting them to the campaign.

## Soviet nuclear program timeline

### International Atomic Energy Agency, Russian Federation country profile

Source:

- Organization: International Atomic Energy Agency.
- Title: Russian Federation, Country Nuclear Power Profile.
- URL: https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/publications/PDF/CNPP2010_CD/countryprofiles/Russia/CNPP2010Russia.htm

Relevant findings:

- Soviet work on the atomic problem resumed in 1942.
- Laboratory No. 2 was created in Moscow in 1943.
- The First Chief Administration was established in 1945 to coordinate atomic science and technology.
- A controlled uranium fission chain reaction was achieved in 1946.
- The first industrial reactor began operation in 1948.
- The first Soviet atomic bomb was tested in 1949.

Design implications:

- Event 23 can plausibly compress several institutional stages into a secret alternate-history breakthrough.
- The opening should imply an organized state program, not one isolated scientist.
- Laboratory, reactor, assembly, storage, and command are separate capabilities.
- Reactor expansion belongs to later escalation because industrial production follows the first successful scientific and administrative stages.

Limitations:

- The profile is a later historical summary.
- It does not describe an alternate early arsenal or game mechanics.

### United States Department of Energy, VNIIEF at Sarov

Source:

- Organization: United States Department of Energy.
- Title: Focus on International Cooperation Continues, Secretary Chu Ends Trip with Visit to Russian Los Alamos.
- URL: https://www.energy.gov/articles/focus-international-cooperation-continues-secretary-chu-ends-trip-visit-russian-los-alamos

Relevant findings:

- The first Soviet bomb, RDS-1, was created there.

Design implications:

- Weapon design and state production should be tied to a dedicated closed institution.
- The opening event can frame the arsenal as the result of a hidden institutional network.
- A real historical site can inspire asset and localisation direction, but exact state placement must be checked against the installed map.

## Command and control

### United States Government Publishing Office, Senate hearing on authority to order use

Source:

- Organization: United States Government Publishing Office.
- Title: Authority to Order the Use of Nuclear Weapons.
- URL: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-115shrg34311/html/CHRG-115shrg34311.htm

Relevant findings:

- Nuclear command faces an always or never dilemma.
- A state wants high assurance that weapons remain usable after attack.
- It also wants high assurance that unauthorized or accidental use never occurs.
- Centralization, delegation, technical controls, communications, and human judgment create tradeoffs.
- No single command design removes every risk.

Design implications:

- Arsenal Readiness represents the always side.
- Command Integrity represents the never side.
- Centralized custody improves control and can weaken survivability.
- Dispersal improves survivability and can weaken control.
- False warnings, delayed confirmation, and human vetoes create real play.
- Low Integrity should block normal use. It should not make launches easy.

Limitations:

- The hearing concerns modern United States command and control.
- The spec uses the underlying dilemma, not modern procedures or hardware.

## Nuclear coercion

### Air University review of Nuclear Weapons and Coercive Diplomacy

Source:

- Organization: Air University.
- Title: Nuclear Weapons and Coercive Diplomacy.
- URL: https://www.airuniversity.af.edu/Aether-ASOR/Book-Reviews/Article/1285489/nuclear-weapons-and-coercive-diplomacy/

Relevant findings:

- The reviewed work distinguishes deterrence, which preserves a status quo, from compellence, which demands change.
- Nuclear compellent threats often face credibility problems because the stakes may not justify use.
- International costs can outweigh the limited gain sought.
- Nuclear states did not show a general coercive advantage in the reviewed historical analysis.
- Even explicit nuclear signals can be misunderstood.

Design implications:

- Target compliance must depend on the demand and campaign state.
- A demonstrated stockpile does not guarantee surrender.
- Limited demands are more credible than national extinction.
- Public threats increase foreign intervention and political cost.
- Empty threats damage credibility.
- Demonstrations can fail to communicate the intended signal.

Limitations:

- The source is a book review that summarizes and critiques a scholarly argument.
- It supports a skeptical coercion model, not one exact probability.

## Humanitarian consequences

### International Committee of the Red Cross

Source:

- Organization: International Committee of the Red Cross.
- Title: Humanitarian impacts and risks of use of nuclear weapons.
- URL: https://www.icrc.org/en/document/humanitarian-impacts-and-risks-use-nuclear-weapons

Relevant findings:

- Blast, intense heat, radiation, and radioactive fallout cause immediate and long-term harm.
- A detonation near a populated area can cause mass death, destruction, displacement, and long-term damage to health, environment, infrastructure, economy, and social order.
- Fallout can cross national borders.
- Existing emergency and health systems cannot adequately address a major populated-area detonation.
- Use and testing leave long-term health and environmental legacies.

Design implications:

- Event 23 must use shared deaths, fallout, contamination, and condemnation.
- Civilian targeting needs the strongest restriction and severe writing direction.
- Tests can also create long-term harm.
- The event should not treat use as a clean one-time combat modifier.
- Cross-border contamination supports global Air Cleanliness and foreign reaction.

## Post-Soviet state succession and custody

### Belfer Center, Non-Proliferation and State Succession

Source:

- Organization: Belfer Center for Science and International Affairs.
- Title: Non-Proliferation and State Succession: The Demise of the USSR and the Nuclear Aftermath in Belarus, Kazakhstan, and Ukraine.
- URL: https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/non-proliferation-and-state-succession-demise-ussr-and-nuclear-aftermath-belarus

Relevant findings:

- Soviet strategic-missile command and control was centralized in Moscow.
- Weapons were located in Belarus, Kazakhstan, Russia, and Ukraine after dissolution.
- Belarus, Kazakhstan, and Ukraine later chose nonnuclear status and relinquished the weapons.
- Domestic and international political factors shaped the outcomes.

Design implications:

- State possession and operational command must be separate.
- Event 5 can distribute physical custody without creating instant launch capability.
- Domestic politics, recognition, foreign pressure, and institutional access matter.
- A negotiated nonnuclear outcome can be a full gameplay route.

### Nuclear Threat Initiative, Ukraine profile

Source:

- Organization: Nuclear Threat Initiative.
- Title: Ukraine.
- URL: https://www.nti.org/countries/ukraine/

Relevant findings:

- More than 1,700 Soviet nuclear weapons were left in Ukraine at independence according to the profile.
- Ukraine never possessed operational control of the weapons.
- The weapons were removed to Russia under an agreement linked to security assurances.

Design implications:

- A breakaway can control territory and negotiate from physical custody without being able to launch.
- Security assurances and removal aid are useful settlement rewards.
- The event should avoid calling every holder a nuclear power.

Source note:

- The page states that its content is from a past project and was no longer updated after 6 April 2026. The historical custody point is still useful, but current policy statements from the page should not be treated as live information.

### United States Office of the Historian

Source:

- Organization: United States Department of State, Office of the Historian.
- Title: The Clinton-Yeltsin Relationship and U.S.-Russian Relations.
- URL: https://history.state.gov/milestones/1993-2000/clinton-yeltsin

Relevant findings:

- Under the Lisbon Protocol, Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, and Kazakhstan agreed to abide by nuclear agreements and to destroy or transfer remaining weapons to Russia.
- Security assurances and economic assistance helped address political concerns.

Design implications:

- Return, dismantlement, security assurances, and economic aid can be linked.
- A transfer is a negotiated political process, not a simple stockpile move.

### Arms Control Association, Lisbon Protocol

Source:

- Organization: Arms Control Association.
- Title: The Lisbon Protocol At a Glance.
- URL: https://www.armscontrol.org/factsheets/lisbon-protocol-glance

Relevant findings:

- Belarus, Kazakhstan, and Ukraine inherited large numbers of strategic and tactical weapons on their territories.
- Tactical weapons were reportedly returned to Russia earlier than strategic systems.
- The Lisbon process linked transfer, treaty participation, dismantlement, material compensation, economic assistance, and security assurances.
- Ukraine claimed administrative control but did not gain operational control.

Design implications:

- Different weapon classes and custody states can move at different rates.
- Joint custody, administrative control, and operational control should remain separate concepts.
- Removal can require transport, observation, dismantlement, and compensation.
- A settlement can give material or economic value without granting operational nuclear capability.

## Research synthesis for the specification

The research supports six central design choices:

1. The breakthrough is institutional. The event needs laboratories, reactors, production, storage, and command.
2. Testing is a technical and political threshold. It changes readiness and foreign knowledge.
3. Command involves a permanent survivability and control tradeoff. Two visible values are justified.
4. Nuclear coercion is uncertain. Demands need credibility, target response, and foreign reaction.
5. Nuclear use creates persistent humanitarian and environmental consequences. Shared systems must own those effects.
6. Soviet Collapse separates location from operational control. Custody, technicians, command, and delivery form distinct stages.
