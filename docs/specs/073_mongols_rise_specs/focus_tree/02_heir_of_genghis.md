# Political route: Heir of Genghis

## Route purpose

This route builds a personal empire around the Great Khan's office. Military success establishes legitimacy, the Yassa defines service, and Karakorum becomes the visible center of command. Its attraction is a more forceful mounted command system and the ability to turn personal loyalty into rapid campaigns. Its cost is dependence on a ruler whose succession must be prepared while the empire is still winning.

The route does not require literal resurrection or an invented bloodline. A real, vetted candidate can claim the title. Where descent is part of that claim, the narrative distinguishes what is documented from what the regime asserts. The institutional office remains playable even when a named candidate is unavailable.

## Root: the right to command

The opening common spine leads to a claim that military success and recognized service justify the Great Khan's authority. The player first gathers support from existing commanders and regional representatives. This has a practical early result: clearer command obligations and access to a first personal command appointment.

The player can favor the most capable commander or the most politically acceptable supporter. Competence strengthens the next campaign. Political acceptability reduces an appointment dispute. These outcomes matter later when the same military network participates in succession.

## Fork: law and service

The Yassa branch decides how the center binds its servants. A strict service settlement requires regular mounted contingents and discipline. A negotiated law settlement allows more local custom in return for predictable obligations. Neither is a claim that a complete medieval legal code survives unchanged for direct implementation in a twentieth-century state.

Strict obligations provide more immediate support while raising the cost of failed promises and arbitrary favoritism. Negotiated obligations produce a slower but more dependable response from diverse regions. Both preserve the Great Khan's authority over the army.

## Commitment: the personal imperial order

Completing the binding imperial settlement locks this route. The political spirit becomes the personal Great Khan's order. Military appointments, succession preparation, and khanate obligations now reflect that choice.

The player chooses a public succession principle. A designated heir gives clarity but can anger powerful commanders. A service-based nomination rewards achievement but leaves more competition. The choice determines which disputes appear at the next vacancy. It does not instantly remove every possible challenger.

## Military and capital payoff

The route develops elite tumens, a limited personal cavalry formation, and stronger offensive command at high Momentum. The personal formation is a prestigious reserve with a real manpower and equipment cost. It is not an unlimited elite-division generator.

Karakorum gives the personal empire a political home. Its early command center works before the capital is moved. Restoring the center while honoring regional obligations strengthens Authority. An expensive capital ceremony during a supply disaster can instead provoke criticism from commanders whose troops need equipment.

## Regional government

Khans receive offices in return for stated obligations. Their disputes concern real promises: a promised region, command of a joint campaign, or the division of a settlement. The player may settle the claim, appoint a rival, or insist on central authority. A successful coercive settlement can work while Momentum is high and become a source of rebellion after a defeat.

Direct rule remains available. Its stronger personal control comes with more administrative burden and a larger exposed rear area. The route cannot solve every occupation problem by declaring universal loyalty to the ruler.

## Capstone: a recognized Great Khan

The capstone requires the restored center, a successful regional campaign, and an implemented succession arrangement. It strengthens the personal command settlement and permits wider imperial demands. It does not automatically proclaim Universal Khan, which remains a separate late achievement.

The capstone also introduces responsibility. A public succession promise is now harder to abandon without consequence. Major khans can refer to it during a vacancy or a dispute.

## Continuing play and failure

After the capstone, the player can appoint and review commanders, settle service disputes, prepare a succession, and pursue any reachable regional campaign. Strong victories can keep a personal empire coherent over a wide area.

The route's main failure is a Great Khan whose command reputation collapses before a succession is settled. A rival may demand a kurultai, one khan may withhold troops, and a defeated region may seek independence. The player can recognize a successor, negotiate autonomy, or fight for the center. An orderly succession preserves earned institutions and surviving forces.

## AI and experience tests

AI should choose this route when military opportunity and command legitimacy are strong. It prepares succession before the empire becomes too large to manage personally. It does not delay the first campaign to finish every ceremonial anchor.

A human should feel the advantage of personal military authority during expansion and understand why the same structure becomes fragile during a contested succession.
