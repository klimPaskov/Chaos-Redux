# Mongols Rise

## The restored empire

Mongolia proclaims a new Mongol Empire and assembles a large mounted army almost immediately. The player begins with the means to fight, a claim to the Great Khan's authority, and several nearby directions of expansion. Industry and ceremony follow the first campaign instead of delaying it. The opening is a regional military emergency for Mongolia's neighbors, not an automatic transfer of their territory.

The normal imperial field army is cavalry. Its strength comes from concentrated attacks, rapid movement, reconnaissance, recovery between battles, and the ability to turn a breakthrough into a new campaign. Artillery, anti-air, anti-tank weapons, trains, trucks, radio networks, and aircraft support that mounted army. They do not turn its line formations into infantry or armored divisions.

The empire has a lasting weakness in static warfare. Low entrenchment, reduced defense, difficult city fighting, forts, and cut supply routes remain dangerous at every Evolution. Modern administration can make a retreat survivable. It cannot remove the military identity that makes stopping the horde a viable strategy.

## First experience

The restoration appears with the opening army, a short explanation of cavalry warfare, and a clear warning about defensive weakness. The player sees the first nearby campaign choices and the political question of who can claim the Great Khan's office. They do not receive a continent of claims or a list of dozens of locked decisions.

The first two short focus steps establish the restoration and the authority to act. A legal expansion demand becomes available within the first two weeks under ordinary independent-country conditions. A subject Mongolia first receives a short sovereignty confrontation with its overlord. The army arrives regardless of whether that confrontation ends in negotiation or resistance, provided Mongolia has a valid place to receive it.

The opening force is a one-time extraordinary muster. Its soldiers are not represented as a repeated drain from Mongolia's small civilian population. Subsequent reinforcements use actual reserves, recruitment, conquest settlements, and tribute. Losses eventually matter.

## The campaign loop

The player selects a reachable objective, prepares a limited campaign, and offers submission or begins a legal war. Holding the objective opens a settlement. A settlement can return equipment, recruit willing auxiliaries into cavalry formations, establish a tributary, create a regional khanate, or retain territory under direct administration. The player then decides whether to exploit the victory or pause to connect supply routes and settle political obligations.

Victories raise Momentum and can support Authority. Unkept promises and bad settlements can damage Authority even during a successful war. A long stalemate erodes Momentum. Losing a key campaign region, the capital, or the army can make the khans question the center. The player therefore balances immediate opportunity against the ability to keep a growing empire together.

Territorial rewards come from the settlement that actually occurred. Capturing a place, losing it, and capturing it again does not repeatedly produce new armies. Defeating the same tiny country cannot support unlimited expansion.

## The Great Khan's settlement

Three political routes offer different ways to govern the same cavalry empire.

**Heir of Genghis** grounds authority in the Great Khan's title, the restored Yassa, military merit, tumen obligations, and Karakorum. It offers the strongest personal military command and a difficult succession problem. Claims of descent are political claims unless a real candidate's genealogy is separately verified.

**The New Great Khan** builds a twentieth-century imperial command system. Dispatch offices, artillery supply, air liaison, trained administrators, and dependable succession arrangements make long campaigns easier to sustain. Cavalry remains the army's core.

**Council of Khans** creates a negotiated confederation. Regional khans keep substantial authority and commit forces, tribute, and routes to a common empire. It handles distance better and demands more negotiation. A council majority cannot make a human-controlled khanate silently surrender its choices.

These routes change obligations, military organization, subject relations, succession, and late play. Their final focuses leave useful decisions and further ambitions available.

## Expansion without one fixed order

Nearby Mongol territories, northern China, Central Asia, and Siberian approaches form the first set of strategic opportunities. China offers industrial depth but harder fighting and a major occupation burden. Central Asia offers western routes and room for khanates. Siberia offers distance, exposed supply, and a difficult choice between limited frontier gains and a larger confrontation.

Later campaigns can establish the Golden Horde, reach Persia and the Middle East through an Ilkhanate settlement, enter Europe, or move toward India, Korea, Southeast Asia, and the rest of China. A reachable route can be pursued before another historical direction is finished. Political branch choice does not force the player to follow a fixed medieval itinerary.

The same territory cannot simultaneously count as a direct-rule province, an independent tribute source, and the property of a newly created khanate. Settlement choices explain the actual owner, controller, obligations, and military relationship before the player accepts them.

## Karakorum and the Silk Roads

Karakorum becomes an imperial project with practical stages. A command center, roads and rail connections, workshops, and civil institutions provide useful gains before the final capital ceremony. A permanent move is a late political choice tied to an actual valid map location. The project does not move the capital to an unrelated state merely because its identifier is convenient.

The Silk Roads reward connected territory, functioning transport links, and reliable agreements. A sequence of protected route sections can support construction, resources, trade, and supply. A broken section suspends the benefits that depend on it. An isolated western khanate does not create an invisible railway across hostile land.

## Evolutions

At Chaos 200+, **The Yassa Restored** provides a larger opening entitlement, stronger mounted offensive capability, and deeper imperial law, appointments, discipline, and succession content.

At Chaos 400+, **The Khanates Return** provides a major regional invasion force and more capable regional khanates. Their armies and resources increase the empire's reach. Their political weight also grows.

At Chaos 600+, **Universal Khan** provides the largest horde, wider submission demands, and faster continental campaigns. Universal claims must still become reachable campaigns and actual settlements. Cities, armor, air attack, forts, severed supply, and prolonged defense can still stop the army.

An Evolution that activates after the event adds only the newly available entitlement. It does not replay the opening or replace casualties for free.

## Succession and collapse

A vacancy in the Great Khan's office opens a kurultai. A designated heir, leading commander, negotiated council candidate, or provincial ruler can compete through the political arrangements built during play. A strong settlement allows a peaceful succession. A weak settlement can produce rival claimants, withdrawal of tribute, regional independence, or civil war.

Collapse develops through visible warning, a chance to negotiate or recover, and a settlement of the surviving empire. It does not happen simply because the player spent one month at peace. A military catastrophe or loss of Mongolia can make the crisis much faster.

Successors keep meaningful territory, forces, leaders or institutions, diplomacy, and future goals. Their borders follow the state of the campaign. The world does not revert to the map that existed before Event 073.

## The opponent's game

Neighbors can submit, negotiate a limited obligation, support a threatened neighbor through existing diplomatic systems, or resist. The strongest resistance uses protected cities, fortified approaches, armor, air power, and attacks against exposed supply. Tribute is a real cost, not an automatic survival guarantee. Refusing tribute creates a clear threat with a response period and a legal war route.

Stopping the empire has a visible effect. Its attack slows, khans become less dependable, and a counteroffensive can break the central coalition. A defeated empire can still survive as a smaller state if it reaches a credible settlement.

## Enduring design tests

The first war must be accessible quickly. Winning must supply the next campaign without producing infinite rewards. Political routes must change how the empire is played. Every region must offer a settlement worth choosing. Cavalry must remain the normal field army. Defensive weakness must survive the final Evolution. Succession must reward prior decisions. Collapse must create playable successors.
