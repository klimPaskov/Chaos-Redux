# World Claims: from regional success to a contested world

## Several legitimate stopping points

World Claims supports a contained border adjustment, a regional unification, a stable greater state, a failed expansion, and a global contest. None requires a separate scripted country path. The map and the country's choices determine which story emerges.

A country can accept automatic claims and do nothing. A country can conquer part of the first ring and consolidate it. A regional government can complete one ambition and retain its old identity. These are valid outcomes rather than failed attempts to reach the last Evolution.

A defeated claimant can lose the territory needed for its current project without losing every native institution. Its unfinished ambition becomes visibly incomplete, its control clocks stop where control is lost, and its history remains coherent. The event does not manufacture a civil war merely to punish a failed checklist.

## Continental projects

Greater Nation can expose a continental project when the country has a substantial, connected base and relevant regional achievements. The candidate must have an actual center on that continent. A tiny remote possession cannot nominate an entire continent by itself.

The project is divided into geographically meaningful pages and milestones. A player should be able to identify the integrated center, the unresolved approaches, and the next reachable frontier. The continental envelope is fixed when adopted, while ownership and qualification remain live.

A continental proclamation does not auto-annex its remaining states. Remaining reachable claims and later waves supply further ambitions. Native warfare and settlement remain necessary to obtain ownership.

## Transcontinental and hemisphere projects

At The World Is Ours, an established power can connect substantial centers on multiple continents. The ambition must explain the geographic relationship: held approaches around a strategic sea, connected land centers, or a supported maritime network joining actual possessions.

A hemisphere project uses the registered map's explicit hemisphere membership, not a modern political list or the position of a country's capital. States intersecting a dividing line need a declared registry rule. That rule cannot shift to exclude an inconvenient opponent after the project begins.

The country can pursue these projects under an imperial, federal, republican, or ideological identity supported by its actual presentation. Their scale changes the territorial challenge. It does not require every country to use the same final constitutional story.

## The global project

A global ambition appears only after the country has completed a continental or transcontinental milestone and holds a substantial base across the world's continental groups. It can then identify the whole valid terrestrial campaign map as its long-term envelope.

An envelope is an objective, not a claim grant. The global ambition does not mark every state as World Claims territory. Claims still progress from owned land and the connected claim network under the firing limits. Disconnected overseas targets remain future objectives until the country acquires an appropriate foothold through ordinary means.

A global proclamation recognizes dominance after the defined possession, integration, and anchor thresholds. It does not announce that every last state is already owned. A separate completed-world milestone requires direct ownership and control of every valid owned terrestrial state in the registered campaign map. Existing foreign survivors cannot be excluded because they are inconvenient.

The system never interprets completion as a command to kill populations, remove countries by script, or end the campaign automatically. It recognizes a territorial result that the campaign has actually produced.

## Rival global projects

Several countries can reach continental or global ambition at once. Their envelopes may overlap completely while their claims and integration histories remain different. The global project is not a unique reserved reward for the first country that opens the category.

The most interesting late-game pressure comes from competing viable centers. One country may integrate rapidly while another controls the better-connected frontier. A newly independent survivor can build around a fresh border. A large country can have claims on a continent that another government has already integrated into cores.

The interface prioritizes actual rival public projects and held disputed anchors. It does not show every claimant as equally urgent or produce a global popup for every ordinary core award.

## Event-owned Chaos contributions

A World Claims firing can increase world pressure when it creates a meaningful new set of competing territorial claims. The proposed rule awards 5 Chaos when at least five participating governments receive at least five previously unissued claimant-state targets in total. At profile IV or V, a wave reaching at least 100 distinct newly targeted foreign states awards 10 instead of 5.

These two amounts are alternatives, not additive rewards. A rolling 360-day cap of 20 limits the claim-wave contribution. Reissuing an already recorded claimant-state relationship, loading a save, acknowledging the event, or refreshing a display cannot count as new territorial pressure.

A publicly adopted continental-or-larger project can create a separate 10-Chaos crisis once per claimant epoch and geographic scale when it already has at least 100 event-owned claimed targets still foreign-owned across at least five other governments and it has begun active pursuit. Merely selecting a name or opening a preview is insufficient. The event-wide rolling cap for this public-project contribution is also 20 per 360 days.

Ordinary conquest, declarations, and annexation already belong to the shared campaign's relevant systems. World Claims does not duplicate their Chaos charges. Receiving Compliance, earning an ordinary core, activating an Evolution, or changing a flag adds no independent Chaos.

## Containment and reconciliation

A completed 180-day assurance may reverse up to 5 Chaos from an identifiable, unreversed World Claims public-project contribution that the agreement actually contains. It cannot reverse more than that contribution originally added, and both signatories refer to the same receipt.

A failed expansion does not automatically refund a whole war's pressure. Only an event-owned contribution tied to an abandoned or contained public project can be settled. Territory lost in battle, native war outcomes, and political penalties retain their own owners.

The contribution ledger has separate issuance and reversal receipts. Repeated offers, broken assurances, changed country names, duplicate callbacks, or renewed projects over the same territorial identity cannot produce an endless positive-negative loop. Historical issuance caps are not reset by a later reversal.

## The aftermath of a greater state

The formed country's integrated heartland remains strategically useful. Its confidence period expires naturally. Its successor ambitions remain available when actual holdings justify them. A temporary loss can force reconsolidation without replaying the original proclamation.

Other systems can continue to transform the world. Territorial transfers, independence waves, new formables, and map-changing campaign events provide new political frontiers. World Claims reassesses the resulting map rather than trying to restore the borders that existed when the first wave fired.

The final tone is one of escalating certainty. At low Chaos, governments discover plausible neighboring claims. At high Chaos, each successful state starts treating geography as a sequence of temporary obstacles to its own national future.
