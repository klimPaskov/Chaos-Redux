# Event 056: The Navy

## Catalog entry

- Event ID: `56`
- Event name: The Navy
- Type: Minor Repeatable
- Status during planning: To Be Reworked
- Chaos level: 1
- Primary cluster: Sudden Abundance
- Primary member severity: Medium
- Additional cluster: Military Preparation
- Additional member severity: Medium

## Event promise

Every country that can operate a navy suddenly receives a coherent but unpredictable naval package. The gift is global, immediate, and indifferent to national plans. A continental power may discover carriers and aircraft in ports it barely uses. A maritime empire may receive a dense submarine force that conflicts with its existing doctrine. A small coastal state may become a regional naval threat overnight, while another receives a convoy reserve and enough escorts to keep trade moving but little offensive power.

The package must feel like a fleet with a recognizable identity. It cannot be a loose handful of random ships. The player should understand what kind of navy appeared, what it can do, why it may be awkward to use, and what operational burden arrived with it.

The event is beneficial in material terms, but the result is not guaranteed to be convenient. Its play comes from adaptation. Countries decide whether to commission the whole package, introduce it gradually, or dismantle part of it into support that better fits their situation.

## Core design principles

### The fleet identity is random

The package is never selected to match the recipient's current doctrine, production plan, geography, enemy, ideology, faction role, or existing fleet. A country can receive exactly the kind of fleet it would not have chosen to build.

Eligibility checks may prevent invalid content. Safety scaling may reduce package size. Neither system may steer the random roll toward an optimal strategic answer.

### The package is coherent

Every package has one primary identity and a defining asset family. A submarine package is mostly submarines. A destroyer swarm is genuinely dominated by destroyers. A carrier package contains carriers, escorts, and air support rather than one carrier placed beside unrelated ships.

A package can include a small secondary element where the fleet needs it to function. This support must strengthen the primary identity instead of blurring it.

### Every coastal country matters

Major powers, minor powers, subjects, civil-war participants, island states, and newly coastal countries can all qualify. Country strength does not determine whether the state receives a fleet. Strength affects only safe operational scaling where a full package would exceed global or local limits.

### Repeat firings remain meaningful

Later firings can give the same country a different fleet identity. The package scale declines after repeated receipts so the event does not create unlimited ship growth. The identity remains strong even when the later package is smaller.

### The normal naval game remains central

The event hands the recipient a fleet and a commissioning choice. After that, the ordinary navy interface, task-force system, supply, fuel, repair, air support, missions, admirals, and combat rules carry the experience.

The event does not need a permanent custom meter or a separate naval management window. Its depth comes from the fleet package and the consequences of using it.

## Baseline event flow

### 1. The world manifestation

The event begins with one global report describing the same impossible pattern across ports around the world. Harbor authorities find complete formations moored inside controlled naval facilities. Crews, stores, manifests, and maintenance records exist, but no government can establish where the fleets were built or how they arrived.

The event never confirms a source. Separate firings can suggest different rumors, but those rumors remain interpretations rather than answers.

### 2. Eligibility is resolved

Every existing country is checked once during the firing. A recipient qualifies when it has direct, usable access to a controlled coast and a legal commissioning location. Landlocked countries are skipped for this firing and remain eligible for later repeats if their geography changes.

The event records why a country was skipped. Invalid countries do not receive ships in arbitrary or hostile ports.

### 3. One package identity is rolled per recipient

Every eligible country rolls independently from the package pool available at the current evolution stage. The roll determines the fleet identity before the country chooses how to commission it.

Countries do not share one global package result. The world should receive a broad mixture of fleets from the same firing.

### 4. Fleet scale is assigned

A local package band establishes the recipient's rough fleet size. A global delivery budget then prevents the combined firing from creating an excessive number of naval entities.

The scale system must preserve the defining asset of each package. When normalization is needed, it cuts secondary escorts and auxiliary ships before removing the feature that gives the fleet its identity.

### 5. Ships and support are assigned to safe ports

The package uses one to three commissioning ports. Small packages should normally appear in one port. Large packages can be divided to avoid an unusable stack and to reflect repair and supply limits.

Where a country controls a coast but lacks a naval base, a limited emergency commissioning facility may be created if a safe state exists. The fleet then arrives through a short delayed process rather than appearing in an invalid location.

### 6. Human recipients make a commissioning choice

Each eligible human-controlled country receives a personal report showing the package identity, rough composition, commissioning ports, support package, and main operational burden.

The human player chooses full commissioning, phased commissioning, or partial breakup when that third choice is valid for the package.

AI countries resolve the same decision through strategic evaluation without receiving a visible popup.

### 7. Normal naval play resumes

The ships become ordinary operational forces after commissioning. They can be reorganized, assigned to missions, repaired, lost, refitted where supported, or combined with existing navies.

The event retains only the limited cohort memory needed for repeat scaling, achievements, event history, and exploit prevention.

## Player experience

The baseline experience should create four immediate questions:

1. What kind of fleet appeared?
2. Where did it appear?
3. Can the country fuel, repair, crew, and protect it?
4. Is it better to use the whole gift or convert part of it into support?

The event should give the player enough information to act without exposing internal allocation formulas. The personal report names the fleet identity and defining ships, gives a compact scale description, identifies commissioning ports, and states the main burden in plain terms.

The player should not need to manage a custom score after the report closes. The consequences remain visible through fuel use, naval range, repair queues, aircraft availability, convoy reserves, dockyard demands, and combat results.

## Baseline fleet identities

The baseline pool contains these main identities:

- balanced surface fleet
- submarine raiding fleet
- destroyer swarm
- convoy-heavy escort fleet
- cruiser-focused fleet
- capital-ship fleet
- carrier-focused fleet
- coastal defense fleet
- invasion-support fleet
- minelaying and sea-denial fleet where the active content supports it

Each identity is defined in the fleet catalog part of this specification.

## Beneficial effect and real burden

The event gives real military value. It should not hide a severe penalty that makes most packages traps. The burden comes from operating a force that the recipient did not plan to sustain.

Relevant pressures include:

- fuel consumption
- naval repair queues
- limited naval base capacity
- missing carrier aircraft
- insufficient screening doctrine or training
- exposure to enemy air power
- poor access to useful sea regions
- competition with existing convoy and dockyard priorities

The event provides enough support to make the fleet usable. It does not erase every logistical cost.

## World consequences

The immediate military balance can change sharply. Small coastal countries can gain deterrence. Existing naval powers can become harder to challenge. Convoy security can improve in some regions while submarine warfare becomes stronger in others. Naval invasions can become possible for countries that previously lacked the ships to attempt them.

These consequences arise through ordinary gameplay. Event 56 does not directly declare wars, create blockades, trigger famine, or force naval missions.

## Repeatable identity

The event remains repeatable because coastlines, countries, naval technology, alliances, and wars change across a campaign. A country skipped during one firing may qualify later. A recipient that received escorts first may receive carriers on a later firing. Evolved pools can introduce assets that did not exist during the first manifestation.

Repeat scaling prevents the event from becoming an unlimited fleet printer. The first package is the full shock. Later packages are reinforcements, reserve formations, or strange additions whose identity stays recognizable at a reduced scale.

## Event memory

The event should remember only what later play needs:

- how many times each country has received a package
- the identity of each received package
- the cohort of ships and support associated with each firing
- the commissioning choice used
- whether an emergency port was required
- whether an experimental or impossible asset was actually delivered
- achievement progress tied to granted ships

The event should not keep an expanding per-ship narrative ledger after ships are lost or reorganized. Cohort tracking must remain bounded.

## Success standard

The event succeeds when a player can receive an awkward fleet, understand it quickly, make a practical commissioning choice, and use the result in the normal naval game. The world should become more capable at sea without receiving so many ships that late-game performance or naval readability collapses.
