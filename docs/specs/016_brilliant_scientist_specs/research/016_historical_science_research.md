# Event 016 historical science research

## Purpose

The event is fictional. Historical research is used to make its institutions, rivalries, secrecy, and moral conflicts feel grounded in the 1936 to 1945 world. None of these precedents should turn Doctor Warren Kruger into a disguised real person.

## Design themes drawn from research

### Rapid scientific mobilization

The MIT Radiation Laboratory grew from a secret wartime transfer of microwave radar knowledge into a large civilian and academic research effort. It drew specialists from many disciplines, connected universities with the military and industry, and produced a wide family of operational systems. This is the main precedent for Kruger's early ability to reorganize a host country's scattered talent into one concentrated program.

Design use:

- University rivalry and forced cooperation.
- Civilian scientists drawn into military problems.
- A laboratory name that hides its actual purpose.
- Fast movement from theory into prototypes.
- A research office that becomes larger than the ministry supervising it.

Primary institutional sources:

- MIT News, `https://news.mit.edu/2015/how-tizard-mission-paved-way-for-MIT-research-1123`
- MIT Lincoln Laboratory, `https://www.ll.mit.edu/about/history/mit-radiation-laboratory`
- MIT News, `https://news.mit.edu/1996/radlab-1030`

### Government policy built around scientific institutions

Vannevar Bush's *Science, The Endless Frontier* argued for continuing public support of basic research through colleges, universities, and research institutes. The National Science Foundation's institutional history treats that report as a foundational statement about national research policy.

Design use:

- Evolution I turns Kruger from an advisor into the center of national policy.
- Universities compete for grants, staff, equipment, and prestige.
- The government can spread Kruger's methods across public institutions or concentrate everything under his private authority.
- Public science lowers rebellion risk but reduces secrecy and makes foreign observation easier.

Sources:

- National Science Foundation history, `https://www.nsf.gov/about/history`
- NSF narrative history, `https://www.nsf.gov/about/history/narrative`
- *The Endless Frontier*, 75th anniversary edition, `https://nsf-gov-resources.nsf.gov/2023-04/EndlessFrontier75th_w.pdf`

### Big science and multi-site secrecy

The Manhattan Project became a model for large-scale scientific organization, with multiple sites, compartmentalized access, security screening, and facilities devoted to different parts of one strategic objective. Its history also supplies a strong ethical warning about technological progress conducted under emergency authority.

Design use:

- Project capacity depends on facilities and specialized sites rather than one abstract button.
- Security can protect the program while increasing Kruger's private control.
- Multiple facilities make the network harder to destroy and make rebellion territory larger.
- Staff may understand only one part of a project, which makes sabotage, defection, and cleanup more complicated.
- Ethical resistance appears through universities, assistants, military officers, and foreign scientists.

Sources:

- U.S. Department of Energy, Manhattan Project background, `https://www.energy.gov/lm/manhattan-project-background-information-and-preservation-work`
- National Park Service, Manhattan Project frequently asked questions, `https://www.nps.gov/mapr/faqs.htm`
- U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Science history, `https://www.energy.gov/science/history`

### Scientific contracting across universities and industry

The U.S. Office of Scientific Research and Development funded work at universities, hospitals, research institutes, and industrial laboratories rather than placing every scientist inside one government building. That structure created fast mobilization and also concentrated prestige and money in a small network.

Design use:

- The host can choose a distributed public network or a concentrated Kruger network.
- Industrial firms can demand patents and production rights.
- Universities compete for access and may become foreign intelligence targets.
- A distributed network is resilient and politically safer, while a concentrated network completes projects faster and strengthens Kruger's independent capacity.

Source:

- Library of Congress OSRD collection, `https://www.loc.gov/collections/office-of-scientific-research-and-development-reports/about-this-collection/`

### Refugee and displaced scholars

The New School's University in Exile was created in 1933 to give dismissed European scholars a new institutional home. The broader period contained many scientists and academics displaced by political persecution and war.

Design use:

- Kruger can first appear through a refugee network, an obscure university post, a border crossing, a private workshop, or a rescued academic group.
- Host-country flavor should vary between established universities, colonial institutes, military laboratories, industrial firms, and exile communities.
- A country that refuses Kruger may send him toward a rival that is more willing to shelter or exploit displaced talent.

Source:

- The New School institutional history and archive material, `https://www.newschool.edu/nssr/history/`

### International recruitment and moral compromise

Operation Paperclip and related postwar competition show how governments pursued foreign expertise even when the scientists' records created moral and political problems. The historical details should not be copied directly into Kruger's biography. The useful design lesson is that a scientist can become a strategic asset whose recruitment changes diplomacy and whose protection creates a moral cost.

Design use:

- Evolution II foreign invitations, extraction attempts, defections, and kidnapping plots.
- Rivals weigh research need against legitimacy and scandal.
- A host can hide Kruger's worst work, publish it, prosecute it, or trade access for alliances.
- Foreign powers may prefer the assistants or project archives rather than Kruger himself.

Sources:

- U.S. National Archives, Operation Paperclip records and research guidance, `https://www.archives.gov/iwg/declassified-records/rg-330-defense-secretary/`
- Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum discussion of Operation Paperclip, `https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/project-paperclip-and-american-rocketry-after-world-war-ii`

### Scientists resisting the political use of research

The Franck Report is a strong precedent for scientists arguing that a weapon's political and moral consequences should shape how it is used. This supports an internal opposition that is scientifically informed rather than simply anti-progress.

Design use:

- Assistants can demand publication, demonstrations, safeguards, or international inspection.
- Public-science routes can preserve benefits while limiting weaponization.
- Suppressing dissent can transfer respected scientists to foreign countries and increase security exposure.
- Kruger can regard ethics committees as useful peer review, intolerable delay, or evidence that ordinary humans cannot govern his work.

Source:

- U.S. Department of Energy Office of Scientific and Technical Information, Franck Report records, `https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/franck_report.htm`

## Research boundaries

- Doctor Warren Kruger remains fictional.
- Real historical scientists should not be portrayed as Kruger's assistants, victims, or ideological supporters unless a later design receives separate source review.
- Historical institutions inspire mechanics and flavor variation. They are not copied as one-to-one event routes.
- Real atrocities or non-consensual experimentation should not be treated as comedy or as a neutral optimization puzzle.
- The project portfolio can become grotesque and dangerous, but final player-facing text must retain consequence and specificity.
