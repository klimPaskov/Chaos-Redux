# 071 Persia: satrapies and imperial diplomacy

## The basic charter

A satrapy is a subordinate regional state with a recognized government, territory, and obligations to Persia. It is not a label applied to every friendly country. A basic charter is available before Evolution I, because satrapies are central to the original premise.

A normal charter defines military access, a limited material or manpower contribution, the degree of local administration, and the conditions under which Persia provides protection. The player chooses the charter when a settlement becomes possible. A subject cannot be created from territory that the empire has no right or supported means to transfer.

The empire can retain a directly governed region instead. Direct administration gives clearer control but requires its own settlement and garrison. A subject arrangement gives regional institutions a role and can make distant territory more manageable. The strongest reward cannot be collected from both arrangements simultaneously.

## A viable regional state

Prefer an existing country identity when its actual territory and institutions fit the settlement. A living country keeps meaningful history, leaders, and an appropriate tree unless a specific accepted change requires otherwise. The restoration must not overwrite an unrelated major country's identity because it controls one relevant state.

A new custom tag is an exceptional last design choice requiring a full installed tag audit, a territorial and character package, flags, localisation, AI, and a reason that no existing identity works. This package does not prescribe a new tag. Its satrapy system is designed to work with existing viable regional states.

A satrapy requires an owned and controlled capital, a coherent territorial base, and a route by which its obligations can be delivered. A disconnected enclave without useful access remains an occupation or a negotiated local arrangement. It does not become a full satrapy for milestone counting.

## Obligations

The basic charter selects one principal material obligation and the standard military-access agreement. A subject with little industry can provide a smaller manpower or frontier-service contribution. An industrial subject can supply equipment. A resource region can provide resource access through supported trade or subject mechanics.

The subject must retain enough resources to remain viable. An obligation cannot debit more equipment or manpower than the subject has available. A shortfall creates a political issue or partial delivery. It does not generate negative stockpiles or conjure the missing equipment.

Routine obligations are settled at 90-day intervals through the event's existing or approved country-scoped scheduling. The first interval begins after the charter is accepted. Creating and dissolving a charter cannot accelerate several payments into the same day. Additional emergency levies are separate demands with consent rules and cooldowns.

## Qualitative status

A satrapy displays one status: loyal, bargaining, defiant, or seceding. It also displays the current obligation and the main reason for its status. It does not expose a separate numerical loyalty meter for every subject.

A loyal subject is meeting its charter and sees its basic protections honored. A bargaining subject has a specific dispute, such as a missed investment or an extra levy. A defiant subject has rejected a demand or suffered a serious breach. A seceding subject has entered a visible breakaway process or war.

Military defeat, loss of a connection, and local occupation can explain non-delivery without proving political treason. The player can distinguish inability from refusal. A subject cut off by an enemy should not be punished as if it deliberately withheld a shipment.

## Evolution I: specialized charters

| Charter | What it offers | What Persia owes | Main political risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Military satrapy | Bounded troop or manpower contribution and access | Equipment support and participation in its defense | A strong local command can challenge extraordinary demands |
| Resource satrapy | Defined resource or material access | Transport and local investment | Interrupted investment or excessive extraction produces bargaining |
| Frontier satrapy | Local defense, access, and frontier administration | Fortification and supply support | A neglected frontier seeks separate security arrangements |
| Autonomous historical kingdom | Durable local institutions and negotiated contribution | Recognition of its charter and ruling settlement | Violating autonomy can unite other chartered subjects |
| Closely governed imperial province | Stronger central administration and material obligations | Higher central administrative commitment | Defeat exposes the weak local legitimacy of the arrangement |

These are gameplay charter types, not a claim that every historical satrapy had these exact forms. Each type changes the imperial settlement and targeted obligations. They do not add five new national spirits to Persia.

A subject can change specialization after a 180-day review period. It must settle outstanding obligations and complete any new investment required by the replacement charter. Reclassification does not reset first-satrapy or regional legitimacy rewards.

## Court disputes and satraps

A satrap is represented by an existing valid regional leader or a properly sourced or clearly fictional character created during implementation. An institutional delegation is acceptable when a personal figure adds no useful gameplay. Do not create an unsupported historical biography to populate a list.

Disputes have distinct causes. A military satrap may object to losing its best troops. A resource satrap may ask Persia to repair the export route. A frontier satrap may demand intervention after a border attack. An autonomous kingdom may contest a central appointment. Each dispute offers a remedy tied to that cause.

A court hearing can enforce the existing charter, renegotiate it, compensate a breach, or authorize intervention. Several subjects can join a common hearing when the same imperial action affects them. This makes the Achaemenid assembly useful and gives the Sasanian and modern routes their own methods of arbitration.

## Tribute and material accounting

There is no universal new tribute currency. Tribute is delivered through a supported resource, equipment, industrial, or manpower agreement. A tribute demand shows what is being requested and what the subject retains.

Persia cannot count the same contribution both as a subject's normal levy and as an extra event shipment. A transfer must debit the sender and credit the receiver once. Equipment types must be legal for both the transfer and its intended use. A failure to support a specific desired transfer is an implementation blocker to resolve, not permission to display a fictional delivery.

A negotiated guarantee or access agreement may provide no tribute at all. Such a country is a partner, not a satrapy, and its status is shown separately.

## Factions and imperial blocs

A Persian bloc can be created when Persia has the required diplomatic authority and at least one consenting member. A subject's participation must follow its actual charter and game rules. Existing faction membership is addressed explicitly. Members of another faction cannot be silently copied into a second one.

The bloc's main purpose is defense of settled regions, access, and coordination. Its route charter determines war commitments. Achaemenid members can require an assembly mandate for an unrelated offensive. Sasanian frontier partners commit more directly to their assigned region. Modern clients negotiate defense and investment obligations.

A member can refuse a demand outside its charter, renegotiate, or leave under the stated conditions. Persia can respond politically or militarily through supported mechanics. Leaving never duplicates the member's troops or refunds completed investment to both parties.

## Evolution III: the outer imperial order

Supremacy requires legitimacy of at least 80 and a completed route conclusion. The empire can then make submission offers to reachable neighboring or strategically connected states. The target must have a reason to consider the offer, such as defeat, isolation, an existing agreement, or a large regional power imbalance.

Acceptance can create a tributary agreement, a guarantee, or a satrapy, depending on the offer. Refusal does not instantly annex the target or start a war without a player choice. A repeat demand waits 180 days and requires a meaningful change in circumstances.

Imperial guarantees create obligations for Persia. If a protected state is attacked, Persia receives an intervention choice with the consequences of honoring or abandoning the commitment. Existing wars are handled according to the agreement's terms. A player cannot guarantee a country after its defeat and claim to have defended it.

## Loss, exit, and recovery

A bargaining subject can be stabilized by completing the missing obligation. A defiant subject can enter a 120-day settlement hearing. A seceding subject requires a negotiated exit, a revised charter, or an actual conflict.

A peaceful release gives up the relevant imperial milestone and can reduce the crisis. A reconquest still needs a new settlement, but does not repeat the original first-completion reward. If Persia collapses, subjects keep their existing assets and follow the explicit breakup rules in part 12. They do not all turn into arbitrary hostile clones.
