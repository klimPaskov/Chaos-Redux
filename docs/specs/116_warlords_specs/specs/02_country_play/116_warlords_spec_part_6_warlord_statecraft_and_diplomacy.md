# Part 6: Governing, bargaining, and expanding

A warlord's first army gives him a chance to survive. Building a durable state requires choices about who governs, who pays, who serves, and who can give orders. This part supplies the political and economic substance behind the country package.

## Personal command or institutions

Personal command concentrates trusted formations around the founder. Its early advantage is reliable local coordination and a useful reserve. Its costs are a narrow officer base, dependence on the founder, and a difficult succession. Retinue benefits apply to a bounded actual cohort. Newly recruited formations do not inherit old personal loyalty merely by using the same template.

An institutional staff broadens recruitment and makes a larger army manageable. It requires real training, support equipment where used, and enough industry to sustain the force. It reduces dependence on the founder but gives the officer establishment interests of its own. A staff that has become an institution can oppose wasteful private adventures and support a lawful successor.

A regional civilian compact shares authority with institutions that can administer the population and economy. It improves the long-term political foundation when its terms are honored. It can constrain coercive mobilization, expansion, or the founder's control of revenue. The compact cannot both surrender powers and grant the military every benefit of unrestricted rule.

These are political commitments with compatible supporting branches. An institutional army can coexist with a civilian compact. A founder cannot keep a fully exclusive personal retinue constitution and simultaneously claim a completed civilian handover. Intermediate reforms can converge, but crossing a fundamental route lock needs an explicit, costly transition with old benefits removed.

## Economic choices with geographic consequences

| Development choice | What it enables | Constraint or exposure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Repair the inherited arsenal | Earlier domestic weapons and replacements | Uses damaged real military factories and repair capacity |
| Develop civilian administration | More sustainable construction and recovery | Delays the fastest military expansion |
| Build a transport backbone | Connects the capital, depots, and productive districts | Requires specific infrastructure and can be interrupted by enemy control |
| Depend on imports | Supports an army beyond local industry | Requires a willing supplier, a real route, and enforceable costs or obligations |
| Standardize equipment | Reduces template and production fragmentation | Retires incompatible plans and may require conversion or training |
| Pursue captured industry | Expands the resource base through war | Creates new fronts, occupation duties, and integration problems |

Focus and decision rewards should improve capacity in named real places or unlock a useful activity. A completed arsenal focus can prepare a production program, reduce an appropriate administrative barrier, or reward an actual industrial milestone. It should not fill the national stockpile regardless of whether the arsenal still exists.

## Patrons and foreign aid

A potential patron considers geography, ideology where relevant, rivalry with the parent, access, costs, and the risk of building a future enemy. Aid can be one shipment, an ongoing native lend-lease arrangement, advisers, volunteers under native rules, access, or an actual intervention. Recognition alone changes diplomatic standing and willingness. It does not transfer industry, summon troops, or create a faction.

Every material agreement names its sender, recipient, goods or forces, route, payment or obligation, duration, and termination terms. The recipient cannot promise the parent's territory before controlling it. A shipment waits for a usable route and debits actual cargo. A blocked route causes a pending delivery, a lawful cancellation, or a real loss according to the transport owner. It never becomes a successful delivery because the patron paid political power.

Aid relationships create choices. A patron may require access, influence over foreign policy, a repayment, or actual military subordination. The warlord can reject the terms, seek another supplier, build local industry, or fight for independence if the agreement becomes coercive. A public factual contract status is enough. No second patron-dependence currency is needed.

Compatible aid from several donors is allowed. Conflicting exclusive military command, access rights, or claims on the same industrial entitlement cannot all be promised and completed. The player must choose, renegotiate, or openly breach the incompatible obligation, with real consequences. When support ends, retire the contract's current benefits and restrictions according to its terms. Correctly delivered goods remain in their actual custody. An independently sustainable country does not collapse simply because a patron flag was cleared.

An adviser is a real person or an explicitly abstract service, not a duplicate famous general. Expeditionaries and volunteers retain their native ownership and return rules. Borrowed equipment remains subject to its contract where the native system distinguishes it. 116 does not promise that every HOI4 loan type can be converted into owned property.

## Rival warlords

Rivals can trade, make local nonaggression agreements, join a temporary coalition, divide a front, or fight. They do not automatically become one faction when The Generals Follow activates. Two regimes with the same origin can have incompatible ambitions and different patrons.

A local agreement can make campaigning easier without creating a common chain of command. A genuine subordination must transfer actual military authority. Annexation must resolve the actual government's and army's fate. A negotiated division of spoils cannot hand over a third country's territory without that war's real settlement.

The founder can pursue regional unification without Generalissimo. That leads to a larger ordinary military or civilian state using the chosen political route. It does not create a second canonical Generalissimo or the special Supreme War State and Final Warlord route. A successful ordinary unifier remains a meaningful campaign outcome.

## Expansion and integration

Expansion creates three distinct tasks: winning the war, establishing political control, and integrating usable capacity. A conquered country may be occupied, annexed, genuinely subordinated, or left independent under treaty. These outcomes carry different manpower, industry, resistance, and command consequences.

Integrating officers requires actual submission, a lawful command appointment, and resolution of prior loyalties. Integrating soldiers requires accounted manpower and equipment, a valid army arrangement, and supply. Integrating territory requires administrative access and the appropriate legal status. None of these is automatically accomplished by changing a cosmetic country name.

A route toward a larger national government can seek limited earned cores where historically and politically appropriate, after sustained control and civilian integration. Its state set must be explicit at implementation and cannot be all world territory or every parent core by default. Claiming to represent a nation does not itself increase recruitable population.

## Civilian transition

A civilian transition requires a functioning nonpersonal governing arrangement, a declared command settlement, and actual removal of the founder's independent political army. It may retain an ordinary national army under civilian authority. Its preparation should span a meaningful period with adequate supply, no unresolved military seizure in the capital, and a plan for veterans and officer appointments.

The country keeps its territory, technology, industry, current people, and ordinary military. Personal-rule bonuses and active warlord recruitment privileges end or transform. Founder history remains. A living founder may retire or hold a lawful subordinate role if the owner permits it. A failed transition can restore an actual military crisis, but it does not reset starting grants or resurrect a removed character.

An independent civilian successor remains an independent political lineage. Generalissimo cannot erase its place in the origin settlement simply because it stopped calling itself a warlord. If it still governs independently with armed forces, bringing the entire origin order under his command still requires a real campaign or genuine subordination.

## Succession, dependence, and long campaigns

A mature warlord state should remain playable after its founder. The chosen command constitution determines whether succession is orderly, contested, or dependent on a patron. New leaders inherit current problems and resources, not the opening army and treasury.

Foreign intervention may keep a weak regime alive, but a landless regime cannot recruit from a vanished home population. Exile support needs an actual host, actual surviving personnel, and owner-approved government mechanics. If no such basis exists, record defeat and surviving people through their real owners. Do not keep a fictional army alive to preserve a narrative branch.

The warlord AI follows this same logic. A small supplied state first secures survival, a growing one invests in replacements and administration, and an ambitious one expands when it can sustain the front. It should accept a tolerable settlement when defeat is probable and refuse a nominal union that gives away its effective independence without a reason.
