# Event 12 Africa specification, part 2

## Charter League, member agency, integration, economy, and diaspora

All names in this file are working labels, not final localisation.

## The Charter League as the main playable system

The Charter League is the political machine that turns the opening fantasy into gameplay. It begins as a defensive association. It can remain a confederation, become a federal union, become a network of crowns, become a revolutionary continental state, become a military hierarchy, or collapse into rival blocs.

The League must be valuable before it becomes coercive. A state should have rational reasons to join even when it intends to remain independent. The unifier earns influence by defending members, delivering aid, building connections, and respecting negotiated clauses. Integration becomes available only after a relationship has history.

The system should use a scripted GUI attached to the main decision category. The normal decision list remains the place for active missions and country actions. The GUI presents the continental picture, member status, regional progress, and the costs of centralisation.

## Player-facing values

### Charter Authority

Charter Authority measures the host’s ability to make continental commitments and have them believed. It rises through successful protection, arbitration, infrastructure completion, military victories, constitutional reform, and fulfilment of member clauses. It falls through broken guarantees, lost wars, forced annexation, abandoned projects, and leadership crises.

Authority affects:

- invitation acceptance
- emergency vote success
- member confidence recovery
- integration speed
- rival bloc formation
- foreign recognition
- access to late focus branches

Authority should not rise passively from owning land. A coercive empire can have wide territory and low Authority.

### Continental Reach

Continental Reach measures practical access. It combines connected ports, railways, supply corridors, air routes, intelligence links, and regional representation. It rises through map projects and member networks.

Reach affects:

- which countries can be invited
- how quickly aid arrives
- intervention cost
- regional congress availability
- diaspora transport capacity
- military reinforcement
- ability to respond to disasters and outbreaks

A member separated from the host by hostile territory can remain politically loyal while being hard to protect.

### Integration Burden

Integration Burden measures the strain created by direct rule, incompatible systems, resistance, rapid expansion, and unfinished regional settlements. It rises through annexation, forced integration, occupied capitals, simultaneous integration projects, and unequal extraction. It falls through representation, infrastructure, local administration, autonomy, demobilisation, and successful regional settlement.

High burden causes:

- slower core projects
- lower stability
- member fear
- production and supply disruption
- resistance growth
- rival claims
- military command penalties
- possible federal crisis

This value is the main check on instant continental snowballing.

### Colonial Pressure

Colonial Pressure is the combined external response to the League. It includes sanctions, intelligence activity, naval concentration, guarantees, concession disputes, ultimatums, and expeditionary planning.

Pressure rises through:

- intervention against a colonial power
- cancellation of foreign concessions
- seizure of bases
- forced annexation of protected territories
- support for liberation abroad
- high-chaos threats
- continent-wide military mobilisation

Pressure falls through:

- negotiated evacuations
- recognised treaties
- compensation agreements
- great-power war exhaustion
- successful deterrence
- foreign powers losing African interests

The value should unlock visible reaction events and missions. It must not be a hidden punishment meter.

### Member Confidence

Every member has its own confidence score. Confidence measures whether the state believes the League improves its security and preserves the clauses it accepted.

Confidence rises through:

- successful defence
- aid delivered on time
- joint projects
- arbitration respected
- autonomy clauses honoured
- regional representation
- shared military victories

Confidence falls through:

- integration pressure
- unequal resource extraction
- abandoned defence
- host defeat
- ideological interference
- foreign bribery or intimidation
- local leadership change

Confidence controls accession to deeper stages, departure risk, emergency support, and willingness to accept common institutions.

## Supporting values

The following values should exist where needed but should not clutter the main header:

- Regional Readiness for each geographic region
- Diaspora Settlement Capacity
- Diaspora Trust
- Common Defence Stockpile
- Member Representation Balance
- Restoration Legitimacy for revived polities
- Ecological Wrath for the high-chaos nature route
- Continental War Readiness during external crises

The GUI can show selected-member breakdowns and selected-region details through tabs or cards.

## Member relationship states

Every African country in the system should have one clear relationship state. The state controls available actions and prevents contradictory decisions.

### Outside state

The country has no formal relationship. It can be contacted, guaranteed, influenced, or opposed.

### Protected partner

The League has promised defence or aid. The country remains outside the faction and can judge the host through a proof mission.

### Associate

The country participates in selected League programmes without a full defence commitment. Associates can receive development aid, observers, and intelligence sharing.

### Chartered member

The country joins the League and gains full member benefits. It receives an initial non-integration protection term unless the country requested faster accession.

### Autonomous federal member

The country accepts common institutions and becomes a federated subject or equivalent political member. It retains a public country identity, local leader, and selected domestic powers.

### Integrated region

The country has completed a route-specific integration settlement. It may be annexed, federated into the common tag, or converted into a permanent regional administration depending on the constitution.

### Resistant partner

The country remains in contact but opposes current League policy. It can negotiate, build a rival coalition, accept foreign support, or return to cooperation.

### Leaving member

A timed withdrawal process is active. The host can negotiate, accept departure, offer concessions, or use coercion. Immediate annexation should not be the default response.

### Rival bloc member

The country belongs to a competing African coalition. Diplomacy, influence, border crises, and war become possible.

### Occupied settlement

The unifier controls the country through war but has not resolved its status. It creates high burden and cannot count as settled for Africa is one.

## Relationship transitions

| From | To | Normal cause | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Outside | Protected partner | guarantee or intervention agreement | host fails to protect |
| Outside | Associate | development or diplomatic pact | foreign pressure blocks deeper ties |
| Protected partner | Chartered member | successful proof and accepted invitation | local opposition |
| Associate | Chartered member | confidence and common project | loss of autonomy fear |
| Chartered member | Autonomous federal member | constitutional accession | integration burden |
| Autonomous federal member | Integrated region | completed representation and administration | resistance or route conflict |
| Chartered member | Resistant partner | low confidence or broken clause | rival influence |
| Resistant partner | Leaving member | failed negotiation | border and asset dispute |
| Leaving member | Outside | peaceful withdrawal | loss of League credibility |
| Leaving member | Rival bloc member | regional coalition exists | military escalation |
| Any cooperative state | Occupied settlement | war and direct control | severe burden and resistance |
| Occupied settlement | Autonomous federal member | negotiated settlement | weak legitimacy |
| Occupied settlement | Integrated region | coercive route completion | lasting resistance |

Transitions should be reversible when the story supports it. A state that leaves can return after a leadership change, external threat, or negotiated reform. A country annexed and later released should remember its prior relationship.

## Protection-first actions

### Guarantee sovereignty

The host offers a limited defensive guarantee. The guarantee has a clear duration, reach requirement, and response mission. Failure damages Authority more than refusing the request.

### Open an aid corridor

The host commits convoys, trains, trucks, fuel, or transport aircraft to a threatened state. The action creates a timed mission and can be disrupted by enemy control.

### Send a Charter mission

A diplomatic and technical mission improves contact, reveals local demands, and can open Associate status. It costs administrative capacity and may expose the host to espionage.

### Deploy volunteers

The host sends route-appropriate volunteers when legally and logistically possible. The decision uses equipment, manpower, command resources, and transport. Volunteer loss affects domestic support.

### Intervene against a coloniser

The host can enter an existing war on the side of an African country when the target is fighting a colonial or overseas power. The intervention should use a war-readiness mission and allow the protected country to refuse direct military entry if it fears annexation.

### Evacuate leaders and archives

When a state is near capitulation, the League can preserve its government, characters, and claim package. This creates an exile partner rather than automatic annexation.

### Recognise a provisional government

The host can recognise an African release movement or state created by another event. Recognition increases Authority when the government survives and causes a penalty if it collapses quickly.

## Invitation and accession

An invitation is a negotiation, not a one-click faction entry.

The target evaluates:

- threat from colonial or neighboring powers
- host Authority
- ability of the host to reach the target
- ideology compatibility
- current war
- relative strength
- existing faction or guarantee
- recent protection history
- integration policy
- regional rivals
- foreign sponsor pressure
- confidence in autonomy clauses

### Clause packages

The host offers one main accession package. Packages are route-sensitive and can be adjusted through decisions.

#### Defence Charter

The member receives strong protection and keeps broad sovereignty. Integration is slow. This is the easiest package for threatened states.

#### Development Charter

The member receives infrastructure and industry projects. The League gains economic access, but resource extraction must follow local benefit rules.

#### Federal Charter

The member accepts common institutions and a planned accession path. It gains representation and shared defence.

#### Crown Charter

A monarchic or restoration route recognises the member’s dynasty or traditional authority inside a continental council of crowns.

#### People’s Charter

A socialist or communal route creates shared labour, land, and production institutions. It increases integration speed among compatible states and provokes conservative resistance.

#### Security Charter

A military route offers immediate defence and command integration. It creates dependency and can become coercive.

#### Sacred or ecological compact

A high-chaos route offers protection through supernatural power. It is unavailable to ordinary AI except under extreme conditions and has dangerous obligations.

### Negotiated clauses

Members can demand:

- guaranteed autonomy term
- no foreign bases
- local command of regional units
- protected monarchy or religious office
- revenue share
- capital or holy-site protection
- representation quota
- right to leave after a term
- protection from forced ideology change
- border arbitration
- recognition of local language or legal institutions

The host can accept, modify, or reject. Accepted clauses become real triggers and failure conditions.

## Member benefits

A member should feel stronger inside the League.

### Common defence

Members can request emergency equipment, volunteers, air support, naval escort, intelligence, or intervention. The host cannot answer every request at once. A shared reserve and active mission cap create prioritisation.

### Development access

Members can propose state projects. Projects use local states, construction capacity, equipment, and transport. The member receives part of the benefit directly.

### Diplomatic shield

The League can resist coercive treaties, guarantee pressure, and foreign puppeting. Strong members can demand that the host defend their diplomatic autonomy.

### Arbitration

Members can submit border disputes or debt conflicts. A fair ruling raises Authority. A ruling that always favors the host creates resistance.

### Shared research and officer training

Later branches open technology sharing, military academies, and route-specific research. The benefits scale with member contribution.

### Disaster and disease relief

The League can pool medical equipment, trains, convoys, and construction capacity. This creates direct links to natural disaster, outbreak, air cleanliness, and death systems.

## Member obligations

Members may be asked to:

- contribute equipment or manpower to the common reserve
- permit transit
- host joint commands
- participate in a defensive war
- join a regional infrastructure project
- accept arbitration
- reduce foreign military access
- send delegates
- support sanctions against a colonial power

Failure should create negotiation before punishment. Repeated refusal can lower confidence across the League.

## Regional congress system

Africa is divided into nine practical regions matching the host overlays. Each region has a readiness score and a congress state.

A congress becomes available when the League has enough members, control, or influence in the region. The host chooses a venue and invites relevant governments, restored polities, liberation movements, and observers.

### Congress inputs

- member confidence in the region
- local military situation
- number of independent states
- number of occupied settlements
- foreign bases
- transport links
- unresolved border claims
- restoration legitimacy
- host Authority
- route constitution

### Congress outcomes

#### Regional charter

States remain separate members and accept common rules. This is the least burdensome result.

#### Federated region

Several states become one regional subject or a linked group of autonomous members. It reduces decision clutter and creates a regional military contribution.

#### Restored polity settlement

A historical identity becomes the regional federal center. This requires local legitimacy and must not erase unrelated states without a consent or war route.

#### Direct integration schedule

States agree to staged annexation and representation. The host gains a clear project chain.

#### Failed congress

States leave without agreement. Confidence falls and a rival bloc may form.

#### Coerced settlement

The host imposes terms. It gains rapid control and high burden.

### Representation balance

A unified Africa should not be governed only from the host capital. Congress outcomes create regional seats, military districts, courts, development boards, or councils. These are institutions, not map names.

Representation balance affects:

- integration burden
- member confidence
- civil war risk
- leader and advisor pools
- capital relocation or rotating congress options
- confederal victory conditions

## Rival blocs

A rival bloc forms when several African states share fear of the host, a regional identity, a foreign sponsor, or a competing constitutional vision.

Possible working bloc families:

- Nile and Red Sea coalition
- Sahel defence league
- Southern African coalition
- Indian Ocean league
- West African congress
- Equatorial river alliance
- monarchic restoration league
- anti-centralist republic league

These are faction or mechanic names. They do not replace country names.

### Rival bloc goals

A rival bloc may seek:

- protection from integration
- control of a region
- a looser continental constitution
- foreign recognition
- removal of the host leader
- division of the League
- war against a coercive unifier
- succession to continental leadership

### Counterplay

The host can offer reform, accept a competing congress, negotiate shared leadership, isolate the bloc, sponsor member defections, or prepare war. Bribing every member with the same resource should not be sufficient.

### Rival victory and succession

If a rival bloc defeats the host and controls enough continental institutions, its leader can inherit the unifier project once. This changes the regional overlay, leader package, and constitutional route. It preserves campaign continuity without allowing repeated infinite succession.

## Integration philosophies

The political route determines which integration tools are strongest.

### Federal consent

This route is slow, legitimate, and resilient. Members gain representation, local government, and a protected accession period. Cores arrive only after public accession, infrastructure, and administration missions.

Strengths:

- high confidence
- low resistance
- strong confederal and federal end states
- easier peaceful unity

Weaknesses:

- slow
- vulnerable to foreign interference
- strong members can block reforms

### Continental republic

This route creates common citizenship and central institutions through congresses and constitutional campaigns. It can integrate republics quickly when legitimacy is high.

Strengths:

- strong Authority growth
- broad diaspora route
- flexible ideology
- powerful public mobilisation

Weaknesses:

- monarchies and traditional authorities may resist
- elections or constitutional crises can interrupt expansion

### Council of crowns

This route preserves or restores monarchies and sultanates within a continental hierarchy. The host is first among several crowns or becomes a route-specific emperor only after broad recognition.

Strengths:

- restoration packages
- strong local legitimacy in compatible regions
- elite military and court diplomacy

Weaknesses:

- succession disputes
- republican hostility
- rival crowns may refuse subordination

### People’s union

This route uses labour councils, land reform, anti-concession policy, and shared production. It can integrate compatible revolutionary governments and support colonial uprisings.

Strengths:

- industry mobilisation
- rapid support for liberation movements
- strong common defence

Weaknesses:

- foreign sanctions
- conservative revolt
- risk of forced collectivisation and local resistance

### Military continentalism

This route treats security and command as the foundation of unity. Members become protectorates or military districts before political integration.

Strengths:

- fast intervention
- strong war preparation
- reliable common command

Weaknesses:

- low confidence
- high burden
- coups and regional commanders

### Confederation

This route accepts that Africa can be one in diplomacy and defence while remaining many states. It has a distinct success condition and does not require annexation.

Strengths:

- strong member agency
- low burden
- wide diplomatic reach

Weaknesses:

- limited direct manpower and industry
- members can leave
- some world-order routes require deeper unity

### High-chaos covenant

This route treats land, rivers, ancestors, animals, or supernatural forces as members of the continental order. It can bypass ordinary diplomacy with impossible power, but it creates ecological obligations and violent backlash.

Strengths:

- unique disaster and nature tools
- nonhuman allies
- extreme defensive power

Weaknesses:

- diplomatic isolation
- unpredictable disasters
- disease and ecological consequences
- route-specific world hostility

## Integration projects

Integration is a family of missions, not one button.

### Constitutional accession

The member accepts representation and common law. The mission requires high confidence and can fail after a local government change.

### Administrative bridge

The host and member connect civil services, currency, taxation, or supply systems. It costs civilian capacity and trained personnel.

### Security unification

The member’s forces enter a common command. It can create officer resistance and requires equipment standardisation.

### Transport connection

The member capital must be connected by rail, port, road, river, or air route. This makes geography matter.

### Local settlement

The host must resolve land, autonomy, leadership, and border issues. A one-size decision should not cover every region.

### Representation guarantee

The member receives seats or a protected regional office. It reduces burden and prevents immediate revolt.

### Core recognition

Only after the route’s required projects are complete does the state become a core or fully integrated federal territory.

## Coercive integration

Coercion remains a valid route and should be powerful enough to tempt the player.

Tools include:

- ultimatum
- leadership replacement
- security protectorate
- forced customs union
- occupation settlement
- annexation after war
- dissolution of a rival bloc

Costs include:

- high Integration Burden
- resistance
- foreign condemnation or pressure
- member confidence collapse
- possible guerrilla and civil war events
- loss of peaceful achievement eligibility
- reduced diaspora trust
- harder Scramble crisis

Coercion should not be blocked by moral text. Its consequences should make the choice legible.

## Economic and resource programme

The economic tree exists to make continental unity practical. It must produce state-level and network-level changes.

### Continental corridors

Each region receives one or more corridor projects:

- trans-Saharan caravan and rail route
- West Atlantic port chain
- Niger and Lake Chad supply network
- Nile and Red Sea corridor
- Congo river transport system
- Great Lakes shipping and rail links
- Swahili coast and Indian Ocean port network
- Cape to interior rail system
- Madagascar and island convoy network

Projects require control, member participation, construction capacity, trains, convoys, trucks, and security. They raise Reach and create strategic targets in war.

### Resource sovereignty

The League can review foreign concessions, demand local processing, create shared stockpiles, or seize assets. Every option has diplomatic and production consequences.

A fair resource programme:

- gives part of the benefit to the local member
- creates infrastructure or industry in the source state
- raises Authority
- limits extraction pressure

An exploitative programme:

- gives more immediate resources to the host
- raises burden and local resistance
- lowers confidence
- increases foreign and internal conflict

### Industrial standardisation

The League can standardise rifles, support equipment, trucks, rail gauges, aircraft maintenance, and naval supply. The focus and decision rewards should unlock production bonuses, licenses, templates, and common procurement rather than only flat output modifiers.

### Regional development funds

Funds are targeted project pools, not passive political power exchanges. The player chooses between transport, food security, mining, ports, arms, medicine, education, and housing. The cost uses factories, equipment, transport, and time.

### Food and drought resilience

Sahel, Horn, and southern routes need food security and drought response. Projects can reduce disaster losses, improve supply, and protect member confidence. High-chaos routes can twist this system into weather bargaining.

### Maritime development

Coastal and island members can build convoys, escorts, ports, dockyards, and naval bases. Maritime strength determines whether the League can protect distant members and diaspora routes.

## Diaspora return route

The diaspora route is a major branch, not one manpower decision. It represents voluntary movement, political exchange, professional recruitment, military service, cultural connection, and contested ideas of home.

The route must avoid treating the diaspora as a free population resource. Return requires transport, housing, employment, citizenship, local consent, and a political settlement.

### Historical inspiration

The route can draw from Pan-African congresses, the Universal Negro Improvement Association, Garveyism, the symbolic role of Ethiopia, transatlantic anti-colonial networks, Black seamen and labour organisations, veterans, scholars, and later wartime political networks. Historical figures and organisations require date checks and sourced portraits when used directly.

### Diaspora streams

- African Americans from the United States
- Caribbean communities
- Afro-Latin American communities
- African students, workers, and seamen in Europe
- wartime veterans and technical specialists
- exiles and political organisers

The player can prioritise one or several streams. The content should not imply that all people in a stream want to move.

### Diaspora Settlement Capacity

Capacity is built through:

- housing
- port and rail access
- jobs
- schools and universities
- medical capacity
- citizenship offices
- land and urban settlement rules
- security guarantees

Capacity limits the size and speed of return programmes.

### Diaspora Trust

Trust rises when the unifier offers rights, representation, safe transport, and real opportunity. It falls through forced settlement, unequal treatment, propaganda-only invitations, military conscription without citizenship, and local displacement.

### Decision families

#### Convene a diaspora congress

The host invites organisations and individuals to define terms. The outcome changes which streams are available and what rights are expected.

#### Recruit technical missions

Engineers, doctors, teachers, lawyers, organisers, artists, and industrial specialists can arrive as advisors, characters, or development capacity. Real figures need source review. Fictional figures need regionally appropriate generated portraits and names.

#### Establish return shipping

The host creates or contracts a transport network. The historical Black Star Line may inspire research, but the 1936 system should not falsely present the defunct company as an active fleet without an alternate-history restoration event.

#### Offer citizenship

The player chooses between immediate citizenship, staged citizenship, dual status, or temporary residence. The choice affects Trust, domestic politics, and integration.

#### Form volunteer cadres

Diaspora volunteers can train pilots, medics, engineers, or infantry. This uses equipment and training time. It should not spawn unlimited free divisions.

#### Build cultural institutions

Universities, archives, radio, publishing, and arts programmes raise Authority and connect regions. They also create political debate.

#### Settle local disputes

Return can create tension over land, jobs, language, class, and political influence. The player must resolve incidents through rights, compensation, local consultation, or coercion.

### Route outcomes

#### Shared citizenship

Diaspora communities become a represented part of the continental state. This strengthens legitimacy and technical capacity.

#### Elite return

The host gains powerful advisors and industry but risks creating an isolated political elite.

#### Military return

Veterans and volunteers strengthen the army. Civil-military tension and unequal rights become risks.

#### Cultural commonwealth

The route emphasises education, art, press, and international influence. It supports the confederal path.

#### Failed promise

Poor conditions or coercion collapse Trust. Recruitment stops, foreign criticism rises, and member confidence falls.

## League military system

Members contribute through a common reserve rather than automatic control of every army.

The reserve can contain:

- infantry equipment
- support equipment
- artillery
- trucks
- trains
- convoys
- aircraft
- fuel
- manpower commitments

The host spends the reserve on active defence missions, reinforcement, or regional commands. Members gain confidence when the reserve is used fairly.

### Shared commands

Regional commands unlock after a congress or federal settlement. They provide AI coordination and unit growth. A command should have a regional commander pool and clear transfer rules.

### Member units

Member countries retain armies until deeper integration. Federal members can contribute divisions or expeditionary forces. Direct annexation converts only part of the force and must avoid stockpile duplication.

## Departure and dissolution

A League member can leave when confidence is low, clauses are broken, the host loses legitimacy, or a rival bloc offers protection.

The departure sequence includes:

1. grievance event
2. negotiation window
3. timed withdrawal mission
4. settlement of units, bases, debt, and borders
5. peaceful exit, renewed membership, or war

The host can accept a peaceful exit. Doing so costs Reach but can preserve Authority. Forcing a member to stay increases burden and can provoke other exits.

The League can dissolve if the host is defeated, membership falls below a minimum, Authority collapses, and no successor exists. Dissolution creates an aftermath rather than deleting all relationships instantly.

## Charter presentation and action-family contract (normative, 2026-08-11)

The recurring Charter interface uses one shared action kernel for quote, payment, target validation, outcome dispatch, AI selection, generation, cooldown, and cleanup. The current action census contains 102 concepts: 96 ordinary source implementations and six deliberately runtime-gated concepts, actions 71–76, whose fictional-disease or strange-formation readiness contracts remain fail-closed.

The recurring Charter GUI owns the normal recurring action families. Six late or episodic families are permanently list-only in decisions and must not acquire duplicate Charter buttons: Scramble response actions 77–84, World order actions 85–92, constitutional route crises 93–99, post-unification governance action 100, host opening action 101, and regional congress/restorations action 102.

The list-only contract is a presentation boundary, not a second mechanic. List decisions and the recurring Charter GUI must preserve the same quote, target, validation, cost, outcome, AI, generation, cooldown, and cleanup semantics where they share an action concept. The player-facing surface must expose an action only when its phase and gates permit it, with no duplicate store, fallback button, or alternate payment lane.

Actions 71–76 remain intentional controlled runtime gates and are not omissions from the action registry, selector, or disposition matrix. Any future presentation change must preserve the six-family list-only contract and the shared action kernel rather than creating a parallel late-action GUI.

## Completion conditions for this part

The Charter system is incomplete unless:

- all five main values change through actual play
- each member has one relationship state
- protection can exist without membership
- membership can exist without integration
- clause packages create real obligations
- members can refuse, leave, and form rival blocs
- regional congresses can produce several settlement types
- integration requires map and political projects
- coercion is strong and costly
- economic projects change states and networks
- resource extraction has local and foreign consequences
- diaspora return uses capacity, trust, rights, transport, and settlement
- League military support uses a limited common reserve
- dissolution and succession have defined outcomes
