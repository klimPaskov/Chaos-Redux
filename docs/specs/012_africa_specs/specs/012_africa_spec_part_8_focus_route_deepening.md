# Event 12 Africa specification, part 8

## Focus-route deepening, internal dilemmas, branch interaction, and route payoffs

All focus labels in this file are working labels, not final localisation. This part refines the path architecture in part 3. It does not replace the shared focus tree with separate country trees.

## Design target

The focus tree should let the player build a kind of Africa, not only reach a continent-shaped border. Every constitutional route needs a different answer to five questions:

1. Who has the right to decide for the continental project?
2. What remains sovereign after membership?
3. How are resources and development distributed?
4. Who commands the armies?
5. What happens when a member refuses, withdraws, or blocks the centre?

The route becomes real through the answers. A federal route that annexes through the same buttons as the military route has failed. A socialist route that differs only through factory modifiers has failed. A crowns route that merely changes portraits has failed. A confederation that eventually behaves like a unitary state has failed.

## Tree size and anti-bloat budget

The complete file may contain many mutually exclusive and hidden focuses, but a normal campaign should see a curated tree.

- shared opening and host module: 14 to 20 visible focuses
- one constitutional route: 18 to 26 visible focuses
- shared League and integration lanes: 22 to 30 visible focuses
- economy, military, diaspora, intelligence, and restoration support chosen during play: 30 to 45 visible focuses
- post-unification and Scramble content: 18 to 28 visible focuses
- high-chaos lane when revealed: 12 to 20 visible focuses

A normal grounded campaign should complete roughly 85 to 115 meaningful focuses. The definition can contain more because incompatible routes, host overlays, regional overlays, crisis recoveries, and high-chaos content are hidden or mutually exclusive.

No branch should add nodes only to create visual length. Every cluster of three to five focuses must deliver at least one of the following:

- a new decision or mission family
- a changed Charter or member rule
- a map project
- a leader, advisor, council, or command change
- a unit, template, doctrine, or mobilisation system
- a constitutional clause with mechanical consequences
- a country identity change
- a regional congress outcome
- a crisis, failure, or recovery path
- a postwar or post-unification institution

## Cross-route state

The tree uses shared route state rather than isolated columns. Four public commitments should shape focus availability and effects.

### Consent commitment

Measures whether the host builds membership through negotiated clauses, referenda, regional congresses, and voluntary association. High consent improves Confidence and lowers Burden, but slows emergency action.

### Central capacity

Measures whether the continental centre can collect revenue, coordinate defence, enforce court decisions, and complete shared projects. High capacity unlocks stronger common actions. Capacity without consent raises withdrawal and rival-bloc risk.

### Regional equality

Measures whether investment, offices, commands, and representation are distributed among regional councils. Low equality makes the host’s original region richer and stronger, but it undermines integration elsewhere.

### Emergency rule debt

Tracks extraordinary powers used during war, civil conflict, withdrawal, or the Scramble. Emergency powers can save the League. Unpaid debt blocks stable constitutional capstones, creates leader and army conflicts, and can corrupt a grounded route into a coercive outcome.

These commitments are not extra decorative meters. They are readable components of the existing Charter values, member confidence, burden, and route state. They should use shared scripted values rather than creating four unrelated GUI bars unless implementation proves that the Charter panel cannot communicate them clearly.

## Shared opening: from claim to mandate

The opening is a practical test before constitutional ideology becomes permanent.

### Opening group 1: identify the host problem

The first focus group activates the host dossier from part 7. It creates one starting problem with a lifecycle and reveals the first domestic coalition. The player should understand that continental leadership depends on solving a real problem at home.

The branch then offers three compatible early responses:

- **administrative repair**, which improves capacity through civil service, transport, and revenue
- **political coalition**, which improves legitimacy through parties, councils, courts, traditional authorities, labour, or regional agreements
- **security preparation**, which protects the capital and corridors but can add emergency debt

The player may take all three. Order matters because the first congress reacts to what the host prioritised.

### Opening group 2: the first corridor

The first corridor is chosen from the host profile. It can be rail, river, road, lake, desert, port, or island convoy. Three focuses should form one compact play loop:

1. survey and choose the route
2. begin a timed construction, escort, or access mission
3. complete the route and use it for a real protection or aid action

Failure does not block the tree. It increases cost, reveals a rival route, or opens a recovery focus involving a different partner.

### Opening group 3: the first protected partner

The player selects or receives a threatened partner. The branch offers public support, material aid, military preparation, and negotiation. The final proof focus completes only after the partner survives, receives meaningful help, or signs a settlement.

The player can abandon the partner. That choice preserves resources but lowers Confidence and affects later focus text, AI trust, and congress votes.

### Opening group 4: the provisional congress

The congress does not ask the player to select one of six constitutions from a menu with no context. Delegates react to:

- the host’s domestic settlement
- how the first partner was treated
- where development was spent
- whether force was used
- which regional councils are represented
- current war and colonial pressure
- the host’s ideology and leader

The congress produces three outputs:

- a provisional membership charter
- a list of unresolved constitutional questions
- one route family that begins with political advantage, without permanently locking the player before the first constitutional debate

### Opening group 5: choose the constitutional principle

The six grounded routes become mutually exclusive at the point where deeper integration needs a binding principle. High-chaos content remains hidden. The route opener should change:

- member relationship rules
- the available integration stages
- which actors vote or negotiate
- the permitted use of pressure and military occupation
- leader and advisor pools
- route-specific decision families
- AI behaviour
- post-unification state form

## Route A: Charter federalism

### Player promise

Charter federalism builds one sovereign continental federation through negotiated regional and member rights. It is the most complete route for peaceful integration, but it is not the easiest. Strong members can block measures, regional chambers can demand redistribution, courts can rule against the host, and emergency action requires political work.

### Internal route map

#### Foundation group: representation before merger

The route begins by choosing the basis of representation.

- **Equal member chamber.** Every member has a protected voice. Small states gain Confidence. Large states resent equal voting on taxation and defence.
- **Population chamber.** Large states gain influence. Small members demand a stronger regional or constitutional court.
- **Two-chamber settlement.** Combines both, costs more time and administration, and becomes the strongest long-term settlement.

The choice changes congress votes and the cost of later federal conversion. It should not be a cosmetic tooltip.

#### Rights group: the protected member

This group defines rights that the centre cannot remove through ordinary decisions.

- language, religion, local law, and education protections where applicable
- limits on compulsory troop transfer
- local control of police or guard forces
- transparent revenue shares
- withdrawal or constitutional challenge procedures
- guaranteed access to the continental court

The player chooses between a narrow bill that is easier to pass and a broad bill that improves long-term Confidence but constrains the centre.

#### Executive group: presidency, council, or rotating chair

Three executive models provide different play.

- **Federal president.** Faster decisions and stronger diplomacy. Election defeats can replace the host leader or party.
- **Executive council.** High member confidence and coalition play. Emergency actions need more votes.
- **Rotating chair.** Best regional equality and confederal compatibility. Central capacity grows slowly.

The executive model changes leader presentation, AI route behavior, and the congress queue.

#### Fiscal group: contribution and equalisation

The player chooses how members fund the centre.

- fixed contribution by capacity
- negotiated project subscriptions
- shared customs and resource revenue
- federal taxation after a higher consent threshold

A separate equalisation branch determines whether poor and landlocked councils receive automatic development shares. Concentrating funds in the host is a valid short-term choice with visible trust loss.

#### Judicial group: court or political arbitration

The route can make the continental court binding, advisory, or appeal-based. Binding judgments improve peaceful border settlement and Confidence, but the host can lose cases. Political arbitration is flexible but vulnerable to power politics.

#### Defence group: common command under law

The route must decide whether member armies remain national, become dual-status forces, or gradually enter a federal command. The strongest option requires officer integration, supply standards, and member votes. A shortcut through emergency transfer raises emergency debt and military concern.

#### Federal conversion group

Members can become federal states, autonomous regions, or retained constitutional subjects. Conversion requires a domestic vote, settled claims, an administrative dossier, and a manageable Burden level. The route must keep at least one lawful refusal option.

#### Capstone: federation with enforceable limits

The capstone is available only if:

- the rights settlement exists
- at least several regions have real representation
- emergency debt is under control
- no unresolved forced integration crisis remains
- the fiscal and defence systems function

It changes the public identity, opens post-unification federal politics, and preserves regional institutions after Africa is one.

### Route dilemmas

#### Deadlock

A member chamber can block the budget, a military action, or an integration proposal. The player can negotiate concessions, call a new congress, use a constitutional override, or invoke emergency power. Each method changes the route’s later legitimacy.

#### Unequal development

A region that contributes less can still demand projects. Refusing is efficient but undermines the federal promise. Approving every project can overload the centre. The player needs a transparent priority formula and emergency exceptions.

#### Strong-member secession

A major member can legally threaten withdrawal. The route offers renegotiation, referendum, court review, or coercion. Coercion remains possible, but it converts federal gains into Burden and can open a military or confederal crisis branch.

### Support-lane mutations

- economy focuses unlock federal project subscriptions and equalisation formulas
- diaspora focuses use common citizenship with protected local participation
- restoration focuses create constituent states rather than simple puppets
- military focuses prioritise dual-status forces and regional commands
- intelligence focuses require legal warrants or member cooperation for internal operations

### Failure and recovery

A failed federal route can become:

- a looser confederation through negotiated retreat
- an emergency presidency that risks military continentalism
- a regional split followed by a second constitutional convention
- a federation in name with high Burden and recurring secession crises

Recovery should require new concessions and completed projects, not a single stability focus.

### Post-unification play

Federal Africa continues to hold elections, budget congresses, regional development votes, court disputes, and constitutional amendments. It is not finished when the map is unified.

## Route B: Continental republic

### Player promise

The continental republic builds a shared political nation through citizenship, parties or movements, elections, mass public institutions, and a common executive. It can integrate rapidly when populations support it, but it risks one-party capture, capital dominance, disputed elections, and conflict with monarchies or regional authorities.

### Internal route map

#### Citizenship group: civic status or staged accession

The route chooses between immediate common citizenship and staged citizenship tied to member accession.

- immediate citizenship raises popular appeal and diaspora Trust, but strains administration and alarms governments
- staged citizenship protects member sovereignty but can create unequal rights during transition
- dual citizenship preserves local identity and eases accession, but complicates military and tax obligations

#### Political vehicle group: party, movement, or coalition

- **Continental party.** Strong organisation and rapid campaigns. High risk of party-state capture.
- **Movement of movements.** Preserves local parties and liberation organisations. Coordination is slower.
- **Constitutional coalition.** Keeps ideological pluralism and member leaders. It is vulnerable to collapse after elections.

The choice changes campaign decisions, advisor pools, and election AI.

#### Franchise group: one electorate or regional colleges

A single continental electorate strengthens common identity and large population centres. Regional electoral colleges protect smaller members. A mixed system creates the strongest legitimacy and the most complex administration.

#### Executive group: president or collegial government

The player chooses a strong elected president, a prime minister responsible to an assembly, or a collegial executive. A strong president can answer crises quickly and accumulate personal power. Parliamentary government can fall during war. A collegial executive slows decisions but improves regional inclusion.

#### Public-service group

This branch builds:

- a continental civil-service examination and regional recruitment system
- local-language and translation capacity
- election administration
- anti-corruption inspection
- public radio and information networks
- a constitutional guard or police service

The player must choose whether the civil service is central, regionally quota-based, or seconded from members.

#### Election group

The first continental election is a real campaign event. Member confidence, party organisation, war, regional equality, and leader reputation affect the result. The player’s preferred coalition can lose. A lost election changes leadership and route priorities without automatically ending the game.

#### Republican guard group

The guard can be small and constitutional, mass-based, or fused with the army. Fusion is strong during crisis but creates coup pressure. The guard must not be a free elite division reward with no political consequence.

#### Capstone: a republic accepted beyond the capital

The capstone requires functioning elections, a civil service in several regions, at least one peaceful transfer or broad coalition settlement, and no unresolved citizenship crisis. It unlocks post-unification party politics and republican diplomacy.

### Route dilemmas

#### Election legitimacy

A close, boycotted, or wartime election can trigger recount, coalition, regional runoff, emergency postponement, or force. The result should change leader legitimacy, member confidence, and the possibility of military intervention.

#### Monarchy and traditional authority

The republic can abolish, retain ceremonially, federalise, or protect local monarchies. Abolition may gain radical support and create regional resistance. Protection improves accession but angers strict republicans.

#### Capital question

A fixed capital strengthens administration and creates regional suspicion. Rotating sessions support equality but are expensive. A distributed capital separates executive, court, military, and economic institutions across regions.

### Support-lane mutations

- diaspora focuses emphasise citizenship, voting rights, and professional missions
- economy focuses use public works, anti-corruption, and regional budget campaigns
- military focuses build a constitutional guard and civilian command
- restoration focuses convert crowns into cultural or federal institutions
- intelligence focuses create parliamentary oversight or a dangerous political police fork

### Failure and recovery

Failure can produce a one-party republic, a military caretaker government, regional election boycotts, or a constitutional coalition collapse. Recovery requires a new election, decentralisation, leader replacement, or negotiated amnesty.

### Post-unification play

Republican Africa has electoral cycles, party splits, regional campaign promises, anti-corruption crises, constitutional amendments, and foreign ideological competition.

## Route C: Council of crowns

### Player promise

The council of crowns preserves kings, sultans, emirs, royal houses, sacred offices, and restored courts inside a continental constitutional order. It should feel plural and negotiated. It must not place every historical polity under one invented emperor or treat traditional authority as politically uniform.

### Internal route map

#### Recognition group: who counts as a crown

The route begins with a legitimacy commission. A claimant can be recognised as:

- a sovereign member crown
- a federal or autonomous constituent crown
- a cultural and ceremonial court
- a disputed restoration under review
- an ineligible claimant with no adequate institution or support

Recognition uses the restoration legitimacy system. The host cannot create prestige by recognising every claimant.

#### Precedence group: seniority without permanent supremacy

The player chooses a precedence model.

- **First among equals.** One senior crown chairs the council. It gains diplomatic speed but cannot inherit members.
- **Rotating seniority.** High confidence and regional balance. Succession and schedule disputes can delay policy.
- **Constitutional hierarchy.** Crown ranks affect votes and military obligations. It is efficient and dangerous.
- **No precedence.** Every recognised crown is equal, while a separate elected secretariat handles administration.

The host’s own crown or traditional authority can lose seniority under rules it created.

#### Dual-chamber group

The route needs a non-royal political channel. The player can pair the council with:

- a chamber of member governments
- a popular assembly
- a regional council chamber
- a congress of traditional and civic institutions

A crowns-only constitution creates high republican resistance and cannot reach the stable capstone without another chamber.

#### Succession group

This group builds rules for:

- disputed succession
- regencies
- elective or rotational titles
- abdication
- extinction of a line
- marriage or personal-union claims
- removal of a ruler who violates the Charter

The council can arbitrate, permit local law, or impose a standard. Imposition raises central capacity and Burden.

#### Land and court group

The player decides whether recognised courts retain land, revenue, judicial roles, or ceremonial property. Broad restoration can improve local legitimacy and create serious social conflict. A constitutional settlement can separate cultural office from economic privilege.

#### Royal service group

Crown members can contribute guards, cavalry or ceremonial units, envoys, court schools, and regional mediation. These should become real military or diplomatic tools, not repeated elite-unit gifts.

#### Continental headship group

The route can end with:

- a ceremonial continental crown chosen or rotated by the council
- a council with no singular monarch
- a hereditary senior crown under strict constitutional limits
- a dual headship combining a crown chair and civic executive

A hereditary continental monarchy is the hardest and most coercion-prone option. It requires broad consent or creates recurring pretender and republican crises.

#### Capstone: crowns bound by the Charter

The capstone requires a functioning non-royal chamber, settled succession rules, limits on forced restoration, and recognised regional diversity. It opens post-unification court politics, regencies, and ceremonial diplomacy.

### Route dilemmas

#### Rival seniority

Two major crowns can claim precedence. The player can rotate, divide functions, hold a council vote, sponsor one claimant, or downgrade both. Sponsorship can start a royal rival bloc.

#### Republics inside the council

Republican members can remain as equal governments, associate members, or second-chamber participants. Forcing them to adopt crowns creates severe concern and can make the route a conquest ideology.

#### Restoration without support

A historic name can be popular abroad and weak locally. The player can fund cultural restoration, delay statehood, create an autonomous region, or impose a client crown. The last option gives fast control and long resistance.

### Support-lane mutations

- restorations receive the deepest legitimacy and overlap mechanics
- military focuses create regional host contingents and a common guard council
- economy focuses choose court endowments, public treasuries, or mixed development trusts
- diaspora focuses use citizenship without granting privileged return courts
- intelligence focuses use court envoys and succession monitoring, with an intrigue risk

### Failure and recovery

Failure can produce a succession war, republican revolt, pretender network, or a council reduced to ceremony while generals govern. Recovery requires a new precedence settlement, regency, or civic chamber, not merely removing a negative idea.

### Post-unification play

The route continues through succession events, royal tours, constitutional disputes, protected cultural institutions, court competition, and a possible transition toward a republic or federal council.

## Route D: People’s union

### Player promise

The people’s union builds continental power through workers, farmers, soldiers, cooperatives, liberation movements, local councils, and common control of land and resources. It should offer several socialist and communal models rather than one generic planned economy. Its power comes with sanctions, elite resistance, production disruption, and a constant risk that the party, army, or bureaucracy captures the union.

### Internal route map

#### Coalition group: who makes the revolution

The route begins with a choice among overlapping bases.

- **Workers and transport unions.** Strong ports, mines, rail, and industry. Weak rural reach.
- **Peasant and cooperative congresses.** Strong food, local legitimacy, and land reform. Slower central industry.
- **Liberation movements.** Strong anti-colonial war and foreign support. High military-political tension.
- **Party-led front.** Fast coordination and ideology spread. High one-party capture risk.
- **Council coalition.** High local autonomy and consent. Slower central planning.

The player can broaden the coalition through later focuses, but the first base shapes leaders, advisors, and decisions.

#### Land group: communal, cooperative, or state control

Three main models are valid.

- communal tenure with local councils
- producer cooperatives with public credit and price guarantees
- state farms and national land administration

A mixed settlement can preserve productive farms and redistribute unused or coercively acquired land. Forced collectivisation gives fast central control and can cause food and member crises.

#### Resource group: expropriation or negotiated transfer

The route decides how mines, plantations, railways, ports, and foreign concessions change ownership.

- immediate expropriation
- compensated transfer
- joint public control during transition
- worker and regional ownership
- continued contracts under strict public law

The choice changes foreign sanctions, technical capacity, regional revenue, and labour support.

#### Planning group: centre, regions, or federated plans

- central plan sets continental quotas and priorities
- regional plans negotiate contributions and projects
- sector councils manage transport, food, mining, power, and industry
- a mixed congress sets broad goals while members choose methods

The strongest plan requires data, trained administrators, transport, and member consent. It cannot appear from one focus.

#### Party and council group

The route chooses whether parties lead councils, councils supervise parties, several socialist organisations compete, or one vanguard party claims emergency authority. This choice affects elections, purges, dissent, and the possibility of a constitutional alliance with federal or republican institutions.

#### Liberation group

This branch creates equipment pipelines, training missions, propaganda, intelligence, and volunteer support for colonial movements. It must use target-specific costs and avoid free war-goal spam. A liberation government can refuse immediate integration and remain a protected ally.

#### People’s defence group

The military can be based on regional militias, a professional revolutionary army, worker and peasant reserves, or liberation fronts integrated after victory. The branch must solve officer training and supply. Mass recruitment without equipment increases casualties and disorganisation.

#### Bureaucracy group

The route needs a visible answer to corruption, patronage, party privilege, and command economy distortion.

- worker inspection and recall
- professional planning service
- party discipline commissions
- regional audit councils
- secret-police enforcement

The last option is powerful and opens a coercive failure branch.

#### Capstone: union through social institutions

The capstone requires functioning food, resource, and defence systems in several regions, a resolved party-council settlement, and no unresolved forced reform crisis. It opens post-unification planning congresses and anti-colonial foreign policy.

### Route dilemmas

#### Labour versus food

Industrial mobilisation can draw workers and transport away from agriculture. The player must invest in mechanisation, seasonal mobilisation, food imports, or slower industrial targets.

#### Liberation versus consolidation

Supporting every revolt can exhaust equipment and create hostile great-power coalitions. Consolidation improves the home economy but disappoints movements promised aid.

#### Local councils versus central plan

Local autonomy can block continental priorities. Central quotas can undermine confidence and create black markets. The route needs bargaining, incentives, and emergency limits.

#### Technical dependence

Foreign engineers and managers may be necessary after expropriation. The player can retain them under contract, train replacements, seek allied missions, or accept short-term production loss.

### Support-lane mutations

- economy becomes a production, food, transport, and resource-planning system
- diaspora focuses prioritise voluntary technical missions, labour rights, and equal citizenship
- military focuses build people’s defence, professional cadres, or liberation armies
- restoration focuses treat historical polities as cultural or autonomous councils unless a local socialist restoration is plausible
- intelligence focuses choose liberation liaison, counter-sabotage, or political security

### Failure and recovery

Failure can produce famine, bureaucratic stagnation, party-state capture, military takeover, regional council revolt, or foreign-backed counterrevolution. Recovery requires food and production reform, a new congress, leadership change, decentralisation, or constitutional guarantees.

### Post-unification play

The union continues through planning congresses, labour disputes, regional quotas, reconstruction, international liberation aid, and a choice between permanent revolution and durable social development.

## Route E: Military continentalism

### Player promise

Military continentalism unifies command before political settlement. It is the fastest route for survival, anti-colonial war, and response to the Scramble. It is also the route most likely to create occupation, regional commanders, coups, permanent emergency law, and a continent held together by force.

### Internal route map

#### Mandate group: emergency or permanent command

The route begins by defining the legal source of military power.

- temporary mandate from the provisional congress
- revolutionary command claiming liberation legitimacy
- host-government emergency authority
- council of regional commanders
- direct soldiers’ state

Temporary authority gains Confidence but creates deadlines. Direct military rule moves quickly and accumulates emergency debt immediately.

#### Command group: general staff or regional commands

- central general staff controls operations and procurement
- regional commands retain operational autonomy
- dual command divides continental and home defence
- rotating war council prevents one commander from dominating

Centralisation improves offensive planning. Regional command reduces mutiny and local supply problems. A dual system is strongest when the player invests in liaison and standards.

#### Army integration group

The player must decide what happens to member armies.

- remain national under common plans
- contribute designated corps
- merge gradually by unit and officer standards
- transfer fully after constitutional settlement
- disarm and replace under coercive occupation

The final option is available, but it sharply raises Burden and rival-bloc risk.

#### Protectorate group

Security members can retain governments, receive military advisers, accept bases, or become occupied command districts. Each stage needs a clear exit and postwar settlement. A protectorate should not be a hidden annexation shortcut.

#### Logistics group

Military continentalism gains unique corridor, depot, rail, port, fuel, and equipment missions. It should be the best route at moving armies across Africa only after completing these projects. Free strategic redeployment bonuses without infrastructure would erase the central challenge.

#### Officer group

The route creates characters and factions among:

- professional officers
- liberation commanders
- regional generals
- traditional military leaders
- foreign-trained cadres
- political commissars or civilian liaison officers where compatible

Promotion, retirement, loyalty, and regional balance affect coup and warlord risk.

#### Civilian relation group

The player must choose:

- civilian supervision with a defence ministry
- military veto over foreign and security policy
- a transitional council with a fixed handover date
- permanent command government

The choice determines whether the route can later converge with federal, republican, or confederal institutions.

#### Mobilisation group

Options include selective professional expansion, regional reserves, mass conscription, labour battalions, or emergency levies. Each has equipment, production, stability, and casualty effects.

#### Capstone: command of Africa

A stable military capstone requires supply integration, officer settlement, successful wars, and a clear relationship to civilian rule. A permanent command state can reach the capstone faster, but it locks in high Burden and a coercive post-unification path.

### Route dilemmas

#### The successful commander

A regional commander who wins several campaigns can demand political power, regional autonomy, or succession rights. The player can promote, rotate, retire, prosecute, or bargain. Mishandling can create a warlord state.

#### Occupation versus partnership

Direct military administration is reliable during war and toxic after peace. The route needs handover missions, local recruitment, courts, and public services. Failure leaves resistance and international condemnation.

#### War tempo versus equipment

The route can mobilise faster than the economy can equip. The player must pause, standardise, rely on foreign arms, or field irregular formations with clear weaknesses.

#### Handover

A promised return to civilian government can be honoured, delayed, rewritten, or abandoned. Honouring it opens a convergence branch. Abandoning it strengthens the command state and creates member exits.

### Support-lane mutations

- economy prioritises arsenals, rail, depots, fuel, ports, and emergency production
- diaspora focuses use veterans, pilots, engineers, and medical missions, with civilian oversight questions
- intelligence focuses build military liaison, reconnaissance, and counter-coup networks
- restoration focuses create military protectorates or regional host contingents, with higher coercion risk
- Scramble focuses give the route the fastest emergency response and the hardest demobilisation

### Failure and recovery

Failure can produce a coup, command split, warlord secession, mutiny, defeated occupation, or civilian mass resistance. Recovery requires a command conference, amnesty, officer rotation, constitutional handover, or regional autonomy.

### Post-unification play

Military Africa continues through demobilisation, veteran settlement, command reform, foreign intervention, military budgets, and the choice between a constitutional defence state and a permanent continental command.

## Route F: Confederation of African states

### Player promise

The confederation treats Africa is one as a durable political concert rather than one unitary country. Members retain sovereignty, governments, armies, and the right to withdraw under defined procedures. The route has the lowest integration Burden and the weakest direct access to member manpower and industry. Its challenge is collective action.

### Internal route map

#### Sovereignty group

The route writes the protected powers of members.

- domestic law and taxation
- army ownership
- foreign representation within agreed limits
- local citizenship
- resource ownership
- withdrawal procedures
- constitutional amendment vetoes

The player can write a narrow or broad sovereignty clause. Broad protection raises confidence and reduces central tools.

#### Voting group

The confederation chooses unanimity, qualified majority, weighted voting, or issue-specific rules. Unanimity protects members and can paralyse war. Qualified majority requires opt-out and compensation rules. Weighted voting raises large-state dominance concerns.

#### Secretariat group

The centre can be:

- a small rotating secretariat
- a permanent civil service with limited competencies
- several regional secretariats coordinated by a congress
- a host-provided administration under strict audit

The host-provided model is cheap and dangerous because it can become disguised centralisation.

#### Defence group

Members can promise:

- mutual defence only
- designated expeditionary forces
- a standing confederal reserve
- shared air and naval commands
- full operational command during declared emergencies

The stronger options require votes, joint exercises, and supply standards.

#### Market group

The player chooses between a free-trade area, customs union, common market, sector agreements, or project-based economic cooperation. A full common market gives Reach and development but forces labour, tariff, and compensation debates.

#### Foreign-policy group

The confederation can require common recognition policy, shared sanctions, joint treaty approval, or only consultation. Members may keep embassies and separate alignments. Divergent foreign patrons can create sponsor blocs inside the confederation.

#### Withdrawal group

A real withdrawal process includes notice, debt settlement, border and minority guarantees, military property, and referendum or legislative approval. The host can honour, delay, or block it. Blocking withdrawal can convert the route into coercive federalism or war.

#### Crisis group

The route needs emergency rules for invasion, blockade, famine, member civil war, and the Scramble. Emergency power can be time-limited, regionally delegated, or vested in a war council. Each use creates a review mission.

#### Capstone: Africa in concert

The capstone requires several functioning joint systems, successful collective action, and at least one resolved disagreement without force. It opens post-unification confederal diplomacy and an alternative victory state where members remain visible.

### Route dilemmas

#### Free rider

A member can take trade and protection benefits while refusing contributions. The player can negotiate, suspend benefits, impose fines, reduce voting rights, or tolerate the behaviour to preserve unity.

#### Divergent war

One member wants war while others refuse. The route can allow a coalition of willing members, require a confederal vote, impose neutrality, or risk collapse.

#### Great-power patronage

Members can accept outside aid that undermines common policy. The player can regulate, disclose, balance, or ban foreign patronage. A ban raises sovereignty concern.

#### Exit contagion

One peaceful withdrawal can encourage others. The route must show that honouring the charter can preserve long-term trust even when membership shrinks.

### Support-lane mutations

- economy focuses create opt-in projects, common standards, and compensation funds
- military focuses build reserves and joint commands without owning all armies
- diaspora focuses use portable citizenship and member-chosen programmes
- restoration focuses allow many protected sovereign or associate identities
- intelligence focuses rely on shared reporting and counter-infiltration agreements

### Failure and recovery

Failure can produce paralysis, sponsor blocs, mass withdrawal, a two-speed confederation, or an emergency centre that refuses to dissolve. Recovery requires a smaller treaty, revised voting, regional compacts, or a federal transition accepted by members.

### Post-unification play

Confederal Africa continues to manage member elections, exits, collective defence, common projects, patron competition, and varying levels of participation. The map need not collapse into one colour for the route to succeed.

## Hidden route G: Covenant with the land and nonhuman congress

### Player promise

The high-chaos constitutional route asks whether human governments can bargain with entities, landscapes, animals, storms, rivers, forests, diseases, or ancient hosts that have become political actors. It is not an ethnic or cultural route. Every nonhuman actor must be visibly impossible, supernatural, or nonhuman.

The route is hidden until high chaos, relevant evolutions, and a concrete reveal event make it understandable. Grounded routes should have time to establish themselves before the Covenant can replace or corrupt them.

### Entry forms

The route can enter through one of four sources.

- a nature covenant that begins as disaster prediction and protection
- a nonhuman state that demands League recognition
- an ancient or living-statue host that reclaims strategic sites
- a disease, oracle, or ecological council that offers power at a public cost

Each source changes the route’s early focuses and asset state. They converge only after the player decides whether the entity can become a political member.

### Internal route map

#### Recognition group: person, power, territory, or threat

The player decides how the Charter classifies the actor.

- a sovereign nonhuman member
- a protected ecological territory
- a treaty-bound power without normal citizenship
- a military ally under containment
- a threat that cannot be represented

Recognition changes diplomacy, member confidence, and whether ordinary governments can vote on the actor’s internal affairs.

#### Consent group: who can agree

The route must not pretend that a single leader can consent for every affected person or ecosystem. Possible institutions include:

- local human councils
- scientific and medical observers
- religious and cultural representatives chosen for the specific site
- regional governments
- the nonhuman actor’s own visible emissaries
- a mixed tribunal

The player can bypass these institutions for speed. That choice increases power and catastrophe risk.

#### Favor and wrath group

Favor can provide disaster forecasts, terrain advantages, food or water support, unusual units, healing, or resource access. Wrath can create storms, drought, flood, disease, contamination, infrastructure damage, or hostile entities. Favor should not be a free resource bar. It rises when the player honours public obligations and falls through broken promises, extraction, battlefield abuse, or containment failure.

#### Boundaries group

The Covenant decides whether sacred, ecological, or anomalous zones are:

- sovereign territories
- protected regions inside member states
- shared continental trusts
- temporary military exclusion zones
- resource sites under strict quotas

This choice interacts with state control, construction, resource extraction, and member sovereignty.

#### Warfare group

The player can allow defensive use, retaliatory use, controlled battlefield use, or unrestricted wrath. Escalation can trigger recurring disasters or outbreaks against enemies, but it can spread to members and raise global condemnation. Weaponised disease requires the existing biological warfare system, containment, and visible ethical consequences.

#### Nonhuman forces group

Strange units can include clearly nonhuman great apes, living statues, impossible animals, ancient hosts, storm-bound formations, or other researched fictional designs. Each formation needs a recruitment source, supply model, limit, counterplay, and story. They cannot be simple elite infantry with a new icon.

#### Human government group

The player chooses whether human institutions remain superior, equal, subordinate, or separate. Subordination creates the strongest high-chaos power and can end ordinary elections, crowns, and member rights. Equality is difficult and unstable. Separation preserves normal politics but limits access to power.

#### Capstone: a Charter that includes the impossible

The stable capstone requires a consent institution, controlled wrath, protected boundaries, and at least one crisis resolved without catastrophe. A domination capstone can ignore those conditions, but it creates a transformed high-chaos state with severe diplomatic and human costs.

### Route dilemmas

#### The demand

The entity makes a public demand involving land, extraction, war, prisoners, disease, worship, migration, or political status. The player can comply, negotiate, substitute another obligation, contain the entity, or refuse. Refusal can cause disasters without openly referencing another event by name.

#### Forecast or manipulation

An oracle or ecological council predicts a disaster. Acting can save lives and increase legitimacy, but it can also be a political test or self-fulfilling act. The player needs decisions that move units, stockpile supplies, evacuate, reinforce infrastructure, or challenge the prediction.

#### Containment breach

A disease, creature, or ancient host can escape control. Recovery needs military, medical, scientific, and political responses. A single political-power payment must never remove the crisis.

#### Member revolt

Ordinary members can refuse nonhuman representation or object to the host sacrificing their territory. The player can protect their right to leave, renegotiate, impose emergency rule, or allow the Covenant to punish them. The last choice moves the route toward domination.

### Support-lane mutations

- economy focuses choose sustainable access, sacrifice zones, or impossible production
- military focuses control, integrate, or unleash nonhuman formations
- diaspora focuses protect human citizenship and voluntary participation, or collapse under a nonhuman order
- restoration focuses can awaken ancient actors only where research and high-chaos logic support them
- intelligence focuses become observation, omen interpretation, containment, and counter-cult work
- Scramble focuses can use impossible weather or disease against expedition forces at global cost

### Failure and recovery

Failure can produce ecological collapse, uncontrolled disease, an entity coup, human civil war, shattered member confidence, or a continent divided between normal and anomalous states. Recovery may require sealing the route, sacrificing access to power, restoring human institutions, or accepting a smaller protected anomaly.

### Post-unification play

The Covenant route continues through environmental obligations, nonhuman diplomacy, global fear, containment, impossible wars, and a final choice between coexistence and domination.

## Shared support lanes with route-specific meaning

The support lanes should not be neutral reward columns. Each constitutional route changes their methods, costs, and payoffs.

## Economy and corridors

### Corridor philosophy fork

The player chooses how continental infrastructure is selected.

- **Strategic first.** Prioritises military and high-value routes. Readiness rises quickly. Regional equality falls when remote members are ignored.
- **Need first.** Prioritises food, health, landlocked access, and low-development regions. Trust rises. Large projects take longer.
- **Contribution first.** Members that provide money, equipment, or labour receive projects. Capacity rises. Poor members can become permanent peripheries.
- **Balanced portfolio.** Uses a public formula with emergency exceptions. It is administratively expensive and best suited to federal or republican routes.

The focus branch then offers several project families rather than identical construction nodes.

### Transport family

- trans-regional rail and road corridors
- river and lake navigation
- desert water and road chains
- coastal port and convoy networks
- island air and shipping links
- cross-border supply and customs posts

Every project names target regions and has a mission that can fail through war, access loss, equipment shortage, or member withdrawal.

### Resource family

The player chooses among extraction expansion, local processing, public ownership, regional revenue shares, or strategic reserves. Resource sovereignty is not a flat production bonus. It changes contracts, foreign relations, local labour, and member contributions.

### Food, water, and health family

The branch creates irrigation, veterinary networks, grain and seed reserves, famine routes, hospitals, disease surveillance, and emergency logistics. It interacts with natural disasters, locusts, outbreaks, contamination, migration, and high-chaos forecasts where relevant.

### Industrial family

Industrial growth can be distributed through regional plans, concentrated in proven hubs, attached to military corridors, or assigned by member contribution. The route must visibly build factories, power, rail, ports, workshops, and training institutions. A late continental plan should upgrade earlier projects rather than create unrelated national spirits.

### Route differences

- federalism uses equalisation and member subscriptions
- republic uses public budgets, anti-corruption, and electoral mandates
- crowns uses crown trusts, public treasuries, and regional endowments
- people’s union uses plans, cooperatives, worker control, and nationalisation
- military uses depots, arsenals, fuel, and emergency construction
- confederation uses opt-in projects, compensation, and common standards
- Covenant uses protected zones, sustainable bargains, or sacrifice economies

## Military and common defence

### Defence purpose fork

The shared military lane begins with a real strategic doctrine choice.

- continental denial and depth defence
- mobile intervention to protect members
- mass liberation warfare
- professional common army
- regional hosts and specialised forces
- high-chaos containment and anomalous warfare

The choice changes unit templates, logistics, command, training, and AI. It should not be five branches that all end with attack and defence modifiers.

### Army development groups

- officer and liaison schools
- equipment standardisation or interoperability
- regional reserve and mobilisation systems
- special terrain forces
- railway, river, desert, mountain, jungle, and island logistics
- medical and contamination protection
- artillery, armour, motorisation, or irregular support based on host capacity
- veteran integration after liberation wars

### Air and naval groups

The air branch develops regional airfields, transport, reconnaissance, coastal patrol, and air defence before advanced air power. The naval branch differentiates Mediterranean, Atlantic, Red Sea, Indian Ocean, lake, and river needs. A landlocked host should lead a joint fleet through members rather than receive free dockyards in impossible states.

### Command crisis content

At least one focus group responds to:

- a commander gaining regional political power
- incompatible equipment or doctrine
- member refusal to deploy
- casualty disputes
- captured colonial officers and specialists
- postwar demobilisation

The military lane should remain politically connected to the constitutional route.

## Diaspora return and exchange

### Programme fork

The player chooses a leading purpose.

- citizenship and family return
- technical and educational missions
- business and cooperative investment
- military and veteran service
- anti-racist international advocacy
- cultural, archival, and university exchange

All programmes remain voluntary. Land settlement requires local consent, housing, services, and capacity.

### Representation group

Diaspora participants can receive individual citizenship, dual citizenship, local representation, a consultative chamber, or no separate political category. A privileged diaspora estate is a dark failure, not the normal reward.

### Capacity group

The branch must build homes, schools, hospitals, transport, employment, and local institutions. Inviting large numbers without capacity raises housing pressure, local distrust, and emigration disappointment.

### Route differences

- federalism offers protected dual citizenship and regional participation
- republic emphasises common citizenship and voting
- crowns uses court, university, military, and cultural missions without hereditary privilege
- people’s union uses labour, cooperative, technical, and liberation links
- military emphasises veterans, pilots, doctors, and engineers under civilian protections
- confederation lets members opt into programmes and recognise portable rights
- Covenant can preserve human refuge and citizenship, or corrupt return into service of a nonhuman order

## Intelligence and counter-intervention

### Intelligence purpose fork

- defensive counter-intelligence
- liberation liaison
- foreign influence exposure
- military reconnaissance
- political security
- high-chaos observation and containment

Internal political security is the dangerous branch. It can improve short-term control and undermine every consent-based route.

### Operational groups

- map foreign concessions and bases
- protect aid and congress communications
- expose rival patronage
- support underground movements
- secure ports, rail, and convoy information
- counter sabotage and coup networks
- divide or deter a Scramble coalition

Operations need targets, risk, intelligence capacity, and consequences. They should not be free hidden effects from focuses.

## Restoration lane

### Restoration sequence

A serious restoration should move through:

1. historical and institutional survey
2. local support and claimant review
3. overlap negotiation
4. provisional cultural or administrative recognition
5. protected membership or autonomy
6. constitutional settlement
7. post-restoration survival and development

A player can skip stages through military imposition. That creates a client or occupation package with low confidence and strong failure risk.

### Route differences

- federalism creates constituent states and protected local institutions
- republic offers cultural autonomy, regional republics, or ceremonial institutions
- crowns supports the deepest royal and court restorations
- people’s union preserves historical identity through councils, communes, or cultural bodies when class politics permits
- military creates protectorates, regional commands, or client restorations
- confederation can admit restored states as sovereign members
- Covenant can awaken impossible actors only after the historical route is clearly distinguished from fantasy

## Scramble response and post-unification branch

The Scramble is a test of the constitution the player built.

### Shared opening

Outside powers issue demands, sanctions, base claims, expedition plans, recognition conditions, or economic pressure. The player must choose among negotiation, selective concessions, coalition division, mobilisation, counter-sanctions, and war.

### Constitutional differences

- federalism needs emergency votes and can be blocked by members
- republic faces wartime elections, opposition, and executive power
- crowns must coordinate sovereign hosts and succession-sensitive commands
- people’s union mobilises production and international movements while facing embargo
- military responds fastest and risks permanent command rule
- confederation can split over war participation
- Covenant can unleash impossible retaliation with global consequences

### Settlement branch

After victory or negotiation, the player chooses:

- recognised sovereignty and treaty access
- demilitarised or jointly administered strategic zones
- compensation and concession settlement
- permanent continental mobilisation
- punitive overseas intervention
- a new world-order sponsorship path

The route should not end at the first super-event or first victory.

## Host-route interaction rules

Host overlays change route emphasis without rewriting the route.

Examples:

- Ethiopia adds imperial-federal and wartime survival conditions to federalism
- Liberia adds concession and citizenship reform to the republic
- Egypt adds Canal, Nile, palace, parliament, and officer conflicts
- Nigeria adds regional revenue and representation to every constitution
- Belgian Congo adds company, corridor, and regional administrative problems
- Uganda deepens the crowns and federal routes through multiple kingdoms
- Madagascar changes continental access through maritime service
- South Africa makes citizenship, land, and civil-war legitimacy mandatory across all routes

A country-specific node should either alter a shared rule, solve a host problem, or change a later payoff. It should not be a decorative branch that becomes irrelevant after the first congress.

## Focus pacing

The opening should be urgent but not instantaneous. Shorter focuses can establish the interface, host problem, and first mission. Major constitutional choices, corridor projects, integration reforms, and post-unification institutions should take longer and often require decisions or missions outside the tree.

The tree should alternate between focus completion and active play. A player should rarely complete eight focuses in a row without needing to protect a partner, finish a project, settle a member, fight a war, hold a vote, or respond to a crisis.

## Reward discipline

Focus rewards should primarily:

- open or change a decision family
- move visible Charter or member values by a meaningful amount
- build named infrastructure or production
- create or reform units and commands
- change constitutional rules
- transform an existing idea
- appoint or remove leaders and advisors
- settle a member or region
- unlock a crisis or recovery route
- create a postwar or post-unification institution

Small modifiers may support these rewards. They should not be the only effect of important nodes.

## Route-aware AI

AI route selection considers:

- host dossier and domestic problem
- current ideology and leader
- number and strength of members
- member government types
- active war and colonial pressure
- regional equality
- equipment and supply
- host industry and manpower
- current Confidence and Burden
- existence of strong crowns, labour movements, officer factions, or confederal partners
- high-chaos reveal state

AI should also know when to change course. A federal AI facing invasion can use temporary emergency powers without becoming permanently military. A military AI that promised handover can honour it when the war ends. A republic that loses an election should continue under the new government. A confederation can accept a peaceful withdrawal.

Invalid routes are hidden or weighted to zero when their required institutions, actors, or campaign conditions do not exist.

## Focus-route acceptance rules

The focus tree is complete only when:

- each constitutional route answers the five design questions
- each route has at least three internal dilemmas with mechanical consequences
- support lanes change meaning by route
- host overlays remain relevant after the opening
- route capstones require proof outside the focus tree
- failure opens recovery, transformation, or collapse content
- post-unification play reflects the chosen constitution
- military, economy, diplomacy, diaspora, restoration, and intelligence content interact
- no route relies mainly on flat modifiers or repeated idea grants
- AI can use every route and its crisis actions
- high-chaos content is hidden, earned, clearly fictional, and mechanically distinct
- the visible tree remains curated through route, phase, region, and crisis gates
