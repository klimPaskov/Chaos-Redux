# Treaty of Tordesillas Restoration: the Iberian relationship


## Confidence and specific obligations

Iberian Confidence ranges from 0 to 100. Its purpose is to make cooperation and strain legible. It is never a substitute for a treaty, legal ownership, sovereignty, or a recorded promise. A pair of countries with high Confidence and an unresolved Galician claim still needs an agreement on that claim.

The settled origin determines the starting relationship. An honored, fully delivered civil-war bargain gives the best start. A peaceful negotiated cession or agreed Portuguese renunciation gives a good start. A merely postponed dispute is less secure. Coercion, repudiation, and intervention on the losing side each produce a lower starting value and a specific reconciliation condition.

Confidence does not rise indefinitely because two countries remain in the same faction. The treaty must earn improvement through completed obligations, accepted restitution, and real mutual support. Long periods without new breaches can support modest recovery only after the outstanding issue is resolved. The recovery ceiling cannot overrule a live dispute.

## Reconciliation after refusal

A rejected demand can be settled by a final border agreement, a compensated transfer, or Portuguese acceptance of the existing border. The process is relatively short because neither country necessarily broke a promise. Spain's refusal is not morally equated with betrayal.

Portugal must accept that the later global treaty cannot be used as a covert means to take Galicia against the signed settlement. Spain must accept that Galicia's final owner is no longer up for annual renegotiation. The border provision becomes a bilateral protected exception to the global sphere rule.

The governments can retain domestic propaganda and political disagreement. They need sufficient working confidence and an enforceable settlement, not identical parties or affection between leaders.

## Reconciliation after repudiation

An earned and repudiated Galicia bargain requires restitution before the two countries can ratify the restored treaty. Restitution can be the promised transfer, a compensation package Portugal accepts as final, or another explicitly accepted settlement. A high diplomatic opinion value does not bypass this step.

The responsible Spanish government must acknowledge the settlement. A later government can take responsibility for repairing its predecessor's breach without pretending it personally signed the original bargain. Portugal can refuse an inadequate offer. There is no forced forgiveness option attached to the Chaos threshold.

After a repudiation, a 90-day period without renewed aggression or support for the partner's domestic enemies is required before ratification. A fulfilled restitution step improves Confidence once. Repeatedly cancelling and signing the same settlement cannot generate relationship credit.

## Reconciliation after an Iberian war

A war-origin relationship needs a recognized border, an ended bilateral conflict, and a mutual non-revision undertaking. Existing wars against other countries do not have to disappear, but the treaty cannot be signed while Spain and Portugal are opposing belligerents.

A peace conference result may have left one country a subject. Such an arrangement can support a temporary regional order, but it does not satisfy the design's two sovereign treaty powers. The subject must regain independent diplomatic authority through a legitimate route before equal treaty restoration becomes possible.

The victorious side can demand a stronger initial institutional position. It cannot use that position to allocate the other partner's homeland, secretly turn it into a puppet, or skip the second signature. A coerced country can decline the restoration and remain a rival.

## Reconciliation after a failed intervention

The victor of a civil war can seek Portuguese non-interference, a border guarantee, and a useful compensatory contribution. These are costs of rebuilding a damaged relationship, not enforcement of the defeated side's promise. Portugal is not required to pay a price it cannot afford merely to keep playing.

Refusing reconciliation closes or postpones the treaty route. Ordinary diplomacy, rearmament, and other events continue. The scenario does not punish refusal with an unavoidable stream of coups or recurring missions.

## What the partners negotiate

The restoration conference settles four connected questions: the Galician border, equality of the two treaty powers, how joint decisions are authorized, and how to handle existing faction commitments. The later worldwide sphere rule is not yet active at Evolution I, but both governments accept that expansion is intended to be complementary.

Both signatures are required. Either government can seek revisions or decline. The proposing country spends the conference cost only when a valid conference actually begins. The accepting country confirms its own participation. An unanswered conference expires without inventing an affirmative vote.

For two human players, neither response can be auto-selected because a monthly tick happened first. For a human and an AI, the AI's answer should follow the same visible diplomatic conditions. A mechanical faction invitation is not treated as ratification of every later doctrine.

## An equal or asymmetric partnership

An honored bargain and comparable strength support an equal partnership. A smaller Portugal that made an indispensable, fully delivered contribution can also negotiate equal status. Equality is a political settlement, not a requirement for identical industry or army size.

A materially unequal or coercive origin can produce a senior-partner arrangement. Seniority affects which government chairs the first conference and the order in which shared strategic proposals are presented. It does not transfer the junior partner's sphere, veto its internal integration choices, or change it into a subject.

The chair can rotate at a later mutually accepted conference. This is a diplomatic action with no farmable reward. There is no fifth 'treaty influence' currency. Seniority is a documented agreement, while Confidence describes whether it is being observed.

## Breaches during the treaty

Serious breaches include deliberately retaining partner-assigned conquests after a valid restitution agreement, attacking the partner, supporting its civil-war enemy in violation of the compact, and unilaterally renouncing the protected Galician settlement. A defensive occupation while fighting a shared enemy is not automatically a breach.

The injured partner can request correction, accept a temporary wartime exception, suspend joint expansion, or seek dissolution. An answer window prevents a single mistaken transaction from instantly destroying a long campaign. Repeated or deliberate violations can still make the treaty fail.

Confidence loss reflects an actual breach once, not each daily check that discovers the same state. Genuine correction can recover part of the loss. The record of the breach remains relevant to achievements and later diplomatic behavior.

## Suspension and recovery

Suspension stops new treaty claims, integration admissions, coordinated ultimatums, and routine restitution transfers. Already owned territory remains owned. Completed cores remain cores. Existing wars continue under the game's ordinary rules. Active projects pause rather than grant cores while their legal basis is disputed.

A limited breach can be repaired through restitution and renewed consent. A government change invites reaffirmation when required, not a replay of the entire Galicia story. A full dissolution ends treaty-specific cooperation and clears only effects that this event actually owns.

Neither suspension nor dissolution deletes another event's claims or takes away unrelated national focus rewards. Cleanup must be conservative where claim provenance cannot be proved. The treaty can stop treating a claim as valid policy without erasing a native claim that another system legitimately supplied.
