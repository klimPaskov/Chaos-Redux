# Treaty of Tordesillas Restoration: the outside world


## Countries are participants, not unclaimed terrain

The treaty's claims are assertions by Spain and Portugal.
Other governments are not obliged to accept their legitimacy.
Reactions should depend on vulnerability, existing relations, territorial exposure, ideology, current wars, and available support.

External countries first encounter the system as a diplomatic issue over Galicia or Spanish intervention.
They later confront the restored alliance, the global allocation of their territory, and finally a potential demand for subordination.
This gives the world's response a progression instead of making every country hostile at the first Portuguese demand.

The event must remain useful when the player is neither Spain nor Portugal.
A third-country player should understand what is happening, see how its territory is assigned, choose a response, and have ways to alter the outcome through normal military and diplomatic action.

## Early reactions

A Portuguese intervention in Spain can attract diplomatic pressure from powers already involved in the civil war.
The event does not recreate the entire non-intervention system.
It uses the current conflict and existing diplomatic situation to inform willingness and the cost of conspicuous support.

A neighboring power may welcome a peaceful Galicia settlement because it reduces Iberian uncertainty.
A government worried about Portuguese or Spanish expansion may view the same settlement as preparation for a stronger alliance.
Neither response requires a world-level hostility modifier on every country.

Foreign guarantees remain real.
A threat against Galicia owned by a third party is not insulated from existing guarantors because the event began as an Iberian story.

## Response to the global partition

At Evolution II, directly affected governments can reject the treaty's claim, seek limited accommodation, or coordinate opposition.
Rejection is the ordinary sovereign position and need not cost political power merely to avoid automatic submission.

A limited accommodation can offer access, commercial cooperation, or a negotiated settlement of a specific dispute.
It does not secretly subordinate the country or count all its states toward a crown's reach.
The treaty powers can accept such terms when they are not ready for war.

An exposed small state might seek a protective relationship with its assigned crown.
A large state split by the line might oppose both.
A country already allied to one crown might request recognition of its independence and delay confrontation.
These differences should shape AI behavior and the available conference targets.

## Organized opposition

Opposition is a diplomatic posture and a set of cooperating countries, not a compulsory global faction.
Existing alliances can adopt an anti-partition priority.
Independent countries can negotiate mutual defense or coordinate with a compatible existing faction.
The event must not collapse all other international politics into a single bloc.

A prominent threatened country can offer a defensive conference.
Participants retain the ability to decline, seek narrower commitments, or insist on a limited geographic guarantee.
The conference is valuable because it creates real access, protection, or joint priorities where the game permits them.

The opposition's useful actions are focused: protect a selected threatened region, support a country currently resisting absorption, and disrupt a major treaty supply connection through actual military operations.
It does not get a new stockpile currency or a monthly global sabotage roll.

No external country receives free ownership claims on both Iberian homelands merely for joining a conference.
Its defensive purpose is to preserve sovereignty and reverse particular conquests, not to invent another unbounded partition mechanic.

## A country split between spheres

A split country can negotiate with both crowns, seek a single political patron with a defined territorial settlement, or refuse division altogether.
The interface must make clear which of its states each crown claims.
Its capital does not decide the fate of the whole country.

The two crowns are encouraged to coordinate an ultimatum rather than send contradictory demands on the same day.
If they demand incompatible subject arrangements, that is an Iberian diplomatic problem, not an automatic target-country failure.
The target can exploit the disagreement through legitimate counteroffers.

A country cannot be native-puppeted to both crowns simultaneously.
A temporary administrative arrangement is permitted only as the explicit transitional status described in Part 08.
Final-world completion needs a genuine legal solution.

## Subordination offers and refusal

A subordination offer states its patron, territorial terms, autonomy implications, access obligations, and any existing wars that will or will not be affected.
The target has a 120-day standard response period during the high-chaos final campaign.
An immediate answer is possible, but delay is not treated as acceptance.

Targets consider the patron's proximity, ability to enforce the demand, treatment of earlier partners, and credible external support.
A country that has just seen a treaty partner repudiate Galicia should reasonably distrust a promise of autonomy.

Refusal gives the treaty powers a political choice about enforcement.
It can justify a warning, further negotiation, or an eligible war goal.
The country is not deleted, annexed, or stripped of its army by the refusal outcome.

Acceptance may prevent a destructive war and preserve a playable local government.
It still counts as a loss of sovereignty in the scenario's political framing.
The writing should not present every coercive accession as universally welcomed liberation.

## Liberation and independence

A liberated country regains whatever independence the actual peace or release mechanism grants.
Its states leave treaty reach where they are no longer under the correct crown's order.
Existing cores do not automatically vanish, but current control and ownership determine readiness.

Liberation cannot repeatedly grant a special Event 093 reward for the same state changing hands.
Generic Chaos effects of liberation, annexation, and puppet changes belong to the shared system.
Event-specific diplomatic consequences are limited to the collapse or repair of the compact itself.

A treaty subject that seeks independence uses the established subject and war systems.
This specification does not invent a separate universal independence meter.
The parent event responds to the actual result and updates its political order accordingly.

## Civilians and devastation

The outside world's suffering is represented by real wars, occupation, destroyed infrastructure, and any established civilian systems those actions affect.
The treaty does not create automatic famine in every resistant country or a civilian-death payment for refusing an ultimatum.

Where integration or military action interacts with an active famine, migration, disease, or population ledger, the existing owner system determines those effects.
Event 093 reacts only through the integration rules in [Part 07](093_treaty_of_tordesillas_restoration_spec_part_07_regional_integration.md#hunger-in-an-enrolled-region): famine pauses a project, and requisition or coercion sends people into the migration system.
It must not double-count losses or fabricate physical population movement for a political settlement.

## Tone

The event can use dry irony about governments treating an old line as ownership of the modern world.
Diplomatic overconfidence, bureaucratic precision, and the contrast between Galicia and global ambition provide the humor.

The people living in assigned territories are not the joke.
Their governments can resist, bargain, or submit under pressure.
Localization should distinguish a crown's propaganda from the narrator's factual description and avoid claiming that a whole population enthusiastically accepts its allocation.
