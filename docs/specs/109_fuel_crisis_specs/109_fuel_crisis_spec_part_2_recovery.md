# Fuel Crisis: recovery and strategic play

## A reserve loss inside a working economy

Fuel Crisis removes stored fuel while leaving the systems that supply and consume it operational. This gives the event a clear identity. The player has to manage the interval between an immediate loss and the restoration of a sustainable operating margin.

The important distinction is between a **stock**, the fuel available now, and a **flow**, the fuel arriving and being consumed over time. More storage does not by itself refill an empty reserve. A strong inflow does not guarantee accumulation if military demand absorbs all of it. The fuel balance can also change as operations, trade delivery, construction, and technology change.

The event should make these relationships more visible through its explanation and ordinary game information. It should not introduce a second fuel calculation.

## What a usable recovery means

There are several useful descriptions of the player's situation. These are explanatory conditions, not named event phases or a new state machine.

| Situation | What the player can infer | Practical pressure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reserve at zero with inadequate usable inflow | Existing commitments cannot all be supplied sustainably | Decide which activity to reduce and where additional supply can come from |
| Reserve small with a nonnegative balance | Current activity is becoming supportable, but interruptions remain dangerous | Build a buffer without sacrificing essential operations |
| Reserve rebuilding with a positive balance | The chosen activity level leaves fuel for later use | Decide when to resume suspended operations |
| Reserve sufficient for the intended plan with dependable supply | The country has restored a sustainable operating margin | Return to ordinary strategic fuel planning |
| Reserve rising while all fuel-using forces are idle | Fuel is accumulating, but the next offensive may recreate a deficit | Judge recovery against the intended return to activity |

A Tasks mission can define a particular measurable recovery target for one player. That target is a challenge contract and does not become a universal recovery threshold for the world.

## Operational priorities and their costs

The following responses are native campaign actions and design examples. Exact controls, legal trade arrangements, and fuel allocation behavior must follow the installed game and active mod systems.

| Priority | Useful actions | What it protects | Real opportunity cost | When it becomes a poor choice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Land operations | Concentrate fuel on essential movement and selected fuel-using formations, reduce unnecessary training or advances | A breakthrough, retreat, encirclement escape, or defense requiring mobility | Lower tempo elsewhere and less fuel for aircraft or ships | A broad advance consumes every new delivery without achieving a decisive objective |
| Air operations | Preserve necessary defensive or offensive missions and reduce lower-value activity | Air defense, a critical battle, or an operation dependent on air cover | Fewer other sorties, less training, or slower reserve rebuilding | Missions continue at a scale the usable inflow cannot sustain |
| Naval operations | Preserve essential escorts and selected task-force activity while reviewing distant operations | Trade delivery, troop movement, and maritime access | Reduced naval reach, less training, or weaker presence elsewhere | Expensive sorties protect little while threatening the routes needed for recovery |
| Reserve rebuilding | Reduce discretionary consumption and accumulate a buffer | Future operational endurance and resistance to delivery interruption | Immediate military opportunities are deferred | The enemy gains a decisive advantage while the country waits for an unnecessarily large reserve |

The event should encourage repeated reassessment. A country can protect escorts first, reopen supply, rebuild a modest reserve, and then resume air or land operations. That sequence emerges from the situation rather than from a scripted progression tree.

The design does not assign fixed fuel shares to the four priorities. The player remains free to use the native controls and actual force composition.

## Routes toward more usable fuel

### Continue and redirect existing supply

Existing imports and production are the fastest potential source of recovery because the event has not removed them. Before committing scarce industry elsewhere, the player can compare current deliveries against essential consumption.

Restricting an unnecessary operation can be a meaningful intervention. It can free fuel for escorts, aircraft, or later maneuvers without creating additional fuel. The cost is the operation that no longer happens.

### Use ordinary oil trade and diplomacy

Where the current rules permit, additional oil trade can increase usable fuel generation. The relevant questions are supplier availability, the country's ability to pay the normal trade cost, route access, and actual delivery.

An agreement with an inaccessible supplier does not solve the shortage. A nominal import figure is insufficient evidence for a dependable-source task if the game distinguishes contracted supply from received supply. A task must measure the usable result with a verified accessor.

Diplomacy can help preserve access and make a source more dependable. Improved relations alone are not fuel. The event should not grant automatic trade acceptance, protected convoys, or preferential prices.

### Protect or reopen an existing route

A route that already matters to the campaign can become more valuable after the loss. The player may preserve escorts, adjust existing access, defend a port, or remove a real obstruction using ordinary play.

This response can produce a difficult allocation problem. The navy may need some of the scarce fuel to protect the delivery that allows the country to recover. The event should leave that tradeoff intact.

A Fuel Crisis task can recognize an achieved route outcome only where the target is reachable and the route contributes usable oil. The task must not create an unrelated enemy or prescribe a new war simply to manufacture an objective.

### Expand domestic supply through ordinary development

Existing resource development, refinery construction, and fuel-related research may help where the country has access to those options. Their ordinary construction time, resource inputs, industrial commitment, technology conditions, and output rules still apply.

Long projects are strategic responses. They are not an immediate cure for an empty reserve. A task that depends on a real project must use a deadline that includes its feasible completion time and the subsequent fuel accumulation or supply hold.

Fuel silos remain intact and can still be built normally. A silo adds storage under the native rules. An empty new silo is not proof of recovery.

### Seek assistance where an existing system supports it

Normal assistance or fuel transfer mechanics may help when they are available in the active ruleset. Other countries also suffered the wipe, so assistance depends on actual recovery and the donor's opportunity cost.

Expensive unofficial trade is a possible response only where an existing campaign system already represents it. The Fuel Crisis specification does not assume that a general fuel black market exists. Any task tied to such a system requires its own verified delivered-fuel outcome. Descriptive flavor must not promise a purchase action that the player cannot perform.

### Compete over oil fields

Oil-producing territory can become more strategically attractive, but ownership, control, extraction, export rules, delivery, and conversion all still matter. Capturing an oil state should not be described as instantly replenishing fuel.

Wars and territorial competition use their existing requirements and consequences. Fuel Crisis can change a country's motive for ordinary action. It does not automatically declare wars or generate a new territorial branch.

## Country patterns

These patterns classify the current campaign. They are not tag-specific bonuses or fixed national scripts.

| Campaign pattern | Likely exposure | Productive response | Balance concern |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strong usable production and modest active demand | Brief loss of buffer | Continue essential operations while refilling | Do not manufacture a long shortage after ordinary recovery |
| Strong production and very high active demand | Persistent low reserve despite resource wealth | Narrow the active commitment or increase usable supply | Oil wealth must not be treated as automatic recovery |
| Dependable imports and manageable demand | Recovery can be quick | Preserve delivery and review activity | Import dependence alone must not imply prolonged failure |
| Imports exposed to enemy action | Recovery vulnerable to interruption | Protect or diversify actual usable delivery | A promised agreement must not count as a dependable source |
| Limited industry with a small fuel-using force | Small absolute losses can still constrain a critical capability | Choose a small practical operating margin | Do not impose a major-power reserve target |
| Little or no current fuel demand | Reserve loss may have little immediate military effect | Continue ordinary planning | Do not assign a contrived recovery task |
| Existing deficit before the event | The wipe reveals an unsustainable operating tempo immediately | Reduce demand or solve the supply deficit | A timer must not require an unreachable refill |
| No feasible short-term supply route | Recovery may depend on restraint or a longer project | Use the options the campaign actually provides | Skip a Fuel Crisis task if no active, measurable, achievable contract exists |

Subjects and unusual countries follow the same wipe rule. Their native diplomatic, economic, or gameplay restrictions matter when selecting a Tasks objective. The task selector must not assume that they can independently purchase oil, declare war, or complete every construction project.

## Illustrative fuel accounting

The examples use invented fuel units and stable daily rates solely to explain the design. They are not historical country data or claims about exact native tick behavior. Real play can change every rate.

| Example | Fuel arriving per day | Consumption per day | Net change per day | Consequence after the wipe |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| High inflow, restrained activity | 6,000 | 2,000 | +4,000 | A 40,000-unit reserve takes about 10 days to rebuild |
| Same inflow, heavy commitments | 6,000 | 8,000 | −2,000 | Sustained accumulation is impossible until the balance changes |
| Dependable importer | 2,000 | 800 | +1,200 | A 12,000-unit buffer takes about 10 days |
| Importer with limited delivery | 500 | 800 | −300 | The country needs better delivery or lower consumption |
| Conservation response | 1,500 | Reduced from 2,000 to 900 | Changes from −500 to +600 | A deliberate reduction creates a recovery path |
| Supply expansion | Increased from 1,500 to 2,600 | 2,000 | Changes from −500 to +600 | More usable inflow creates the same accumulation rate through a different cost |

These examples show why the event does not need a fixed shortage modifier. The same reserve wipe can be mild or severe depending on the surrounding economy and military commitment.

## AI behavior

All AI countries suffer the same world write. The specification relies on the native AI's existing fuel, trade, construction, and military management. It adds no event-owned priority lottery.

The intended observable response is that the AI continues trying to operate within the fuel it can actually obtain. A stronger producer may recover with little intervention. A constrained country may reduce activity, change ordinary trade, or remain short because of its campaign position. These are behavior expectations to inspect in the supported game build, not claims that new AI code has been implemented.

Tasks remains player-only under its prior brief. The Fuel Crisis adapter must therefore expose no AI mission assignment and must not add rewards that subsidize AI recovery.

If live evidence shows that the native AI mishandles the temporary loss, record the specific behavior and its effect on balance. Any later AI policy change requires a separate, narrowly justified owner change and matched scenario evidence. It must not be hidden inside the stockpile setter.

## Balance questions worth testing

| Question | What the test should distinguish |
| --- | --- |
| Does a strong surplus recover very quickly? | Intended economic recovery from an accidentally persistent zeroing effect |
| Does a deficit country remain short? | A genuine unsustainable fuel balance from an unrequested production penalty |
| Does a fleet still operate briefly? | Native unit fuel behavior from a failed national-stockpile write |
| Does additional oil help? | Actual usable delivery from a nominal agreement |
| Can military restraint restore a reserve? | Real player conservation from fuel consumption collapsing automatically because the country is empty |
| Can a country with a small economy receive a fair task? | A feasible scaled objective from a large fixed target |
| Does the event remain readable in multiplayer? | One world effect and one announcement from repeated per-recipient execution |

The event succeeds when its consequences arise from the lost reserve, the response options remain legible, and countries recover at rates justified by their real fuel economies.
